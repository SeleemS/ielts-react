  <p>The candidates who struggle most in <strong>IELTS Writing Task 2</strong> are rarely the ones with the weakest English. They are the ones who start writing before they know what they want to say. Ten minutes in, they realise their second body paragraph has no real idea behind it, they contradict their introduction, and they run out of time. The fix is not more vocabulary &mdash; it is a short, disciplined plan. This guide gives you a five-minute planning routine that turns a blank prompt into a clear, developed essay every time.</p>

  <h2>Why five minutes of planning saves the whole essay</h2>
  <p>You have <strong>40 minutes</strong> for Task 2 and must write at least <strong>250 words</strong>. Spending five of those minutes planning feels risky when the clock is ticking, but it is the highest-value time you will spend. Two of the four marking criteria &mdash; <strong>Task Response</strong> and <strong>Coherence and Cohesion</strong> &mdash; reward a clear position and a logical progression of ideas, and both are decided before you write a single sentence. A planned essay stays on topic, develops each point fully, and reads as one connected argument. An unplanned one wanders, repeats itself, and gets capped at Band 5 or 6 for Task Response no matter how good the grammar is.</p>
  <p>A sensible time budget looks like this: <strong>5 minutes planning, 30 minutes writing, 5 minutes checking.</strong> Here is exactly what to do with those first five minutes.</p>

  <h2>Minute 1: Decode the question</h2>
  <p>Read the prompt twice and underline the <strong>topic</strong> (what the essay is about) and the <strong>task words</strong> (what you must actually do). The task words tell you the essay type, and getting this wrong is the single most expensive mistake in Task 2.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><em>"To what extent do you agree or disagree?"</em> &mdash; take a clear position and defend it.</li>
    <li><em>"Discuss both views and give your own opinion."</em> &mdash; cover both sides <strong>and</strong> state what you think.</li>
    <li><em>"What are the advantages and disadvantages?"</em> &mdash; balance both, no personal opinion required.</li>
    <li><em>"What problems does this cause and what solutions can you suggest?"</em> &mdash; link each solution to a problem.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>If the question has two parts, both must appear in your plan. Answering only half is the fastest way to lose Task Response marks.</p>

  <h2>Minute 2: Decide your position first</h2>
  <p>Before brainstorming ideas, commit to an answer. Ask yourself the question out loud and give a one-line reply: <em>"Yes, I mostly agree, because the benefits to public health outweigh the cost."</em> That sentence becomes your <strong>thesis</strong>, and everything else in the essay now has a job: to support it. Deciding your stance first stops you generating clever ideas that pull in opposite directions and leave you sitting on the fence &mdash; a common Band 6 trap.</p>
  <p>You do not have to believe your position. Examiners score how you argue, not whether you are right. Choose whichever side is <strong>easier to support with examples</strong>, even if it is not your genuine view.</p>

  <h2>Minute 3: Brainstorm ideas fast</h2>
  <p>Now generate raw material. Do not write sentences &mdash; jot two or three word fragments. If your mind goes blank, run the topic through these idea-generating angles:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Economic:</strong> cost, jobs, money saved or spent.</li>
    <li><strong>Social:</strong> families, communities, equality, health.</li>
    <li><strong>Environmental:</strong> pollution, resources, sustainability.</li>
    <li><strong>Personal / educational:</strong> skills, freedom, well-being, learning.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>For a prompt on free university education, that might produce: <em>social &ndash; fairer access for poor students; economic &ndash; skilled workforce, but high tax cost; personal &ndash; less debt, more career choice.</em> Sixty seconds of fragments gives you far more than two body paragraphs need.</p>

  <h2>Minute 4: Select and pair with examples</h2>
  <p>You do not need every idea &mdash; you need <strong>two strong ones you can develop</strong>. Pick the two you can explain and support best, and cross out the rest. Beside each, note a quick example: a reason, a consequence, or a realistic instance. Depth beats breadth. One idea explained across four or five sentences scores higher than four ideas mentioned in passing, because Task Response rewards <strong>extension</strong> &mdash; explaining <em>why</em> and <em>what results</em>, not just listing points.</p>

  <h2>Minute 5: Outline the paragraphs</h2>
  <p>Sketch the skeleton so you never pause to think about structure while writing. A reliable four-paragraph shape works for almost every task:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Introduction:</strong> paraphrase the question, then state your thesis.</li>
    <li><strong>Body 1:</strong> first main idea + explanation + example.</li>
    <li><strong>Body 2:</strong> second main idea + explanation + example.</li>
    <li><strong>Conclusion:</strong> restate your position and summarise the two reasons.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Your finished plan might be six lines of notes. That is enough. With the thinking done, the 30 minutes of writing become pure translation of the plan into sentences &mdash; which is where your grammar and vocabulary can finally shine.</p>

  <h2>Common planning mistakes to avoid</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Skipping the plan to "save time."</strong> You lose the time later, rewriting or drying up.</li>
    <li><strong>Planning too many ideas.</strong> Two developed points beat four thin ones.</li>
    <li><strong>Not deciding a position.</strong> A vague stance produces a vague essay and a low Task Response mark.</li>
    <li><strong>Writing full sentences in the plan.</strong> Notes are faster; save your best phrasing for the essay itself.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Practise planning under time pressure</h2>
  <p>Planning is a skill, and it gets faster with drilling. Take real prompts from our <a href="/writingquestion">IELTS Writing Questions</a> page and spend only five minutes producing a plan &mdash; position, two ideas, examples, outline &mdash; without writing the full essay. Do ten of those and you will decode any prompt in seconds. When you do write a complete answer, paste it into the free <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">IELTS Writing Checker</a> for instant feedback on Task Response and Coherence, and pair this routine with our guide to the <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-band-7-structure">Task 2 structure that scores Band 7</a> so your plan flows straight into a proven shape.</p>

  <p>Decode the task, fix your position, brainstorm in fragments, and outline before you write &mdash; five minutes of planning is the difference between an essay that argues and one that merely fills the page.</p>
