  <p>Part 3 is where the IELTS Speaking test stops being about you and starts being about ideas. After the two-minute long turn in <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-2-cue-card">Part 2</a>, the examiner spends roughly four to five minutes asking broader, more abstract questions linked to that topic. Many candidates cruise through Parts 1 and 2 and then lose half a band here, because they answer a discussion question the way they would answer a personal one: briefly.</p>

  <h2>What Part 3 Actually Tests</h2>
  <p>Speaking is marked on four equally weighted criteria: <strong>Fluency and Coherence</strong>, <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong>, and <strong>Pronunciation</strong>. Part 3 is the only section that reliably pushes you toward the higher end of all four, because abstract questions force you to speculate, compare, and justify — which in turn requires conditional structures, opinion language, and topic vocabulary you would never need to describe your hometown.</p>
  <p>The examiner is not judging whether your opinion is correct. There is no "right" view on whether governments should fund the arts. They are listening for whether you can develop a position without stalling.</p>

  <h2>The Question Types You Will Meet</h2>
  <p>Part 3 questions fall into a small number of recognisable shapes. Learning to spot them buys you a second or two of thinking time:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Opinion:</strong> "Do you think children spend too much time online?"</li>
    <li><strong>Comparison:</strong> "How is shopping today different from thirty years ago?"</li>
    <li><strong>Cause and effect:</strong> "Why do people in cities move house so often?"</li>
    <li><strong>Speculation about the future:</strong> "Will printed books disappear?"</li>
    <li><strong>Advantages and disadvantages:</strong> "What are the drawbacks of working from home?"</li>
    <li><strong>Evaluation:</strong> "How effective are recycling programmes?"</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Notice that none of these ask about your life. If you catch yourself saying "In my family, we..." as your whole answer, you have slipped back into Part 1 mode.</p>

  <h2>The PEEL Method for Extended Answers</h2>
  <p>Aim for roughly 30 to 45 seconds per answer — about four to six sentences. The most reliable shape is PEEL:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Point</strong> — answer the question directly in one sentence.</li>
    <li><strong>Explain</strong> — give the reasoning behind it.</li>
    <li><strong>Example</strong> — make it concrete with a specific case.</li>
    <li><strong>Link</strong> — round off with a consequence, contrast, or qualification.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Here is PEEL applied to <em>"Do you think governments should pay for public transport?"</em>:</p>
  <p><strong>Point:</strong> "Yes, I'd say heavily subsidised transport is one of the better uses of public money." <strong>Explain:</strong> "The main reason is that it's the only realistic way to get people out of private cars — if a bus ticket costs more than petrol, most commuters simply won't switch." <strong>Example:</strong> "In Vienna, for instance, an annual travel pass costs about a euro a day, and public transport use there is far higher than in comparable cities." <strong>Link:</strong> "That said, it only works if the service is genuinely frequent and reliable; cheap tickets for a bus that never arrives won't change anyone's habits."</p>
  <p>That final <strong>Link</strong> sentence is what separates Band 6 from Band 7. It shows you can qualify a view rather than just assert it.</p>

  <h2>Language That Earns Marks Here</h2>
  <p>Part 3 gives you natural openings for structures the examiner is actively listening for:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Hedging:</strong> "It tends to be the case that...", "By and large...", "This varies a lot depending on..."</li>
    <li><strong>Conditionals:</strong> "If schools were better funded, we probably wouldn't see...", "Had that policy come earlier, things might have been different."</li>
    <li><strong>Contrast:</strong> "Whereas older generations valued job security, younger workers seem to prioritise flexibility."</li>
    <li><strong>Speculation:</strong> "I imagine we'll see...", "It's likely that...", "I wouldn't be surprised if..."</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Use these because they are true to your meaning, not as decoration. An examiner notices a memorised phrase bolted onto an unrelated idea immediately, and it hurts Lexical Resource rather than helping it.</p>

  <h2>Five Mistakes That Cap You at Band 6</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Answering in one sentence.</strong> "Yes, I think so." then silence. The examiner will move on, and you have thrown away your best chance to display range.</li>
    <li><strong>Listing without developing.</strong> Three shallow reasons score worse than one reason explained properly with an example.</li>
    <li><strong>Being personal instead of general.</strong> Personal examples are welcome as <em>support</em>, but the answer itself should address people, societies, or trends.</li>
    <li><strong>Filling silence with "actually" and "you know".</strong> A brief, natural pause is better than a string of fillers. Fluency means coherent, not fast.</li>
    <li><strong>Faking data.</strong> "Studies show 73% of people..." invites doubt and adds nothing. "There's a lot of research suggesting..." is safer and just as effective.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>What to Do When You Have No Idea</h2>
  <p>Part 3 questions can be genuinely hard — "What role should museums play in society?" is not something most people have a ready view on. You are allowed to think. Buy time honestly:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>"That's not something I've thought about much, but off the top of my head..."</li>
    <li>"I suppose it depends on what kind of museum we're talking about."</li>
    <li>"Let me think about that for a second."</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Then reach for a familiar angle: cost, time, technology, education, or generational change will unlock almost any Part 3 topic. Saying "I don't know" and stopping is the only genuinely wrong answer.</p>

  <h2>How to Practise</h2>
  <p>Record yourself. Take a Part 2 topic, invent three abstract questions from it, and answer each for 40 seconds without stopping. Play it back and check one thing only: did every answer have an <em>Explain</em> and a <em>Link</em>? Most candidates find they have the Point and the Example and nothing else.</p>
  <p>Work through the topic sets on our <a href="/speakingquestion">IELTS Speaking practice pages</a> to build a bank of ideas across common themes, and use the <a href="/band-calculator">free band score calculator</a> to see how your Speaking estimate combines with your other sections into an overall band.</p>

  <h2>The Takeaway</h2>
  <p>Part 3 rewards candidates who treat every question as an invitation to think out loud for 40 seconds. Answer directly, explain why, ground it in an example, then qualify it. Do that consistently and the vocabulary and grammar the examiner needs to hear will appear on their own.</p>
