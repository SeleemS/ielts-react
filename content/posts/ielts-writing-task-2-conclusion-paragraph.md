  <p>Plenty of candidates write a strong introduction and two solid body paragraphs, then run out of time and tack on a rushed, vague final sentence. That is a costly mistake. The conclusion is the last thing the examiner reads before deciding your band, and a weak one can undercut an otherwise good essay. The good news: a strong Task 2 conclusion follows a simple, repeatable formula. This guide shows you exactly what to write.</p>

  <h2>What a conclusion is actually for</h2>
  <p>In IELTS Writing Task 2 you write a discursive essay of <strong>at least 250 words</strong> in roughly <strong>40 minutes</strong>. The conclusion has one job: to <strong>restate your position and summarise your main points</strong> so that your answer to the question is unmistakably clear. It is not the place to introduce a brand-new argument, add a fresh example, or suddenly change your mind.</p>
  <p>Task 2 is marked on four equally weighted criteria — <strong>Task Response</strong>, <strong>Coherence and Cohesion</strong>, <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, and <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong>. A clear conclusion directly supports Task Response (it confirms you answered the question fully) and Coherence and Cohesion (it gives your essay a logical end). Skipping it, or writing something muddled, hurts both.</p>

  <h2>The three-part formula</h2>
  <p>Almost every high-scoring conclusion contains three moves, in this order:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Signal the ending.</strong> Open with a short cohesive phrase so the examiner knows you are closing.</li>
    <li><strong>Restate your position.</strong> Answer the question directly, using different words from your introduction.</li>
    <li><strong>Summarise your reasons.</strong> Refer back to the main points from your body paragraphs in a single sentence.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>That is it. Two to three sentences is enough. A conclusion does not need to be long — it needs to be clear.</p>

  <h2>How to signal the ending (without cliché)</h2>
  <p>You need a linking phrase, but tired ones like "In conclusion" are fine and safe. What matters is that you do not overuse the same connector you used elsewhere. Reliable options include:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>In conclusion,</strong> …</li>
    <li><strong>To conclude,</strong> …</li>
    <li><strong>Overall,</strong> …</li>
    <li><strong>In summary,</strong> …</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Avoid "All in all" and "Last but not least" — they read as informal and are overused. For more on connectors across the whole essay, see our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-linking-words-cohesive-devices">linking words and cohesive devices</a>.</p>

  <h2>Restate your position — don't copy it</h2>
  <p>The single most common error is copying the thesis word-for-word from the introduction. Repetition does not demonstrate range and can be penalised. Instead, <strong>paraphrase</strong>: change the vocabulary and, where possible, the grammatical structure.</p>
  <p>Suppose your introduction said: <em>"I firmly believe that governments should invest more heavily in public transport."</em> Your conclusion could rephrase it as: <em>"It is clear that greater state funding for public transport networks would bring the most benefit."</em> Same position, fresh wording. If paraphrasing feels difficult, our post on <a href="/blog/ielts-paraphrasing-techniques">paraphrasing techniques</a> breaks the skill down.</p>

  <h2>Sentence templates you can adapt</h2>
  <p>These frameworks work across the common Task 2 question types. Fill in the brackets with your own content — never memorise a whole essay, but a flexible skeleton is fair game.</p>
  <p><strong>Opinion / agree-disagree essays:</strong></p>
  <ul>
    <li>"In conclusion, while [opposing view] has some merit, [your position] because [reason 1] and [reason 2]."</li>
    <li>"Overall, I am convinced that [restated opinion], primarily due to [main reason]."</li>
  </ul>
  <p><strong>Discussion (discuss both views) essays:</strong></p>
  <ul>
    <li>"To conclude, although both [view A] and [view B] carry weight, I believe [your position] is the more persuasive stance."</li>
  </ul>
  <p><strong>Problem / solution and advantage / disadvantage essays:</strong></p>
  <ul>
    <li>"In summary, [problem] can be addressed through [solution 1] and [solution 2], provided that [condition]."</li>
    <li>"Overall, the benefits of [topic] appear to outweigh the drawbacks, chiefly because [key benefit]."</li>
  </ul>
  <p>For a worked example of a full opinion essay, including how the conclusion fits, read our <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-band-7-structure">Band 7 Task 2 structure guide</a>.</p>

  <h2>Common conclusion mistakes to avoid</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Adding new ideas.</strong> A new argument or example in the conclusion confuses the reader and suggests poor planning. Keep new content in the body.</li>
    <li><strong>Contradicting yourself.</strong> If your body clearly argued one side, do not hedge into "it depends" at the end. Commit to your position.</li>
    <li><strong>Being too short.</strong> A one-word "In conclusion, yes." is not a conclusion. Restate and summarise properly.</li>
    <li><strong>Being too long.</strong> Do not re-explain every argument. A single summarising sentence is enough — spend the extra minutes checking grammar instead.</li>
    <li><strong>Rhetorical questions.</strong> Ending on "So what will governments do?" leaves your answer open. Task 2 rewards a clear final position, not an open one.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Timing: leave 3–4 minutes</h2>
  <p>The reason so many conclusions are weak is timing, not ability. Plan your essay so you begin the conclusion with at least <strong>three or four minutes left</strong>. Because the formula is fixed, you can write it quickly once your body paragraphs are done — and a calm, formulaic ending beats a panicked one every time.</p>
