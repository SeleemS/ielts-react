  <p>Part 3 of <strong>IELTS Listening</strong> is where a lot of otherwise strong candidates quietly lose four or five marks. The test has <strong>four parts, 40 questions and about 30 minutes of audio</strong>, and the recording plays <strong>once only</strong>. Section 3 is questions <strong>21 to 30</strong>, and unlike the single-speaker lecture in Part 4, it is a <strong>discussion between two, three, or occasionally four people</strong> — most often two students and a tutor, or two students planning an assignment together. The academic content is manageable. The real difficulty is procedural: several voices speak quickly, they interrupt and correct each other, and the answer frequently depends not on <em>what</em> was said but on <em>who</em> said it.</p>

  <h2>What makes Section 3 different</h2>
  <p>Two features set Part 3 apart, and both change how you should listen:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Multiple speakers.</strong> You are given the speakers' names before the audio starts. Wrong answer options are very often things a <em>different</em> speaker said, or an idea one speaker raised and another rejected. Hearing the words is not enough; you must attach them to the right person.</li>
    <li><strong>It is a real conversation.</strong> People agree, disagree, change their minds, and finish each other's sentences. The final answer is usually the view a speaker <em>commits to</em>, which may come several seconds after the topic is first mentioned.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>This is why so many Part 3 questions are <strong>multiple choice</strong> and <strong>matching</strong> — both reward tracking speakers, and both are built so that every option is plausible. For the mechanics of the trickiest type, see our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-multiple-choice">Listening multiple choice</a>.</p>

  <h2>Use the preview to mark up who's who</h2>
  <p>Before Part 3 plays, you get time to read the questions. Do not just read them — annotate them:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Write each speaker's initial next to matching questions.</strong> If the task asks "What does Maria think about…", jot an <em>M</em> so your ear is primed for her voice, not the tutor's.</li>
    <li><strong>Underline the stem, not the options.</strong> In multiple choice, read what the question is actually asking first; the three options are designed to all sound familiar in the audio.</li>
    <li><strong>Predict the shape of the answer.</strong> For a matching task, glance at the option list so you can slot answers in the instant they resolve.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Listen for commitment, not mention</h2>
  <p>The single most useful habit in Part 3 is separating what a speaker <strong>mentions</strong> from what they <strong>commit to</strong>. A typical exchange runs: one student proposes an idea, a second raises a doubt, and then they settle on something — often the second option, not the first. If you mark your answer the moment you hear a keyword, you will be caught by the correction that follows. Wait for the resolution.</p>
  <p>Train your ear for the <strong>signal phrases</strong> that carry meaning between speakers:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Agreement:</strong> "That's a good point," "Exactly," "I hadn't thought of that."</li>
    <li><strong>Disagreement or correction:</strong> "Actually," "I'm not so sure," "But wouldn't it be better to…"</li>
    <li><strong>Changing direction:</strong> "On second thoughts," "Let's go with," "In the end."</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The words after these phrases usually carry the answer. "We could interview staff — actually, let's send a questionnaire instead" resolves to <em>questionnaire</em>, even though <em>interview staff</em> was said first and louder.</p>

  <h2>Common Section 3 traps</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>The wrong-speaker trap.</strong> The option is correct information, but it belongs to a different person than the question asks about. Your speaker initials in the margin defend against this.</li>
    <li><strong>The abandoned idea.</strong> An option is discussed enthusiastically, then dropped. It appears among the choices precisely because it was mentioned.</li>
    <li><strong>The paraphrase gap.</strong> The audio rarely repeats the exact words in the options. If a choice matches word-for-word what you hear, be suspicious — the correct answer is usually reworded.</li>
    <li><strong>Falling behind.</strong> Miss one answer and the discussion moves on without you. Abandon anything you miss instantly, mark your best guess later, and lock onto the next question rather than chasing the last.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Never leave a blank</h2>
  <p>There is no penalty for a wrong answer in IELTS Listening, so an empty box is a wasted chance. If you lose a question, put a plausible option down and move on — for multiple choice, eliminate any option you are sure was rejected and guess from the rest. In the paper test you transfer answers at the end; on the computer-delivered test they are entered directly, so use the checking time to fill any gaps.</p>

  <h2>How to practise Part 3 specifically</h2>
  <p>General listening practice will not fix the speaker-tracking problem — you have to target it. Work through full <a href="/listeningquestion">IELTS Listening practice sets</a>, and after each Part 3, replay it with the transcript and mark exactly where each answer resolved and which speaker owned it. You will start to see the same distractor patterns repeat. If you are stuck around band 6, our guide on moving from <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-band-6-to-band-7">band 6 to band 7 in Listening</a> covers the accuracy habits that close the gap, and the single-speaker challenge of the final part is broken down in our <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-section-4-strategy">Section 4 strategy guide</a>.</p>

  <p>Section 3 rewards preparation more than raw listening ability. Mark the speakers, wait for the point where the discussion settles, and treat every familiar-sounding option as a suspect until you know who committed to it. Do that consistently and the ten questions that trip up most candidates become some of the most predictable in the test.</p>
