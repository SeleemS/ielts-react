  <p>The environment is one of the most reliable topic families in IELTS. It appears in Writing Task 2 questions about pollution, energy and government policy, in Speaking Part 3 discussions about recycling and climate, and constantly in Reading and Listening passages. Most candidates prepare for it the wrong way: they memorise isolated impressive words — "deforestation," "biodegradable," "anthropogenic" — and then deploy them in sentences that do not quite work. Examiners marking Lexical Resource are listening for something different: <strong>collocation</strong>, the natural pairing of words that fluent English actually uses. "Heavy pollution" is right; "strong pollution" is wrong; both use simple words. This guide gives you the collocations that matter, organised by sub-topic, with notes on accurate use.</p>

  <h2>Why collocations beat rare words</h2>
  <p>The Band 7 descriptors for both Writing and Speaking explicitly mention collocation awareness and precision. A candidate who writes "governments should <strong>impose stricter regulations</strong> on factories that <strong>discharge waste</strong> into rivers" is demonstrating exactly that — every pairing is natural. A candidate who writes "governments should do harder rules on factories that throw dirt in rivers" knows the ideas but not the pairings; a candidate who writes "governments should promulgate draconian environmental jurisprudence" has memorised rare words and produced something no fluent writer would say. The middle path — ordinary words, correctly partnered — is what scores. For how this fits into the marking criteria as a whole, see our breakdown of the <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-band-descriptors">Writing band descriptors</a>.</p>

  <h2>Pollution and waste</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>air/water/plastic pollution</strong> — and note it is "heavy" or "severe" pollution, never "big" or "strong."</li>
    <li><strong>emit greenhouse gases; cut/reduce emissions</strong> — "Factories that emit greenhouse gases should pay for the emissions they produce."</li>
    <li><strong>discharge waste / dump waste illegally</strong> — discharge is the precise verb for factories releasing waste into water.</li>
    <li><strong>single-use plastics; plastic packaging</strong> — "Banning single-use plastics is a popular but partial solution."</li>
    <li><strong>dispose of waste; waste disposal</strong> — remember the preposition: dispose <em>of</em>.</li>
    <li><strong>landfill sites; end up in landfill</strong> — "Most household waste still ends up in landfill."</li>
    <li><strong>exhaust fumes; congestion</strong> — for city-traffic questions: "Charging drivers to enter the city centre would cut both congestion and exhaust fumes."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Climate and energy</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>climate change; global warming</strong> — climate change is the safer general term; "the climate is changing" also works in Speaking.</li>
    <li><strong>carbon footprint; carbon emissions</strong> — you <em>reduce</em> or <em>shrink</em> a footprint: "Flying less is the quickest way for individuals to reduce their carbon footprint."</li>
    <li><strong>renewable energy; fossil fuels</strong> — the classic contrast: "The transition from fossil fuels to renewable energy will define this century."</li>
    <li><strong>solar panels; wind farms</strong> — concrete examples lift Task 2 body paragraphs and Part 3 answers alike.</li>
    <li><strong>energy-efficient; energy consumption</strong> — "Energy-efficient appliances cut household energy consumption without changing behaviour."</li>
    <li><strong>extreme weather events; rising sea levels</strong> — standard, natural phrases for climate consequences.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Conservation and nature</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>protect endangered species; wildlife conservation</strong> — species are "endangered," then "extinct"; the verb is "die out" or "become extinct."</li>
    <li><strong>natural habitats; habitat destruction</strong> — "Urban expansion destroys the natural habitats of countless species."</li>
    <li><strong>deforestation; cut down forests</strong> — use the noun for Writing, the phrasal verb in Speaking: both are natural.</li>
    <li><strong>green spaces; national parks</strong> — for city-planning questions: "Green spaces make cities measurably more liveable."</li>
    <li><strong>ecosystem; the food chain</strong> — "Removing one species can unbalance an entire ecosystem."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Solutions and policy</h2>
  <p>Most environment essays are really policy essays, so the highest-value collocations are the ones for solutions:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>impose a tax on / introduce a ban on</strong> — "Many governments have imposed taxes on plastic bags."</li>
    <li><strong>tighten regulations; enforce laws</strong> — laws exist; the issue is whether they are <em>enforced</em>.</li>
    <li><strong>raise awareness of</strong> — "Campaigns raise awareness of the problem, but awareness alone changes little."</li>
    <li><strong>offer incentives / subsidise</strong> — "Subsidising public transport gives commuters a genuine alternative to driving."</li>
    <li><strong>environmentally friendly; eco-friendly; sustainable</strong> — "sustainable development" and "a sustainable lifestyle" are the natural pairings.</li>
    <li><strong>tackle/address the problem</strong> — far more natural than "solve pollution."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Using them in Writing and Speaking</h2>
  <p>Here is the difference collocation makes in a Task 2 sentence. Flat version: "The government should make rules to stop pollution from cars." Collocated version: "Governments should <strong>tighten emissions regulations</strong> and <strong>subsidise public transport</strong> to <strong>tackle</strong> urban <strong>air pollution</strong> at its source." Same idea, same grammar level, a full criterion apart. In Speaking Part 3, the same phrases work at a more conversational register: "Honestly, I think raising awareness only gets you so far — people know about plastic waste, they just need cheaper alternatives." Practise deploying them against real prompts: the environment appears regularly in our <a href="/ielts-writing-task-2-topics">Task 2 topic bank</a> and across the <a href="/speakingquestion">Speaking practice questions</a>.</p>

  <h2>How to learn these so they stick</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Learn pairings, never single words.</strong> Record "impose a tax on" in your notes, not "impose."</li>
    <li><strong>Write one true sentence per collocation</strong> about your own city or country. Personal sentences are remembered; template sentences are not.</li>
    <li><strong>Use five, not fifty.</strong> In any one essay or answer, a handful of precise collocations used accurately outscores a parade of them. Density reads as memorisation.</li>
    <li><strong>Test them under pressure.</strong> Write a timed essay on an environment prompt and run it through the <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">IELTS writing checker</a> — the Lexical Resource feedback will tell you whether the phrases are landing naturally or being forced.</li>
    <li><strong>Harvest from Reading passages.</strong> Environment passages in the <a href="/readingquestion">Reading bank</a> are full of these pairings in context — when you finish a passage, mine it for three collocations before moving on.</li>
  </ol>
