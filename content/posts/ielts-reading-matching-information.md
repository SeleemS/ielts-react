  <p>Matching Information is the question type most likely to wreck your IELTS Reading timing. You are given a passage split into lettered paragraphs (A, B, C...) and a list of statements, and you must decide which paragraph contains each piece of information. The instruction usually reads: <em>"Which paragraph contains the following information? Write the correct letter A-H in boxes 1-6."</em> Often there is a note saying you may use any letter more than once.</p>
  <p>The reason it eats time is simple: the answers are <strong>not in passage order</strong>. Every other question type lets you move steadily down the text. This one can send you from paragraph G back to paragraph B and forward again. With 40 questions and 60 minutes across three passages, a set of six matching questions can quietly consume fifteen minutes if you attack it wrongly.</p>

  <h2>First, know what it is not</h2>
  <p>Candidates routinely confuse this with <a href="/reading/matching-headings">Matching Headings</a>. The difference decides your whole approach:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Matching Headings</strong> asks for the <em>main idea</em> of a paragraph. You read for the overall point and ignore detail.</li>
    <li><strong>Matching Information</strong> asks where a <em>specific detail</em> appears. That detail may sit in a single sentence in the middle of a long paragraph, and it may have nothing to do with what that paragraph is mainly about.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>This is why skimming for gist fails here. You are hunting a sentence, not a theme.</p>

  <h2>The order to do things in</h2>
  <p>Do Matching Information <strong>last</strong> within its passage. If the passage also has True/False/Not Given, sentence completion or multiple choice, answer those first. They follow passage order, so by the time you finish them you have read most of the text and know roughly where things are. Then the matching set becomes a recall exercise instead of a fresh search.</p>

  <h2>A five-step method for each statement</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Underline the concrete thing in the statement.</strong> Numbers, dates, names, places, and unusual nouns are your anchors — they are hardest for the writer to paraphrase. In "a comparison between two methods of measuring rainfall", the anchor is <em>rainfall</em>, not <em>comparison</em>.</li>
    <li><strong>Decide what kind of information it is.</strong> Statements are often labelled by function: <em>an example of</em>, <em>a reference to</em>, <em>a description of</em>, <em>a reason for</em>, <em>a comparison between</em>, <em>a prediction about</em>. If you are told to find <em>a reason</em>, scan for because, due to, owing to, as a result of. If it is <em>an example</em>, scan for such as, for instance, a case in point.</li>
    <li><strong>Scan, do not read.</strong> Move your eyes down the left and right edges of each paragraph looking only for the anchor word or an obvious synonym. Do not process sentences you are not searching for.</li>
    <li><strong>Read the surrounding two sentences properly</strong> when you get a hit, and check the whole statement is covered — not just the keyword. Half-matches are the trap.</li>
    <li><strong>Write the letter and move on.</strong> Do not go back to confirm; you rarely change a correct answer and you always spend time.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Worked example</h2>
  <p><strong>Statement:</strong> "a reference to the unexpected cost of maintaining the system"</p>
  <p><strong>Paragraph D text:</strong> "Engineers had budgeted for installation alone. What nobody had anticipated was that the sensors would need recalibrating twice a year, an ongoing expense that soon exceeded the original outlay."</p>
  <p>The passage never uses the words <em>unexpected</em>, <em>cost</em> or <em>maintaining</em>. It uses <em>nobody had anticipated</em>, <em>ongoing expense</em> and <em>recalibrating</em>. That is the level of paraphrase you should expect — which is exactly why keyword-spotting alone fails and you must read the hit sentence.</p>

  <h2>Five traps that cost marks</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Assuming each paragraph is used once.</strong> Unless the instructions say so, a paragraph can answer two questions and several paragraphs may answer none. Never eliminate a letter just because you have used it.</li>
    <li><strong>Matching a word instead of a meaning.</strong> The keyword often appears in three paragraphs. Only one of them contains the full statement.</li>
    <li><strong>Answering from the topic sentence.</strong> The detail is frequently buried mid-paragraph, and the topic sentence may point somewhere else entirely.</li>
    <li><strong>Getting stuck.</strong> If a statement resists after roughly 90 seconds, leave it and return once the other letters are placed — elimination does half the work for you.</li>
    <li><strong>Leaving blanks.</strong> There is no penalty for a wrong answer, and in the paper-based test there is no extra transfer time in Reading, so guess before the hour ends rather than after.</li>
  </ul>
