  <p>Education is arguably the single most common topic family in IELTS. It drives Writing Task 2 questions about school subjects, university funding and online learning; it fills Speaking Part 1 (your studies), Part 2 (a teacher who influenced you) and Part 3 (the purpose of education); and it appears constantly in Reading and Listening. Most candidates prepare by hoarding impressive-sounding words — "pedagogy," "meritocracy," "autodidact" — and then bolt them onto sentences that do not quite work. Examiners marking Lexical Resource are listening for something quieter and more valuable: <strong>collocation</strong>, the natural pairing of ordinary words. "Sit an exam" is right; "make an exam" is wrong; both use simple words. This guide gives you the education collocations that matter, grouped by sub-topic, with notes on accurate use.</p>

  <h2>Why collocations beat rare words</h2>
  <p>The Band 7 descriptors for both Writing and Speaking explicitly reward collocation awareness and precision, not exotic vocabulary. A candidate who writes "universities should <strong>charge lower tuition fees</strong> so more students can <strong>pursue a degree</strong>" is demonstrating exactly that — every pairing is natural. A candidate who writes "universities should do cheaper money so more students can do a degree" knows the ideas but not the pairings; a candidate who writes "tertiary institutions should recalibrate their pecuniary thresholds" has memorised rare words and produced something no fluent writer would say. Ordinary words, correctly partnered, is what scores. For how this fits the marking as a whole, see our breakdown of the <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-band-descriptors">Writing band descriptors</a>.</p>

  <h2>School and studying</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>attend school / go to university</strong> — you "attend" a school and "enrol on" or "enrol in" a course.</li>
    <li><strong>a compulsory / an optional subject</strong> — "In many countries, a second language is a compulsory subject until age sixteen."</li>
    <li><strong>the school curriculum; the syllabus</strong> — the curriculum is the whole programme; a syllabus is one course's content.</li>
    <li><strong>do / take homework and assignments</strong> — you "hand in" or "submit" an assignment by its "deadline."</li>
    <li><strong>sit an exam; take a test; resit an exam</strong> — never "make an exam"; if you fail, you "resit" it.</li>
    <li><strong>pass / fail an exam; get good grades</strong> — "Students who get good grades are not always the ones who learn the most."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Higher education and cost</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>pursue / study for a degree; graduate from university</strong> — "She graduated from university with a degree in engineering."</li>
    <li><strong>tuition fees; student loans; a scholarship</strong> — students "take out a loan," "pay off" a loan, or "win a scholarship."</li>
    <li><strong>an undergraduate / a postgraduate course</strong> — precise terms that lift a Task 2 body paragraph.</li>
    <li><strong>higher education; vocational training</strong> — the natural contrast for essays that weigh degrees against practical skills.</li>
    <li><strong>gain a qualification; a recognised qualification</strong> — "Employers increasingly value skills over a formal qualification."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Teaching and learning</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>teaching methods; a hands-on / interactive approach</strong> — "Interactive teaching methods keep younger learners engaged."</li>
    <li><strong>acquire knowledge / develop skills</strong> — you "acquire" knowledge and "develop" or "build" skills; do not swap the verbs.</li>
    <li><strong>rote learning; memorisation</strong> — the standard critical term: "Rote learning produces high test scores but shallow understanding."</li>
    <li><strong>critical thinking; problem-solving skills</strong> — the pairings examiners expect when you argue for the purpose of education.</li>
    <li><strong>distance learning; online courses</strong> — "Distance learning widens access but demands far more self-discipline."</li>
    <li><strong>a well-rounded education; lifelong learning</strong> — high-value phrases for Part 3 and Task 2 conclusions alike.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Using them in Writing and Speaking</h2>
  <p>Here is the difference collocation makes in a Task 2 sentence. Flat version: "The government should give money to schools so students can learn better things." Collocated version: "Governments should <strong>fund state schools</strong> more generously so that every child can <strong>develop critical thinking skills</strong>, not merely <strong>pass exams</strong>." Same idea, same grammar level, a full criterion apart. In Speaking, the same phrases work at a more conversational register: "Honestly, I learned more from one hands-on project than from a whole year of memorisation." Practise deploying them against real prompts across the <a href="/speakingquestion">Speaking practice questions</a>, where education themes appear in all three parts.</p>

  <h2>How to learn these so they stick</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Learn pairings, never single words.</strong> Record "take out a student loan," not "loan."</li>
    <li><strong>Write one true sentence per collocation</strong> about your own education. Personal sentences are remembered; template sentences are not.</li>
    <li><strong>Use five, not fifty.</strong> A handful of precise collocations used accurately outscores a parade of them; density reads as memorisation.</li>
    <li><strong>Test them under pressure.</strong> Write a timed education essay and run it through the free <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">IELTS writing checker</a> — the Lexical Resource feedback tells you whether the phrases are landing naturally or being forced.</li>
  </ol>
