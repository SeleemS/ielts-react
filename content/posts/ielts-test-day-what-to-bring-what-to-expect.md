  <p>Most candidates spend months on grammar and vocabulary and then lose easy marks on test day to something completely avoidable — the wrong ID, a watch left on the wrist, or a misunderstanding about how the Listening transfer time works. This guide walks you through exactly what to bring, what happens from the moment you arrive, and the logistical traps that quietly drag scores down. Get these right and you free up all your attention for the parts that actually test your English.</p>

  <h2>What to bring — and what will get you turned away</h2>
  <p>The single non-negotiable item is your <strong>identification</strong>. You must bring the <strong>exact same ID document you used when you registered</strong> — usually a valid passport, or a national identity card for some test types and locations. If the ID at the door does not match your booking, you will not be allowed to sit the test, and no amount of preparation changes that. Check the document is unexpired and that the name matches your registration precisely.</p>
  <p>Beyond ID, keep it minimal. For the pen-and-paper test you may bring pencils, an eraser and a pen, though many centres supply stationery. You may usually bring a <strong>transparent water bottle with the label removed</strong>. Almost everything else stays outside the exam room:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Phones and smartwatches must be switched off</strong> and handed in or stored. A phone that makes a sound during the test can be treated as malpractice.</li>
    <li><strong>No wristwatch of any kind.</strong> There is a clock in the room; watches are not permitted on the desk or your wrist.</li>
    <li>No notes, dictionaries, or paper of your own. Rough work is done on the question paper (paper test) or on-screen scratch space (computer test).</li>
  </ul>
  <p>You will also have a <strong>photograph taken at the centre</strong> and, in many locations, a fingerprint or other identity check. Arrive around <strong>30 minutes before the start time</strong> so registration, the photo and security checks are done calmly rather than in a panic.</p>

  <h2>The running order of the test</h2>
  <p>On test day the three written papers are taken <strong>back to back in one sitting, with no break between them</strong>, in this fixed order:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Listening</strong> — 4 sections, roughly 30 minutes of audio, 40 questions.</li>
    <li><strong>Reading</strong> — 60 minutes, 40 questions.</li>
    <li><strong>Writing</strong> — 60 minutes, two tasks.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>That is about <strong>2 hours 45 minutes</strong> of continuous testing. Your <strong>Speaking</strong> test (11–14 minutes, face to face with an examiner) may be on the same day or up to a few days before or after — check your confirmation carefully so you do not miss it. If you are still deciding which format to sit, our guide to <a href="/blog/computer-delivered-vs-paper-based-ielts">computer-delivered vs paper-based IELTS</a> explains how the two differ on the day.</p>

  <h2>The Listening transfer-time trap</h2>
  <p>This is where format matters most. On the <strong>paper-based test</strong>, the recording plays once and you write answers on the question paper as you listen; you then get an extra <strong>10 minutes at the end to transfer your answers onto the answer sheet</strong>. Use that time deliberately — and mind your <strong>spelling and word limits</strong>, because a correctly heard answer spelled wrong scores zero.</p>
  <p>On the <strong>computer-delivered test</strong> there is <strong>no 10-minute transfer</strong>; you type answers directly and get only about <strong>2 minutes to check</strong> at the end. Candidates who expect the extra ten minutes are caught out. Know which one you are sitting and rehearse accordingly. You can practise both under realistic timing on our <a href="/listeningquestion">IELTS Listening questions</a> page.</p>

  <h2>Small logistics that quietly cost marks</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Reading has no transfer time.</strong> Unlike Listening, you must write answers straight onto the answer sheet within the 60 minutes — do not plan to copy them over at the end.</li>
    <li><strong>Watch the Writing split.</strong> Task 2 is worth twice Task 1, so aim for roughly <strong>20 minutes on Task 1 and 40 on Task 2</strong>, and leave a few minutes to check.</li>
    <li><strong>Answer every question.</strong> There is no negative marking, so a blank is a guaranteed zero while a guess has a chance.</li>
    <li><strong>Follow word limits exactly.</strong> "No more than two words and/or a number" means a three-word answer is marked wrong even if the meaning is right.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The night before and the morning of</h2>
  <p>Lay out your ID and permitted items the night before, confirm your test centre address and start time, and plan your journey with margin for delays. Sleep beats last-minute cramming — by test day your English is what it is, and a rested, calm brain reads faster and hears detail more accurately than a tired, anxious one.</p>
  <p>Finally, go in with a realistic idea of where you stand so nerves do not push you into rushing. Sit a timed practice set, mark it honestly, and convert your raw scores with our free <a href="/band-calculator">IELTS band score calculator</a> to see the band you are walking in with — then you can focus on execution instead of guessing.</p>
