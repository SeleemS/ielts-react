  <p>The <strong>"positive or negative development"</strong> question is one of the most misread prompts in IELTS Writing Task 2. It describes a change happening in the world and asks whether you regard that change as a good thing or a bad thing. Candidates lose marks not because their English is weak, but because they treat it like an agree/disagree essay, forget to evaluate the <em>development</em> itself, or sit on the fence when the question demands a clear judgement. This guide shows you how to recognise the type, take a defensible position, and structure an answer that satisfies all four marking criteria.</p>

  <h2>What the question actually asks</h2>
  <p>The prompt names a trend and then asks for your evaluation of it. Typical wordings include:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>"In many countries, people are choosing to have children later in life. Do you think this is a <strong>positive or negative development</strong>?"</li>
    <li>"More and more people are working from home rather than in an office. Is this a positive or negative development?"</li>
    <li>Variants ask whether a change is a "positive or negative trend," or whether "the advantages outweigh the disadvantages" of the change.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Two things define the type. First, the question is about a <strong>development</strong> — a change or trend — not a static opinion, so your whole essay must evaluate that change. Second, the word "you" makes it a personal-opinion question: the examiner wants <em>your</em> clear stance, held consistently from the introduction to the conclusion.</p>

  <h2>Why it is not an agree/disagree essay</h2>
  <p>The two types feel similar because both ask for a position, but the task is different. An <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-agree-or-disagree">agree or disagree</a> question gives you a fixed statement to accept or reject. A positive/negative question gives you a neutral trend and asks you to judge its <em>effects</em>. That means your body paragraphs should be built around consequences — what the change leads to — rather than around whether a claim is true. Mislabelling the type is the single most common way candidates drift off task and lose marks under <strong>Task Response</strong>, which is one of the four equally weighted criteria (alongside Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy), each worth 25% of your Task 2 band.</p>

  <h2>Choosing your position</h2>
  <p>You have three defensible options, and all can reach Band 9 if handled well:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Mostly positive.</strong> Argue the change is largely beneficial, then acknowledge one limitation briefly.</li>
    <li><strong>Mostly negative.</strong> Argue the drawbacks dominate, while conceding one genuine benefit.</li>
    <li><strong>Both, but on balance one side.</strong> Present it as a mixed development, but still commit to which side you think outweighs the other.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>What you must not do is refuse to decide. "It is both positive and negative" with no verdict answers the question only halfway and caps your Task Response. Pick the side you can develop with the clearest examples — not the side you privately believe. Your job is to argue a position well, not to be sincere.</p>

  <h2>A reliable four-paragraph structure</h2>
  <h3>Introduction (2–3 sentences)</h3>
  <p>Paraphrase the trend in your own words, then state your position outright. For a "later parenthood" prompt: "It is increasingly common for people to delay having children until their thirties or beyond. In my view, this is a largely <strong>positive development</strong>, as it allows for greater financial and emotional readiness, though it carries some medical risk." The examiner now knows your stance in the first three lines.</p>
  <h3>Body paragraph 1 — your main line of argument</h3>
  <p>Develop the strongest reason your judgement holds. Use a <strong>point–explain–example–link</strong> pattern: state the consequence, explain the mechanism, give a concrete illustration, then tie it back to the word "positive" or "negative." Depth wins here — two fully developed reasons beat five thin ones.</p>
  <h3>Body paragraph 2 — a second reason, or the concession</h3>
  <p>Either add a second supporting reason, or use this paragraph to concede the opposing side and then explain why it does not change your overall verdict. A well-handled concession actually strengthens your position because it shows balanced thinking without abandoning your stance.</p>
  <h3>Conclusion (2 sentences)</h3>
  <p>Restate your judgement and summarise why. Introduce no new ideas. The conclusion should echo the position you set out in the introduction so the essay reads as one consistent argument.</p>

  <h2>The mistakes that quietly cost marks</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Describing the trend instead of evaluating it.</strong> Explaining <em>why</em> people work from home is off task; you must judge whether it is <em>good or bad</em>.</li>
    <li><strong>Fence-sitting.</strong> No clear verdict means an incomplete Task Response, however fluent the English.</li>
    <li><strong>Switching sides.</strong> Arguing "positive" in the introduction and "negative" in the conclusion breaks coherence and confuses the examiner about your position.</li>
    <li><strong>Ignoring the second half of a two-part prompt.</strong> If the question adds "and what problems might this cause?", you must answer both halves.</li>
    <li><strong>Memorised idea-dumps.</strong> Generic advantages pasted from a template rarely fit the specific trend and read as off-topic.</li>
  </ul>
