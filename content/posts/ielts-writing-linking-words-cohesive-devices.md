  <p><strong>Linking words</strong> — also called cohesive devices or connectors — are one of the most misunderstood parts of IELTS Writing. Candidates memorise long lists of "advanced" connectors, then stuff them into every sentence believing this signals a higher band. In reality, examiners are trained to spot the <em>mechanical, overused</em> linking that actually lowers your score. This guide explains what cohesion really means, which devices to use, and how to make your writing flow without sounding like a template.</p>

  <h2>What examiners are actually marking</h2>
  <p>Linking words fall under <strong>Coherence and Cohesion</strong>, one of the four equally weighted criteria in both Writing tasks, each worth 25% of that task's band (the others being Task Response/Achievement, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy). Crucially, the band descriptors reward the <em>logical progression of ideas</em> first, and connectors second. The Band 7 descriptor specifically warns against <strong>over-use or under-use of cohesive devices</strong>, and it is over-use that trips up most candidates aiming for 7.</p>
  <p>In other words: a paragraph whose ideas already follow one another logically needs very few connectors. Cohesion is not a layer of words you sprinkle on top — it is the underlying order of your thinking made visible.</p>

  <h2>The categories that cover almost everything</h2>
  <p>You do not need fifty connectors. You need a reliable handful in each function and the judgement to know when a sentence needs none. Here are the categories that matter:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Adding a point:</strong> in addition, furthermore, moreover, also.</li>
    <li><strong>Contrast:</strong> however, on the other hand, nevertheless, whereas, while.</li>
    <li><strong>Cause and effect:</strong> as a result, consequently, therefore, because of this.</li>
    <li><strong>Giving examples:</strong> for instance, for example, such as, a case in point.</li>
    <li><strong>Sequencing:</strong> first, then, finally (use sparingly — mostly in Task 1 process descriptions).</li>
    <li><strong>Concluding:</strong> in conclusion, to sum up, overall.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Notice how ordinary most of these are. "However" and "for example" are fine at Band 8. Reaching for "notwithstanding" or "in light of the aforementioned" does not raise your band; it raises the risk of using the word wrongly, which costs you under Lexical Resource instead.</p>

  <h2>Cohesion is more than connectors</h2>
  <p>The highest-scoring writing links ideas in ways that have nothing to do with "linking words" at all. Three techniques do the heavy lifting:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Referencing.</strong> Use pronouns and words like <em>this</em>, <em>these</em>, <em>such</em>, and <em>it</em> to point back to earlier ideas: "Governments subsidise public transport. <em>This</em> encourages…" The word "this" links two sentences more smoothly than "Moreover" ever could.</li>
    <li><strong>Substitution and synonyms.</strong> Refer to the same idea with varied wording — "cars" then "these vehicles" — so your writing coheres without repetition.</li>
    <li><strong>Topic sentences.</strong> Starting each body paragraph with a clear controlling idea is itself a cohesive device: it tells the reader what the whole paragraph is about before you develop it.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>The over-use trap, shown</h2>
  <p>Compare two versions of the same point.</p>
  <p><strong>Over-linked (Band 6 feel):</strong> "Firstly, cars cause pollution. Moreover, they create traffic. In addition, they are expensive. Furthermore, therefore, governments should act. However, on the other hand, people like them."</p>
  <p><strong>Natural (Band 7–8 feel):</strong> "Cars are a major source of urban pollution, and their sheer number clogs city roads at peak times. Running one is also costly. For these reasons, many governments now discourage car use — though drivers value the convenience too much to give it up easily."</p>
  <p>The second version uses <em>fewer</em> connectors, links ideas through meaning and reference, and reads like a person thinking, not a checklist. That is exactly what the Band 7+ descriptors reward.</p>

  <h2>Practical rules that keep you safe</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>One connector per idea, not per sentence.</strong> If two connectors sit close together ("Furthermore, therefore…"), delete one.</li>
    <li><strong>Do not start every sentence with a linker.</strong> Vary sentence openings; many should begin with the subject.</li>
    <li><strong>Punctuate correctly.</strong> "However" at the start of a sentence takes a comma after it; it cannot join two independent clauses with only a comma before it.</li>
    <li><strong>Avoid speech-only connectors.</strong> "Well," "anyway," and "plus" are too informal for the essay.</li>
    <li><strong>Never let a memorised phrase replace an idea.</strong> Templated openers padded with connectors read as off-topic and hurt Task Response.</li>
  </ul>
