  <p>The first topic most candidates meet in IELTS Speaking Part 1 is where they live. After checking your identity, the examiner almost always begins with your home, your hometown, or the area you live in — familiar ground chosen deliberately, because Part 1 is designed to let you warm up on subjects you can discuss without thinking hard. That makes this topic family the best possible place to rehearse your Part 1 technique: if you can answer these questions fluently, you start every test with momentum.</p>

  <h2>What Part 1 is actually testing</h2>
  <p>Part 1 lasts four to five minutes and covers two or three everyday topics. The examiner is not interested in your opinions about urban planning; they are listening for the same four criteria that run through the whole test: <strong>fluency and coherence</strong>, <strong>lexical resource</strong>, <strong>grammatical range and accuracy</strong>, and <strong>pronunciation</strong>. In practical terms, a strong Part 1 answer does three things: it answers the question directly, it adds a reason or a detail, and it stops. Two to four sentences is the sweet spot. One-word answers give the examiner nothing to assess; two-minute monologues suggest you have memorised a speech.</p>

  <h2>Original model answers: home and hometown</h2>
  <p>The answers below are <strong>original model answers written for practice on this site</strong> — they are not transcripts of real exam interviews, and you should not memorise them word for word. Use them as templates: notice the shape of each answer, the natural vocabulary, and the way each one extends the response with a reason or example.</p>

  <h3>Where is your hometown?</h3>
  <p><em>"I'm from a mid-sized city in the north of my country, about two hours from the capital by train. It's mainly known for its university and a river that runs right through the centre, so it has quite a young, lively feel despite being fairly small."</em></p>
  <p><strong>Why it works:</strong> a direct answer, then two concrete details. "Mid-sized," "runs right through the centre," and "a young, lively feel" are natural collocations, not forced formal vocabulary.</p>

  <h3>What do you like most about your hometown?</h3>
  <p><em>"Probably how walkable it is. I can get from my flat to the market, the gym and my old school in about fifteen minutes on foot, which I really took for granted until I visited bigger cities and spent half my day in traffic."</em></p>
  <p><strong>Why it works:</strong> "Probably" is a natural spoken opener; the answer develops with a specific example and a light comparison, and the phrase "took for granted" is a high-value idiomatic chunk used accurately.</p>

  <h3>Has your hometown changed much since you were a child?</h3>
  <p><em>"Yes, quite a lot actually. When I was young it was mostly low-rise buildings and family shops, but over the last decade they've built a shopping centre and a couple of apartment complexes. It feels more modern now, though some of the character has been lost, to be honest."</em></p>
  <p><strong>Why it works:</strong> this question invites a tense contrast — past state, present result — and the answer delivers it: "was mostly," "they've built," "feels more modern now." A balanced comment ("though some of the character has been lost") adds coherence without turning into an essay.</p>

  <h3>Would you like to live there in the future?</h3>
  <p><em>"I'm honestly not sure. If I could find a good job there I'd consider it, because my whole family is nearby. But realistically, most opportunities in my field are in the capital, so I'll probably end up there for at least a few years."</em></p>
  <p><strong>Why it works:</strong> a conditional ("If I could find… I'd consider it") plus a future form ("I'll probably end up") shows grammatical range naturally. Uncertainty is fine — you are graded on language, not on having a plan.</p>

  <h3>Do you live in a house or an apartment?</h3>
  <p><em>"An apartment, on the fifth floor of a fairly new building. It's not huge, but it gets a lot of natural light in the morning, which is the thing I'd miss most if I moved."</em></p>

  <h3>What's your favourite room in your home?</h3>
  <p><em>"The kitchen, without a doubt. It's where everyone ends up when friends come over, and I've got a small table by the window where I usually work and have my coffee. It's the most lived-in part of the flat."</em></p>

  <h3>Is there anything you'd like to change about your home?</h3>
  <p><em>"If I could, I'd add more storage. The flat came with almost no built-in cupboards, so things pile up. Apart from that, I'd probably repaint the living room — the previous owner chose a fairly gloomy grey."</em></p>

  <h2>The pattern behind every answer</h2>
  <p>Look back through the models and you will see one repeating shape: <strong>direct answer → reason or detail → optional extra comment</strong>. That is the whole method. It keeps you fluent because you always know what comes next, it produces the connected speech the fluency criterion rewards, and it naturally generates range — reasons need "because" and conditionals, details need description, comparisons need comparatives.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Answer the actual question first.</strong> If the examiner asks whether you like your hometown, your first phrase should make your answer clear — "I do, mostly" — before you explain.</li>
    <li><strong>Use everyday precision, not rare words.</strong> "Walkable," "low-rise," "lived-in" and "gloomy" score better than misused formal vocabulary like "aesthetically pleasing metropolis." Accuracy and appropriacy beat rarity.</li>
    <li><strong>Vary your openers.</strong> "Probably…," "Honestly…," "Yes, quite a lot actually…" — natural spoken discourse markers signal fluency. Starting every answer with "Well, in my opinion" signals memorisation.</li>
    <li><strong>Let tenses do the work.</strong> Hometown questions are a gift for range: past habits, present description, recent changes with the present perfect, and future intentions can all appear across one topic without any forcing.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Mistakes that hold candidates at Band 6</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>The one-line answer.</strong> "It's a small city. I like it." gives the examiner nothing to credit. Always add one reason or detail.</li>
    <li><strong>The rehearsed speech.</strong> Examiners hear the same memorised hometown paragraph daily, and delivery flattens the moment a script starts. Prepare ideas and phrases, never full answers.</li>
    <li><strong>Upgrading into error.</strong> Swapping "beautiful" for "picturesque" is only an upgrade if you pronounce it and place it correctly. An accurate simple word always beats a broken impressive one.</li>
    <li><strong>Answering a different question.</strong> If asked about your favourite room, describing your whole flat costs coherence marks. Listen for the question's focus word and stay on it.</li>
  </ul>
