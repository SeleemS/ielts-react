  <p>Of the four things an examiner marks you on in <strong>IELTS Speaking</strong>, pronunciation is the one candidates worry about most and train least. Many assume it means "sounding British or American," panic that their accent will hold them back, and then do nothing about it. That is a mistake worth avoiding, because pronunciation is one of <strong>four equally weighted criteria</strong> — alongside Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy — so it accounts for a full <strong>25%</strong> of your Speaking band. The good news: the examiner is not listening for a native accent at all. This guide explains what pronunciation actually measures and gives you concrete features to practise.</p>

  <h2>What the examiner is really marking</h2>
  <p>Read the public band descriptors and one word appears again and again: <strong>intelligibility</strong>. At Band 8 the descriptor says your speech "is easy to understand throughout" and that a first-language accent "has minimal effect on intelligibility." Nowhere does it ask you to erase your accent. What separates the bands is two things: how <strong>easy you are to understand</strong>, and how wide a <strong>range of pronunciation features</strong> you use and sustain.</p>
  <p>In plain terms, the ladder looks like this:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Band 6</strong> — you use a range of features with mixed control; you can generally be understood, but mispronunciation of individual words or sounds sometimes reduces clarity.</li>
    <li><strong>Band 7</strong> — you show all the positive features of Band 6 <em>and</em> some of Band 8, holding effective pronunciation for longer stretches.</li>
    <li><strong>Band 8</strong> — you use a wide range of features, sustain flexible control with only occasional lapses, and are easy to understand throughout.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Notice what moves you up: not accent reduction, but <strong>control</strong> and <strong>consistency</strong>. You can learn both. For the full picture of how the other three criteria interact with this one, see our <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors">breakdown of the Speaking band descriptors</a>.</p>

  <h2>The five features that actually raise your band</h2>
  <p>"Pronunciation" is really a bundle of skills. Work on these five, in roughly this order of impact.</p>
  <h3>1. Word stress</h3>
  <p>English puts a strong beat on one syllable in every longer word, and getting it wrong is a top cause of "sorry, could you repeat that?" Say <em>com-FOR-table</em>, not <em>COM-for-TABLE</em>; <em>pho-TO-graph-y</em>, not <em>PHO-to-graph-y</em>. When you learn a new word for the test, learn where the stress falls at the same time.</p>
  <h3>2. Sentence stress</h3>
  <p>Fluent speakers hit the <strong>content words</strong> — nouns, main verbs, adjectives — and glide over the small grammar words. "I was <strong>thinking</strong> about <strong>moving</strong> to a <strong>bigger city</strong>" has four beats; the rest is unstressed. Flattening every word into the same weight is what makes speech sound robotic and hard to follow.</p>
  <h3>3. Intonation</h3>
  <p>Your pitch should rise and fall, not stay flat. Falling intonation signals you have finished a thought; a slight rise can signal a list or a question. Monotone delivery caps you no matter how accurate your grammar is, because it makes you tiring to listen to.</p>
  <h3>4. Chunking and pausing</h3>
  <p>Group words into meaningful phrases and pause <em>between</em> them, not in the middle. "When I was younger / I used to play tennis / almost every weekend" is clear; pausing after "used" or "every" is not. Good pausing also buys you thinking time without sounding lost.</p>
  <h3>5. Individual sounds</h3>
  <p>Finally, target the specific sounds your first language does not have — the <em>th</em> in "think," the difference between <em>ship</em> and <em>sheep</em>, or final consonants many speakers drop. You do not need every sound perfect; you need the ones that cause misunderstanding fixed.</p>

  <h2>How to practise so it actually transfers</h2>
  <p>Reading pronunciation tips does nothing; your mouth has to build new habits. Three methods work better than the rest:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Shadowing.</strong> Play 20–30 seconds of a clear English speaker — a podcast, a news clip — and speak along a beat behind, copying their rhythm and melody, not just the words. Do this daily for ten minutes.</li>
    <li><strong>Record and compare.</strong> Record yourself answering a <a href="/speakingquestion">Speaking practice question</a>, then listen back critically. You will hear your own flat intonation or dropped endings far more easily than you can feel them while talking.</li>
    <li><strong>Mark the stress.</strong> Take a model answer, underline the stressed syllables and content words, then read it aloud until the rhythm feels natural. Transfer the habit to your own speech afterwards.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Mistakes that quietly cost you marks</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Speaking too fast to sound fluent.</strong> Speed is not fluency. Rushing blurs your stress and sounds and drags your score down. A clear, well-paced answer beats a fast, mumbled one every time.</li>
    <li><strong>Over-correcting into a fake accent.</strong> Straining for a native accent usually makes you <em>less</em> intelligible. Keep your natural accent; just make it clear and controlled.</li>
    <li><strong>Memorised answers.</strong> Rehearsed scripts have a giveaway sing-song rhythm examiners are trained to spot, and it hurts your pronunciation and fluency marks at once.</li>
    <li><strong>Ignoring the ends of words.</strong> Dropping final sounds ("nex" for "next," "wan" for "want") is one of the most common intelligibility problems — and one of the easiest to fix once you notice it.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Put it in context</h2>
  <p>Pronunciation is 25% of Speaking, and Speaking is one of four sections that each contribute a quarter of your <strong>overall band</strong>, rounded to the nearest whole or half band. So clear, controlled pronunciation lifts a full quarter of a quarter of your final score — a bigger lever than most candidates realise. To see how a stronger Speaking result changes your overall number, try our free <a href="/band-calculator">IELTS band score calculator</a> and model different combinations before test day.</p>
  <p>You will not shed your accent in a few weeks, and you should not try to. What you <em>can</em> do is train stress, intonation, chunking and the handful of sounds that trip listeners up — the exact features the descriptors reward. Ten focused minutes a day, recorded and reviewed, will move you further than any amount of worrying about how "native" you sound.</p>
