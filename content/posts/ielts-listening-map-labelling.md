  <p>Map and plan labelling is the question type candidates fear most in IELTS Listening, and the fear is misplaced. The vocabulary is simple. The recording is not fast. What defeats people is orientation: they lose track of where the speaker is on the page, miss one label, and then miss the three that follow. This guide fixes that.</p>

  <h2>Where this question type appears</h2>
  <p>IELTS Listening has four parts, around 30 minutes of audio, and 40 questions in total — ten per part. Map, plan and diagram labelling usually appears in Part 2, a monologue in an everyday social context: a tour guide describing a park, a manager explaining a new office layout, a volunteer coordinator walking through a festival site. Occasionally a labelled diagram of a process or object appears in Part 4.</p>
  <p>Two rules make the type manageable. First, the answers come <strong>in the order they appear on the recording</strong>. Second, you are almost always choosing from a lettered box of options or writing a word taken directly from the audio. You are not being asked to invent anything.</p>

  <h2>The 30 seconds before the recording starts</h2>
  <p>You get preparation time before each part. On a map task, spend it doing four specific things rather than reading the labels passively.</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Find the entrance, the "you are here" marker, or the compass.</strong> This is where the speaker will begin, and it defines what left and right mean.</li>
    <li><strong>Say the fixed landmarks to yourself.</strong> Reception, car park, lake, main road. These are the anchors the speaker will describe things in relation to.</li>
    <li><strong>Number the gaps in reading order and note which are close together.</strong> Two gaps side by side means two labels arriving in quick succession — that is where people fall behind.</li>
    <li><strong>Predict the ordering.</strong> If the gaps run clockwise from the entrance, expect the speaker to move that way too.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Direction language you must recognise instantly</h2>
  <p>Nearly every map answer hinges on one preposition or phrase. If you have to translate it, you have already missed the next sentence. Learn these until they are automatic:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Position:</strong> opposite, facing, adjacent to, next to, beside, between, behind, in front of, at the far end, at the rear</li>
    <li><strong>Movement:</strong> head towards, follow the path, go past, carry on, turn left at, take the second turning, cross over</li>
    <li><strong>Relative:</strong> just before you reach, immediately after, directly north of, on your right-hand side, halfway along, in the top left-hand corner</li>
    <li><strong>Corrections:</strong> actually, sorry, I mean, it used to be there but, we've since moved it</li>
  </ul>
  <p>That last group is the real trap. Speakers frequently name a location and then correct themselves. The answer is always what they say <em>after</em> the correction. This mirrors the distractor patterns described in our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-common-traps">the most common IELTS Listening traps</a>.</p>

  <h2>Listen with your finger, not your memory</h2>
  <p>During the recording, physically track your position on the map with a pen or your cursor. Move it as the speaker moves. This sounds trivial; it is the single biggest difference between candidates who score 8 or 9 on this task type and those who score 5.</p>
  <p>The second habit is equally important: <strong>if you miss one, let it go immediately</strong>. Write nothing, keep tracking, and catch the next landmark. Candidates lose four marks trying to recover one. Because answers arrive in order, the following question tells you exactly where the speaker now is — but only if you are still listening rather than replaying the last ten seconds in your head.</p>

  <h2>A worked example</h2>
  <p>Suppose the audio says: <em>"From the main entrance, follow the path with the lake on your left. You'll go past the café — that's the building with the glass roof — and the first building after that, on your right, is the equipment store."</em></p>
  <p>Three pieces of information do the work here. The path direction fixes your orientation. "Go past the café" tells you the café is <strong>not</strong> the answer, even though it is named first and named clearly. "The first building after that, on your right" pinpoints one option. Candidates who label the café have not misheard anything — they simply stopped tracking movement.</p>

  <h2>Common mistakes to eliminate</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Assuming north is up.</strong> If a compass is printed, check it. Some maps are rotated deliberately.</li>
    <li><strong>Reusing a letter.</strong> Unless the instructions say otherwise, each option is used once. A repeated letter means one answer is wrong.</li>
    <li><strong>Writing more words than allowed.</strong> "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS" is scored strictly. Three words is zero, even if the meaning is right.</li>
    <li><strong>Misspelling a landmark.</strong> Spelling counts in Listening. If the word is printed elsewhere on the map, copy it exactly.</li>
    <li><strong>Leaving blanks.</strong> There is no penalty for a wrong answer. Guess a plausible letter for anything you missed.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>How to practise this properly</h2>
  <p>Do not do one map task and move on. Do five in a row, then review differently: replay each recording with the transcript open and highlight the exact phrase that gave each answer. You will find the same twenty expressions doing the work every time, and after a week they stop sounding fast.</p>
