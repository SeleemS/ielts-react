  <p>Paraphrasing — saying the same thing in different words — is the single most transferable skill in IELTS. It is how you rewrite the question in your Writing introduction, how you spot the answer in Reading when the passage never uses the words from the question, how you decode a Listening distractor, and how you avoid repeating yourself in Speaking. Yet most candidates paraphrase badly: they swap a couple of words for thesaurus synonyms and hope for the best. This guide gives you five reliable techniques, worked examples for each, and the mistakes that turn a good paraphrase into a bad one.</p>

  <h2>Why paraphrasing matters in all four skills</h2>
  <p>Examiners and test writers deliberately avoid word-for-word matches. In <strong>Reading and Listening</strong>, the correct answer is almost always a paraphrase of the text — the question says "children," the passage says "youngsters"; the recording says "the meeting was postponed," the question asks what happened to it. If you only scan for identical words, you will miss answers and fall for traps that repeat the question wording exactly. In <strong>Writing and Speaking</strong>, paraphrasing is scored directly under <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, one of the four equally weighted criteria. Rewording the prompt instead of copying it shows range; repeating the same phrase over and over caps your band.</p>

  <h2>Technique 1: Synonyms — but only where they fit</h2>
  <p>The obvious move is replacing words with synonyms, and it works — as long as the synonym genuinely fits the context. "Cars" becomes "vehicles," "big" becomes "significant," "children" becomes "youngsters." The danger is that few synonyms are perfect. "Countries" and "nations" are interchangeable; "economical" and "economic" are not. Rule of thumb: change the words you are confident about and leave the rest. A precise original beats a wrong synonym every time.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Original:</strong> The chart shows the number of visitors to three museums.</li>
    <li><strong>Paraphrase:</strong> The graph illustrates how many people visited three different galleries.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Technique 2: Change the word form</h2>
  <p>You often do not need a different word at all — just a different <em>form</em> of the same word. Turn a verb into a noun, an adjective into a noun, or an adjective into an adverb. This changes the sentence structure around it, which reads as far more sophisticated than a bare synonym swap.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>pollute (verb) → pollution (noun):</strong> "Factories pollute rivers" → "The pollution of rivers by factories…"</li>
    <li><strong>important (adjective) → importance (noun):</strong> "Education is important" → "the importance of education"</li>
    <li><strong>successful (adjective) → successfully (adverb):</strong> "a successful policy" → "the policy was implemented successfully"</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Technique 3: Change the voice — active to passive</h2>
  <p>Switching between active and passive voice lets you rewrite a sentence while keeping every content word. This is especially powerful in Reading and Listening, where the same fact is stated from the opposite direction.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Active:</strong> The government introduced the law in 2015.</li>
    <li><strong>Passive:</strong> The law was introduced by the government in 2015.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>In Listening, a recording might say "The council <em>rejected</em> the proposal," while the question asks "The proposal was <em>turned down</em>." Recognising that the actor and the receiver have swapped places is often the whole trick.</p>

  <h2>Technique 4: Change the sentence structure</h2>
  <p>Reordering the parts of a sentence — moving a time phrase to the front, combining two ideas with a relative clause, or splitting one long sentence into two — produces a genuinely different sentence rather than a lightly edited copy. This is what separates a real paraphrase from a disguised copy.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Original:</strong> Online shopping has grown rapidly because it is convenient.</li>
    <li><strong>Paraphrase:</strong> Owing to its convenience, e-commerce has expanded at a rapid pace.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Notice that this example combines all the techniques so far: a synonym (online shopping → e-commerce), a word-form change (grown → expanded / rapidly → rapid pace), and a structural change (moving the reason to the front). Strong paraphrasing almost always stacks two or three techniques at once.</p>

  <h2>Technique 5: Use a defining relative clause or a change of opposites</h2>
  <p>When no clean synonym exists, describe the idea instead. "Unemployed people" becomes "people who do not have a job." You can also paraphrase by <strong>negating the opposite</strong>: "cheap" becomes "not expensive," "increased" becomes "did not fall." Use this sparingly — it is a rescue tool for when a direct synonym escapes you, not your first choice, because overused it makes writing wordy.</p>

  <h2>The three mistakes that make paraphrasing backfire</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Blind thesaurus swaps.</strong> Replacing "help" with "facilitate" or "big problem" with "gargantuan quandary" produces English no native speaker would write. Wrong or unnatural word choice lowers Lexical Resource instead of raising it. Only use words you truly know.</li>
    <li><strong>Changing the meaning.</strong> A paraphrase must say <em>exactly</em> the same thing. If the question is about "the main cause" and you write "one cause," you have changed the claim — and in Writing that costs you Task Response, while in Reading it makes a True/False/Not Given answer wrong.</li>
    <li><strong>Paraphrasing everything.</strong> Some words have no good synonym and should be kept: proper nouns, numbers, and technical terms like "photosynthesis" or "GDP." Trying to reword these creates errors. Keep them as they are.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>How to practise until it becomes automatic</h2>
  <p>Paraphrasing is a muscle. Take any question prompt and rewrite it three different ways using three different techniques from this list. In Reading, when you find an answer, pause and note <em>which</em> words the passage changed from the question — this trains your eye to spot the paraphrase under exam pressure. Practise the recognition side on our <a href="/reading/true-false-not-given">True/False/Not Given</a> and <a href="/reading/matching-headings">Matching Headings</a> practice, where nearly every correct answer hinges on decoding a reworded statement.</p>
  <p>For the productive side, write a full introduction that paraphrases the essay prompt, then run your answer through the free <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">AI IELTS essay checker</a> — it flags unnatural word choices and repetition so you can see whether your paraphrasing is genuinely lifting your Lexical Resource or quietly hurting it. Build the habit on real prompts on our <a href="/writingquestion">IELTS Writing Questions</a> page, and paraphrasing will stop being a task you think about and become something you simply do.</p>
