  <p>The <strong>two-part question</strong> — also called the <strong>direct question</strong> or <strong>double question</strong> essay — is one of the four most common Task 2 types, and the one candidates most often half-answer. The prompt asks you two separate questions, and if you answer only one, your <strong>Task Response</strong> mark is capped no matter how good your English is. This guide shows you how to recognise the type, split the prompt correctly, and build an essay that covers both parts in full.</p>

  <h2>How to recognise a two-part question</h2>
  <p>Unlike an opinion essay, a two-part prompt gives you a statement and then asks two direct questions — often, but not always, joined by "and". The questions are usually different in kind: one asks about causes or reasons, the other about solutions, effects, or your view. A typical prompt looks like this:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>"Many people find it difficult to balance work and personal life. <strong>Why is this the case? What can be done to solve the problem?</strong>"</li>
    <li>"More people are choosing to live alone than in the past. <strong>What are the reasons for this? Is it a positive or negative development?</strong>"</li>
    <li>"Children today spend less time reading books. <strong>Why has this happened, and what effect does it have?</strong>"</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The key habit is to <strong>underline every question word</strong> before you plan. Two question marks — or one question that clearly contains two demands — means two things to answer. This is not the same as a "discuss both views" essay, where you weigh one issue from two sides; here you answer two distinct questions.</p>

  <h2>The single most common mistake</h2>
  <p>The classic error is answering the first question well and forgetting the second, or turning the whole essay into an opinion piece when only one half asked for your view. Because <strong>Task Response is 25% of your Writing band</strong>, an essay that addresses only one of the two questions is capped at <strong>Band 5</strong> for that criterion — a ceiling no amount of vocabulary or grammar can lift. Before you write a word, note down a one-line answer to <em>each</em> question. If you cannot, you are not ready to start.</p>

  <h2>A structure that guarantees both parts are covered</h2>
  <p>The safest plan gives each question its own body paragraph. You have about 40 minutes and a 250-word minimum, and this four-paragraph shape covers both demands without running over time.</p>
  <h3>Introduction (2 sentences)</h3>
  <p>Paraphrase the statement, then signal that the essay will answer both questions. For example: "It is true that a growing number of adults now live by themselves. This essay will examine the main reasons behind the trend and argue that, on balance, it is a positive development."</p>
  <h3>Body 1 — answer the first question (about 90 words)</h3>
  <p>Develop one main reason or cause fully: state it, explain why it leads to the situation, and give a specific example. Depth beats breadth — one well-explained reason outscores three named in passing.</p>
  <h3>Body 2 — answer the second question (about 90 words)</h3>
  <p>Now answer the second question directly. If it asks whether the trend is positive or negative, commit to a clear view here and support it. If it asks for solutions or effects, develop one fully in the same way.</p>
  <h3>Conclusion (1 sentence)</h3>
  <p>Summarise your answer to <em>both</em> questions in a single sentence. Add nothing new.</p>

  <h2>Model paragraphs</h2>
  <p><strong>Question:</strong> More people are choosing to live alone than in the past. What are the reasons for this? Is it a positive or negative development?</p>
  <p><strong>Body 1 (the reason):</strong> "The main driver of this trend is rising financial independence, particularly among younger adults. As wages and career opportunities have grown in many cities, people can afford to rent or buy a home without sharing costs with family or a partner. A graduate in her twenties with a stable salary, for instance, may value the freedom of her own space far more than the savings of living with others, and so chooses to live alone as soon as she can."</p>
  <p><strong>Body 2 (the view, answering the second question):</strong> "In my opinion, this is largely a positive development. Living alone teaches practical self-reliance — managing bills, cooking, and household repairs — that people who never leave the family home often lack. It also allows individuals to focus on careers and personal goals without compromise. While loneliness is a genuine risk, it can be managed through friendships and community, and the independence gained outweighs that drawback for most adults."</p>
  <p>Notice that Body 2 does not merely list good and bad points — it takes a clear position ("largely a positive development") and defends it, which is exactly what the second question demands.</p>

  <h2>Language that keeps the two answers clear</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Introducing a reason:</strong> <em>the main reason for this is</em>, <em>this is largely driven by</em>, <em>a key cause of the trend is</em>.</li>
    <li><strong>Introducing an effect:</strong> <em>the most noticeable effect is</em>, <em>this has led to</em>, <em>one consequence of this is that</em>.</li>
    <li><strong>Stating your view:</strong> <em>in my opinion</em>, <em>I would argue that</em>, <em>on balance, this is a positive/negative development</em>.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Avoid memorised openers such as "In this modern era, there are many reasons and solutions." Under <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, memorised phrasing earns no credit, and topic-specific vocabulary always scores higher than decorative filler.</p>

  <h2>Common mistakes checklist</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Answering only the first question and forgetting the second.</li>
    <li>Giving an opinion when the question asked only for reasons, or vice versa.</li>
    <li>Merging both questions into one vague paragraph instead of one each.</li>
    <li>Treating a "positive or negative" question as "discuss both sides" and never committing to a view.</li>
    <li>Listing three reasons without developing any of them.</li>
    <li>Writing under 250 words, which loses marks automatically. Aim for 260 to 290.</li>
  </ul>
