  <p>The <strong>"to what extent do you agree or disagree"</strong> essay is the single most common task in IELTS Writing Task 2, and it is also where candidates most often lose easy marks. The problem is rarely weak English. It is a fuzzy position: examiners cannot reward an opinion they cannot find. This guide gives you a reliable way to decode the prompt, commit to a clear stance, and build an essay that scores Band 7 or above on Task Response.</p>

  <h2>Recognising the Opinion Essay</h2>
  <p>Agree or disagree prompts always ask for <em>your</em> view on a single statement. The wording varies but the signal is consistent:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>"To what extent do you agree or disagree?"</li>
    <li>"Do you agree or disagree?"</li>
    <li>"What is your opinion?"</li>
  </ul>
  <p>This is different from a <strong>discussion essay</strong> ("Discuss both views and give your opinion"), which asks you to cover two sides before stating your position. In an agree or disagree essay you are not obliged to argue both sides, you are asked to defend one clear view. Confusing the two is a common way to drift off task.</p>

  <h2>How Task 2 Is Marked</h2>
  <p>Your essay is scored on four equally weighted criteria, each worth 25%:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Task Response:</strong> Do you present a clear position throughout and develop it fully?</li>
    <li><strong>Coherence and Cohesion:</strong> Is the essay logically organised and clearly linked?</li>
    <li><strong>Lexical Resource:</strong> Do you use a range of accurate, appropriate vocabulary?</li>
    <li><strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy:</strong> Do you mix structures with few errors?</li>
  </ul>
  <p>On this task type, <strong>Task Response is where opinion essays are won or lost</strong>. A vague or shifting position caps that criterion no matter how polished your language is.</p>

  <h2>Step 1: Decide How Far You Agree</h2>
  <p>Before you write a word, choose one of three defensible positions and stick to it:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Full agreement:</strong> you agree completely. Both body paragraphs support the statement.</li>
    <li><strong>Full disagreement:</strong> you disagree completely. Both body paragraphs argue against it.</li>
    <li><strong>Partial agreement:</strong> you agree with conditions ("I agree, but only for..."). One paragraph explains what you accept, the other your reservation.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>All three can score Band 9. What matters is that your position is <strong>clear and consistent</strong> from the introduction to the conclusion. The one thing you must not do is sit on the fence, offering points for and against with no stated view of your own. That reads as an unanswered question.</p>

  <h2>Step 2: Use a Four-Paragraph Structure</h2>
  <p>Opinion essays fit neatly into the standard Task 2 shape:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Introduction:</strong> paraphrase the statement, then state your position directly. Make your opinion unmissable, for example: "I strongly agree that..." or "While this argument has some merit, I largely disagree because...".</li>
    <li><strong>Body paragraph 1:</strong> your strongest reason, with a topic sentence, an explanation, and a specific example.</li>
    <li><strong>Body paragraph 2:</strong> your second reason, developed the same way.</li>
    <li><strong>Conclusion:</strong> restate your position and summarise your two reasons. Introduce no new ideas.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Depth beats breadth. Two fully developed reasons score higher than four thin ones. This is the same skeleton described in our guide to the <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-band-7-structure">Writing Task 2 structure that scores Band 7 and above</a>, applied to the opinion prompt.</p>

  <h2>Step 3: Signal Your Opinion Throughout</h2>
  <p>Examiners look for your view in every part of the essay, not just the introduction. Weave in opinion language so your stance never fades:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Stating a view:</strong> "In my view...", "I am firmly of the opinion that...", "It seems to me that...".</li>
    <li><strong>Conceding then countering:</strong> "Although some argue that..., I believe...".</li>
    <li><strong>Emphasising:</strong> "This is precisely why...", "The most compelling reason is...".</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>A Worked Mini-Example</h2>
  <p>Prompt: <em>"Some people think that children should start school at a very early age. To what extent do you agree or disagree?"</em></p>
  <p>A partial-agreement thesis might read: <em>"I agree that early schooling benefits children socially, but I believe formal academic instruction should begin later."</em> That single sentence hands the examiner a clear, developable position, one paragraph on the social benefits you accept, one on why academics should wait, and the whole essay stays on task.</p>

  <h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Sitting on the fence.</strong> Listing pros and cons without choosing caps Task Response. Always commit.</li>
    <li><strong>Switching sides mid-essay.</strong> If you say you agree in the introduction, do not start arguing the opposite in body paragraph two.</li>
    <li><strong>Treating it as a discussion essay.</strong> You are not required to give equal weight to both sides here.</li>
    <li><strong>Writing under 250 words.</strong> Task 2 has a 250-word minimum, and falling short costs marks automatically. Aim for 260 to 290.</li>
    <li><strong>Memorised templates.</strong> Over-rehearsed openers add nothing and can lower your Lexical Resource if they do not fit the prompt.</li>
  </ul>
