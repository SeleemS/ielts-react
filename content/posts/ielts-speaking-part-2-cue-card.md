  <p>For many candidates, <strong>Part 2 of the IELTS Speaking test</strong>, often called the "long turn" or the cue card, is the most intimidating moment of the whole exam. You are handed a card with a topic, given one minute to prepare, and then expected to talk on your own for up to two minutes with no help from the examiner. The silence feels enormous. The good news is that Part 2 is the most predictable part of the Speaking test, and with a reliable method you can turn it from a source of panic into your strongest section.</p>

  <h2>What Actually Happens in Part 2</h2>
  <p>The full <strong>IELTS Speaking test lasts 11 to 14 minutes</strong> and has three parts. Part 2 sits in the middle. The examiner gives you a card with a topic and three or four bullet points to guide what you say, plus a pencil and paper for notes. You then get <strong>exactly one minute to prepare</strong> and should speak for <strong>one to two minutes</strong>. The examiner will stop you at two minutes and may ask one short rounding-off question. That is the entire task, but how you use that first minute decides almost everything.</p>

  <h2>How Part 2 Is Marked</h2>
  <p>Like the rest of Speaking, Part 2 is assessed on four equally weighted criteria:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Fluency and Coherence:</strong> Can you keep talking without long pauses, and do your ideas connect logically?</li>
    <li><strong>Lexical Resource:</strong> Do you use a range of vocabulary, including some less common words, accurately?</li>
    <li><strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy:</strong> Do you mix simple and complex structures with few errors?</li>
    <li><strong>Pronunciation:</strong> Are you easy to understand, with natural stress and intonation?</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Notice what is <em>not</em> on the list: there are no marks for having brilliant, original ideas, and none for telling the truth. You are being scored on how you speak, not what you say, which is liberating. A slightly invented story told fluently beats a true story delivered in nervous fragments.</p>

  <h2>Use Your One Minute of Prep Wisely</h2>
  <p>Do not try to write full sentences in 60 seconds, you will run out of time and then read robotically. Instead, jot down <strong>quick keywords</strong> next to each bullet point on the card. A useful shortcut is to answer <em>who, what, when, where, why,</em> and <em>how you felt</em>. Those prompts alone will give you more than two minutes of material for almost any topic.</p>
  <p>For a card like "Describe a place you enjoy visiting," your notes might simply read: <em>beach near grandparents / summers as a child / sunset walks / peaceful, escape city / smell of salt / want to return.</em> Six fragments, and you will never run dry.</p>

  <h2>A Simple Structure for the Long Turn</h2>
  <p>Follow the bullet points on the card in order, because they are designed to build a natural mini-story. A dependable shape is:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Introduce</strong> the topic in one sentence: "I'd like to talk about a place I really love visiting, which is a small beach near my grandparents' house."</li>
    <li><strong>Cover each bullet point</strong>, spending 20 to 30 seconds on each and adding one detail or example beyond the obvious answer.</li>
    <li><strong>Finish with a reflection</strong>: how you feel about it, or why it matters to you. This gives the examiner a clear ending and lets you use evaluative language.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>The final bullet on most cards ("and explain why...") is your chance to show off complex grammar with structures like <em>"The main reason I keep going back is that..."</em> or <em>"What I find so appealing about it is..."</em></p>

  <h2>How to Fill Two Full Minutes</h2>
  <p>The single biggest fear is drying up early. The cure is <strong>extension</strong>: never give a one-word answer, always add a reason, an example, or a contrast. Practise these expanding phrases until they are automatic:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Add a reason:</strong> "...and the reason for that is..."</li>
    <li><strong>Give an example:</strong> "For instance, I remember one time when..."</li>
    <li><strong>Add contrast or time:</strong> "These days it's changed a lot, whereas back then..."</li>
    <li><strong>Speculate:</strong> "I suppose that's probably because..."</li>
  </ul>
  <p>If you genuinely run out before two minutes, do not stop and apologise. Loop back to an earlier point and expand it, or compare it to something else. Fluency means keeping the flow, hesitation and self-correction cost you more than a slightly repetitive idea.</p>

  <h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Memorising scripts.</strong> Examiners are trained to spot rehearsed answers, and they will interrupt or change the question. Memorised speech also sounds flat and hurts your pronunciation score.</li>
    <li><strong>Reading your notes aloud.</strong> Notes are prompts, not a script. Glance at them and speak naturally.</li>
    <li><strong>Going off topic.</strong> You can be creative, but keep answering the actual card. Wandering into an unrelated story confuses the coherence marking.</li>
    <li><strong>Rushing to finish.</strong> Speaking too fast to "get it over with" causes slips and unclear pronunciation. Aim for a calm, steady pace.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Practise Out Loud and Time Yourself</h2>
  <p>Part 2 improves fastest when you rehearse the real conditions: pick a card, set a timer for one minute of notes, then record yourself speaking for two. Play it back and check where you paused, repeated yourself, or ran short.
