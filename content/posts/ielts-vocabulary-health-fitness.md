  <p>Health, fitness and lifestyle come up constantly in <strong>IELTS</strong> — in Speaking Part 1 ("Do you have a healthy lifestyle?"), in Part 3 discussions about healthcare, and in Writing Task 2 essays on government spending, junk food or exercise. Because the topic is so predictable, examiners have heard every memorised phrase a hundred times. The candidates who score well are not the ones with the longest word lists; they are the ones who use accurate, natural <strong>collocations</strong> in the right place. This guide gives you the health vocabulary that actually moves your band, and shows you how to use it.</p>

  <h2>Why This Vocabulary Matters</h2>
  <p><strong>Lexical Resource</strong> is one of the four equally weighted marking criteria in both Speaking and Writing, so it accounts for roughly a quarter of your score in each. The descriptors do not reward rare or "advanced" words for their own sake — they reward range, precision and <strong>natural collocation</strong>. A Band 5 candidate says "do exercise"; a Band 7 candidate says "take up regular exercise" or "lead an active lifestyle." Same idea, but the second sounds like a real English speaker rather than a translated word list.</p>

  <h2>Learn Collocations, Not Single Words</h2>
  <p>English pairs certain words together in ways that cannot be guessed from grammar rules. You <em>gain</em> weight but <em>put on</em> weight; you <em>follow</em> a diet, not "do" a diet. Memorising these chunks is what makes your English sound fluent under pressure. Group them by sub-topic so you can pull the right set when a question lands.</p>

  <h3>Diet and Nutrition</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>a balanced diet</strong> — eating a sensible mix of foods</li>
    <li><strong>cut down on / cut back on</strong> sugar, salt, processed food</li>
    <li><strong>fresh produce</strong> — fruit and vegetables</li>
    <li><strong>fizzy drinks</strong> and <strong>junk food</strong> — high in sugar and fat</li>
    <li><strong>nutritional value</strong> — how healthy a food is</li>
    <li><strong>overeat</strong> / <strong>skip meals</strong></li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Exercise and Fitness</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>lead an active lifestyle</strong> vs <strong>a sedentary lifestyle</strong> (sitting most of the day)</li>
    <li><strong>take up</strong> a sport; <strong>work out</strong> at the gym; <strong>stay in shape</strong> / <strong>keep fit</strong></li>
    <li><strong>build up stamina</strong> and <strong>muscle</strong></li>
    <li><strong>burn calories</strong>; get out of breath</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Mental Wellbeing</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>mental wellbeing</strong> / <strong>mental health</strong></li>
    <li><strong>cope with stress</strong>; <strong>feel under pressure</strong>; <strong>burn out</strong></li>
    <li><strong>unwind</strong> / <strong>recharge your batteries</strong> after work</li>
    <li>a good <strong>work-life balance</strong></li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Healthcare and Society (for Task 2)</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>preventive healthcare</strong> — stopping illness before it starts</li>
    <li><strong>public healthcare</strong> vs <strong>private healthcare</strong>; <strong>free at the point of use</strong></li>
    <li><strong>put a strain on the health system</strong>; <strong>raise awareness</strong> of a health issue</li>
    <li><strong>life expectancy</strong>; <strong>a sedentary population</strong>; <strong>obesity rates</strong></li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Model Sentences You Can Adapt</h2>
  <p>Vocabulary only earns marks when it sits inside a natural sentence. Notice how the collocations below do the work without any "big" words:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><em>Speaking Part 1:</em> "I try to keep fit by cycling to work, and I've cut back on fizzy drinks recently — I just feel more energetic."</li>
    <li><em>Speaking Part 3:</em> "I think governments should invest more in preventive healthcare, because encouraging people to lead active lifestyles is far cheaper than treating illness later on."</li>
    <li><em>Writing Task 2:</em> "As more people adopt a sedentary lifestyle and rely on processed food, obesity rates have risen, placing a growing strain on public health systems."</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Each of these would strengthen the <strong>Lexical Resource</strong> band without a single memorised idiom sounding forced.   <h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Forcing memorised idioms.</strong> Dropping in "an apple a day keeps the doctor away" to prove you know an idiom sounds unnatural and can lower your score. Use everyday collocations naturally instead.</li>
    <li><strong>Wrong word form.</strong> "Healthy" (adjective) and "health" (noun) are mixed up constantly — "a health lifestyle" is a common slip that costs accuracy marks.</li>
    <li><strong>Overusing one word.</strong> Repeating "healthy" ten times signals a narrow range. Vary it: "nutritious," "good for you," "beneficial," "in good shape."</li>
    <li><strong>Too informal in Writing.</strong> "Junk food" and "fizzy drinks" are fine, but avoid slang; keep an academic register in Task 2.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>How to Practise</h2>
  <p>Do not try to memorise all of these at once. Pick one sub-topic, write three or four of your own sentences using its collocations, and say them aloud until they feel automatic. Then recycle them across question types — a diet collocation used in Speaking Part 1 can reappear in a Task 2 essay. Build the same habit for other frequent themes, such as the language in our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-vocabulary-environment">IELTS vocabulary for the environment</a>, and rehearse your answers on the <a href="/speakingquestion">IELTS Speaking questions</a> bank. Precise, natural collocations used with confidence are exactly what separates a Band 6 from a Band 7 in Lexical Resource.</p>
