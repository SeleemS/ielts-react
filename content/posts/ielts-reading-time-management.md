  <p>The <strong>IELTS Reading test</strong> gives you 60 minutes to answer 40 questions across three passages, and there is no extra time to transfer your answers. Candidates rarely fail because the texts are too hard; they fail because they run out of time on passage three. Managing the clock is a skill you can train, and it is often worth a full band on its own.</p>

  <h2>The Format at a Glance</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Academic Reading:</strong> three long passages taken from books, journals, and newspapers, increasing in difficulty.</li>
    <li><strong>General Training Reading:</strong> shorter texts in section one and two (notices, advertisements, workplace documents) building to one longer passage in section three.</li>
    <li>Both versions have <strong>40 questions</strong> and both are marked out of 40 raw points that convert to a band score.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Your 60-Minute Plan</h2>
  <p>A simple, effective split is <strong>20 minutes per passage</strong>. Because the passages get harder, some students prefer 17, 20, and 23 minutes. The key rule is the same either way: <strong>when your time for a passage is up, move on.</strong> A question you cannot answer is worth exactly the same as one you spend five minutes agonising over, one mark, so never let a single question sink your pacing.</p>

  <h2>Skim First, Then Scan</h2>
  <p>Do not read every word before looking at the questions. Instead:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Skim the passage</strong> for one to two minutes to grasp the topic, the structure, and roughly what each paragraph is about.</li>
    <li><strong>Read the questions</strong> and underline keywords (names, dates, technical terms).</li>
    <li><strong>Scan the text</strong> for those keywords or their synonyms to locate the answer quickly.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Most questions follow the order of the passage, so once you find the answer to question three, the answer to question four usually appears further down, not above.</p>

  <h2>Know the Question Types</h2>
  <p>Different question types reward different tactics:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>True / False / Not Given</strong> and <strong>Yes / No / Not Given</strong>: match the statement precisely against the text. "Not Given" means the information simply is not there, do not use outside knowledge.</li>
    <li><strong>Matching headings</strong>: read the first and last sentence of each paragraph, where the main idea usually sits.</li>
    <li><strong>Sentence completion and short answer</strong>: obey the word limit exactly (for example "no more than two words"), or the answer is marked wrong.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Common Time Traps</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Re-reading the whole passage</strong> for every question instead of scanning for the relevant section.</li>
    <li><strong>Perfectionism</strong> on hard questions when easy marks remain elsewhere.</li>
    <li><strong>Leaving blanks</strong> at the end. There is no penalty for wrong answers, so guess every remaining question in the final minute.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Build Speed Through Practice</h2>
  <p>Reading speed improves the same way running speed does: through regular, timed effort. Practise full passages with a visible timer and review not just which answers you missed but <em>why</em> you were slow. Work through authentic material on our <a href="/readingquestion">IELTS Reading Questions</a> page, where a built-in timer mirrors real exam conditions.</p>

  <p>Once pacing feels natural, apply the same discipline to the audio sections, our guide to the <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-common-traps">most common IELTS Listening traps</a> shows how timing and attention interact under pressure. Finish every passage, guess intelligently, and you will convert the reading skill you already have into the band score you deserve.</p>
