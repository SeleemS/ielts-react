  <p>The bar chart is the single most common visual in Academic <strong>Writing Task 1</strong>, and that is exactly why it is worth mastering. A bar chart can compare categories at one moment, track them across several years, or show two variables side by side &mdash; and the language you need shifts with each. Candidates lose marks not because bar charts are hard to read, but because they list every bar mechanically instead of grouping and comparing. This guide gives you a structure that works for any bar chart, the comparison language examiners reward, and a full model answer.</p>

  <h2>What the examiner is marking</h2>
  <p>Task 1 is scored on four equally weighted criteria: <strong>Task Achievement</strong>, <strong>Coherence and Cohesion</strong>, <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, and <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong>. You have 20 minutes and must write at least 150 words. For a bar chart, Task Achievement means you have selected the key features, supported them with accurate figures, and included a clear <strong>overview</strong>. A report with no overview is capped at Band 5 for Task Achievement, however polished the rest of the writing is.</p>

  <h2>First, identify which kind of bar chart you have</h2>
  <p>Before you write a word, decide what the chart is really comparing. This choice determines your grammar and your grouping.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Static bar chart (no time axis):</strong> it compares categories at one point in time &mdash; for example, average earnings by profession. Use <strong>present or past tense</strong> and lean on comparison language: <em>higher than, twice as much as, the highest.</em></li>
    <li><strong>Bar chart over time:</strong> the horizontal axis shows years or periods, so it behaves like a line graph. Use <strong>trend language</strong> &mdash; <em>rose, fell, remained stable</em> &mdash; alongside comparisons.</li>
    <li><strong>Grouped bar chart:</strong> several bars per category (for example, men and women in each age group). Compare <em>within</em> each group first, then across groups.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The four-paragraph structure</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Introduction (1 sentence):</strong> paraphrase the question. Change the wording but keep the meaning &mdash; swap "shows" for "illustrates", "amount" for "figure", and so on.</li>
    <li><strong>Overview (2 sentences):</strong> state the most striking features with no specific numbers. For a static chart, name the highest and lowest bars; for a chart over time, state the overall direction and the biggest change.</li>
    <li><strong>Body paragraph 1:</strong> describe the largest or most important bars in detail, with figures.</li>
    <li><strong>Body paragraph 2:</strong> describe the remaining bars, again with figures and comparisons.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>The golden rule for grouping: never read the chart left to right like a list. Group bars that behave similarly &mdash; the tall ones together, the short ones together, or the risers versus the fallers &mdash; so your report shows selection and judgement rather than mechanical description.</p>

  <h2>Comparison language that scores Band 7</h2>
  <p>Strong Lexical Resource on a bar chart comes from varying <em>how</em> you compare, not from rare words. Rotate through these structures:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Comparatives:</strong> "Spending on transport was <em>considerably higher than</em> spending on leisure."</li>
    <li><strong>Multiples:</strong> "The figure for the UK was <em>roughly three times</em> that of Japan."</li>
    <li><strong>Superlatives:</strong> "Germany recorded <em>the highest</em> figure, at just over 60 per cent."</li>
    <li><strong>Approximation:</strong> soften figures with <em>just over, just under, nearly, approximately, around</em> so you are not repeating "exactly" numbers.</li>
    <li><strong>Contrast linkers:</strong> "<em>By contrast</em>, France reported the lowest total", "<em>whereas</em> the youngest group barely changed."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Model answer</h2>
  <p>Imagine a static bar chart showing the average weekly hours spent on housework by four age groups: 18&ndash;29 (6 hours), 30&ndash;44 (11 hours), 45&ndash;59 (14 hours), and 60+ (18 hours). A Band 7 report might read:</p>
  <p><em>"The bar chart compares the average number of hours per week that four age groups devote to housework. Overall, time spent on housework rose steadily with age, with the oldest group doing by far the most and the youngest group the least. Adults aged 18 to 29 spent the fewest hours on domestic chores, at just six per week. This figure climbed to 11 hours among those aged 30 to 44, roughly double the youngest group's total. The upward pattern continued in the older brackets: people aged 45 to 59 averaged 14 hours, while those aged 60 and over recorded the highest figure of all, at 18 hours &mdash; three times the amount reported by the youngest respondents."</em></p>
  <p>Notice how the report groups the trend, names the extremes in the overview, and varies its comparisons between multiples ("double", "three times") and plain figures. That variety is what the descriptors reward.</p>

  <h2>Common mistakes to avoid</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Describing every bar in turn.</strong> Select and group; you do not need to mention all data, only the key features and enough figures to support them.</li>
    <li><strong>Using trend verbs on a static chart.</strong> If there is no time axis, nothing "rises" or "falls" &mdash; compare instead.</li>
    <li><strong>Forgetting the overview.</strong> Begin it with <em>Overall</em> and state the big picture without figures. This is where the easiest marks are won.</li>
    <li><strong>Adding opinions or reasons.</strong> Task 1 is a factual report; never explain <em>why</em> a figure is high or say whether it is good.</li>
    <li><strong>Misreading the axis units.</strong> Check whether the scale is percentages, thousands, or millions, and label your figures accordingly.</li>
  </ul>
