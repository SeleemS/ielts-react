  <p>If there is one paragraph that decides whether your Academic <strong>Writing Task 1</strong> answer reaches Band 6 or Band 7, it is the overview. The band descriptors for <strong>Task Achievement</strong> — one of the four equally weighted criteria alongside Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy — explicitly reward a response that "clearly presents an overview of main trends, differences or stages." Examiners are trained to look for it, and a report without one is capped at Band 5 for Task Achievement no matter how good your sentences are. This guide shows you exactly what an overview is, where it goes, and how to write one in two sentences.</p>

  <h2>What an overview actually is</h2>
  <p>An overview is a short paragraph — usually two sentences — that describes the <strong>biggest, most general features</strong> of the chart, map, table or diagram <em>without any specific numbers</em>. It is the answer to a simple question: if you had to describe this visual to someone over the phone in ten seconds, what would you say? It is <strong>not</strong> a summary of everything, and it is <strong>not</strong> the place for data. Its whole job is to zoom out and state the headline.</p>
  <p>Remember that Task 1 asks you to <strong>report</strong> information, not to explain or give opinions. You have a minimum of <strong>150 words</strong> and a recommended <strong>20 minutes</strong>. The overview is where the examiner confirms that you have understood the data as a whole rather than just listing figures.</p>

  <h2>Where the overview goes</h2>
  <p>A clean four-paragraph structure works for almost every Task 1 prompt:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Introduction</strong> — one sentence paraphrasing the question (what the visual shows, and its units and time frame).</li>
    <li><strong>Overview</strong> — two sentences of main features, no data.</li>
    <li><strong>Body paragraph 1</strong> — the first group of specific details with numbers.</li>
    <li><strong>Body paragraph 2</strong> — the second group of specific details with numbers.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Placing the overview immediately after the introduction — high up, where it is impossible to miss — is the safest choice. Some high-scoring writers put it at the end, and that is acceptable, but second position guarantees the examiner sees it early.</p>

  <h2>How to find the main features</h2>
  <p>What you highlight depends on the type of visual. Ask the right question for each:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Line graph or bar chart over time:</strong> What is the overall trend — up, down, stable, or fluctuating? Which category is highest and which is lowest overall?</li>
    <li><strong>Chart at a single point in time (no dates):</strong> Which is the largest and which is the smallest? Is there a big gap or are the values similar?</li>
    <li><strong>Two charts together:</strong> What is the one clearest feature in each? Devote one overview sentence to each visual.</li>
    <li><strong>Map:</strong> What is the single biggest change — did the area become more built-up, more industrial, more residential?</li>
    <li><strong>Process diagram:</strong> How many stages are there, and is the process natural or man-made, cyclical or linear?</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The rule of thumb: pick the two or three features that would survive if all the small detail were deleted.</p>

  <h2>Signal the overview clearly</h2>
  <p>Start the paragraph with a phrase that tells the examiner "this is my overview." Useful openers include:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Overall,</strong> …</li>
    <li><strong>In general,</strong> …</li>
    <li><strong>It is clear that</strong> …</li>
    <li><strong>Broadly speaking,</strong> …</li>
  </ul>
  <p>"Overall," is the most reliable — one word, unmistakable, and it costs you nothing.</p>

  <h2>Model overviews</h2>
  <p>Here is how the theory turns into sentences. For a <strong>line graph</strong> showing car, bus and train use rising and falling from 2000 to 2020:</p>
  <p><em>"Overall, car use rose steadily across the whole period and remained the most popular form of transport throughout, whereas bus travel showed the opposite pattern and fell to the lowest level by the end."</em></p>
  <p>For a <strong>pie chart pair</strong> comparing household spending in two countries:</p>
  <p><em>"In general, housing accounted for the largest share of spending in both countries, while the smallest proportion in each case went to leisure. The most striking difference was the far higher spending on food in the first country."</em></p>
  <p>Notice what these have in common: they name the biggest features, use comparison language (<strong>whereas</strong>, <strong>while</strong>, <strong>the most striking difference</strong>), and contain <em>no exact figures</em>. The numbers belong in your body paragraphs.</p>

  <h2>Common overview mistakes</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Including data.</strong> Writing "car use rose to 45%" in the overview drags a body-paragraph detail up too early. Keep numbers out.</li>
    <li><strong>Describing every category.</strong> The overview is the headline, not the whole story. Two or three main features are enough.</li>
    <li><strong>Skipping it entirely.</strong> Jumping straight from the introduction into figures is the most expensive error in Task 1 — it caps Task Achievement at Band 5.</li>
    <li><strong>Adding opinions or reasons.</strong> Never explain <em>why</em> a trend happened; you cannot know, and Task 1 does not ask.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Practise it in isolation</h2>
  <p>Because the overview is a self-contained skill, you can drill it fast. Take any chart, set a two-minute timer, and write only the overview — one signposting sentence naming the main features, one comparison sentence. Do ten of those and the habit becomes automatic.   <p>The overview is not a formality — it is the paragraph that proves you can see the data as a whole. Two sentences, a clear signpost, the biggest features, no numbers. Master those, and you have secured the mark most candidates leave on the table.</p>
