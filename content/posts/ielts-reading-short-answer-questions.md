  <p>Short-answer questions are one of the most bankable question types in IELTS Reading. You are asked a direct question, the answer is copied word-for-word from the passage, and the questions follow the order of the text. That combination should make them easy marks — yet candidates lose them constantly by breaking the word limit, answering with their own words, or writing a full sentence when a single noun was wanted. This guide gives you a reliable method, a worked example, and the specific traps to train against so the type becomes a strength rather than a leak.</p>

  <h2>What the question type asks</h2>
  <p>You are given a set of direct questions about factual detail in the passage, together with an instruction such as "Answer the questions below. Choose <strong>NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER</strong> from the passage for each answer." Three rules define the type:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Answers come straight from the passage.</strong> You copy the words exactly as they appear — you do not paraphrase, change the grammar, or supply your own synonym.</li>
    <li><strong>The word limit is absolute.</strong> "No more than three words" means a four-word answer scores zero even if the correct words are in there. A hyphenated word such as "self-employed" counts as one word, and a number written as a figure counts within the "and/or a number" allowance.</li>
    <li><strong>The questions follow passage order.</strong> The answer to question 5 sits after the answer to question 4 in the text. This makes location dramatically easier — never waste it.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The five-step method</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Read the instruction and lock the word limit in your head.</strong> Say it to yourself — "three words max." This is the cheapest mark-protection in the whole test, and it changes how you copy every answer.</li>
    <li><strong>Turn the question word into a target.</strong> A question beginning "Who" wants a person or organisation; "Where" wants a place; "How many" or "How much" wants a number or quantity; "What" usually wants a noun. Knowing the shape of the answer before you read stops you copying the wrong phrase.</li>
    <li><strong>Choose stable locator keywords.</strong> Pick the words in the question least likely to be paraphrased — names, dates, technical terms — and scan for them or their synonyms. Expect the everyday words to be reworded; that is the test's design.</li>
    <li><strong>Read the whole sentence you land on, not just the phrase.</strong> The answer usually sits within a line of your locator, but the exact word you need is often decided by the rest of the sentence.</li>
    <li><strong>Copy the answer exactly, then re-read it against the question.</strong> It must answer the question asked and stay inside the limit. If it does neither, you have the wrong phrase or too many words.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>A worked example</h2>
  <p><strong>Passage extract:</strong> "The first commercial refrigerators reached British households in the 1920s, but they remained a luxury; it was not until manufacturers introduced hire-purchase schemes after 1945 that ownership spread to ordinary families."</p>
  <p><strong>Question:</strong> What allowed ordinary families to afford refrigerators? <em>(NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS)</em></p>
  <p>The question word is "What," so a noun phrase is coming, and the phrase "allowed ... to afford" signals a cause. Locators: "ordinary families," which the passage repeats almost verbatim. The cause the text gives is "hire-purchase schemes." Answer: <strong>hire-purchase schemes</strong> — three words, copied exactly. Notice the traps sitting nearby: "commercial refrigerators" and "the 1920s" both appear in the extract and both fit grammatically, but neither answers <em>why</em> families could afford them. The question word and the cause-and-effect phrasing rule them out.</p>

  <h2>The traps, named</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>The word-limit trap.</strong> The passage says "government-backed hire-purchase schemes" and the limit is three words. Candidates who copy every modifier break the limit and score zero. Strip the answer to the words that actually answer the question.</li>
    <li><strong>The full-sentence trap.</strong> The instinct is to answer a question in a whole sentence. Here that guarantees a zero — copy only the words that answer it, nothing more.</li>
    <li><strong>The paraphrase-blindness trap.</strong> Scanning for the question's exact words fails when the passage rewords them. If your locator is "afford," be ready to find "cost," "price," or "within reach."</li>
    <li><strong>The order violation.</strong> If your answer to question 6 sits <em>before</em> your answer to question 5 in the passage, one of them is wrong. The order rule is a free error-detector — use it to catch slips.</li>
    <li><strong>The spelling giveaway.</strong> You are copying from a visible text, so a misspelled answer is an unforced zero. Copy unfamiliar words letter by letter.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>How this differs from its neighbours</h2>
  <p>Short-answer questions belong to the copy-from-the-text family, which is why the discipline overlaps so closely with <a href="/blog/ielts-reading-sentence-completion">sentence completion</a>: both take words verbatim from the passage in order, and both live or die on the word limit. The difference is framing — sentence completion gives you an incomplete statement to finish, whereas short-answer gives you a genuine question to answer, which makes the question word your single most useful clue. Do not confuse either with <a href="/blog/ielts-reading-multiple-choice">multiple choice</a>, where you select a provided option rather than copy your own words, or with <a href="/blog/ielts-reading-true-false-not-given">True/False/Not Given</a>, which tests whether a claim is supported rather than asking for a fact. Knowing which rules apply to which type is worth marks on its own.</p>
