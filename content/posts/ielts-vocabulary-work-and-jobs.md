  <p>Work is one of the topics you are almost guaranteed to meet in <strong>IELTS</strong>. It turns up in Speaking Part 1 (your job or studies), in Part 2 cue cards about a person you admire or a place you work, in Part 3 discussions about careers, automation and retirement, and in Writing Task 2 questions on job satisfaction, work-life balance and whether people should stay in one job for life. The mistake most candidates make is preparing with stiff, formal vocabulary — "remuneration," "occupational endeavours," "gainful employment" — that no fluent speaker actually uses. What the <strong>Lexical Resource</strong> criterion rewards is natural collocation: everyday words in the pairings real English uses. This guide gives you those pairings, grouped by sub-topic, with notes on using them accurately.</p>

  <h2>The principle: natural pairings over big words</h2>
  <p>Examiners are trained to notice when vocabulary is memorised rather than owned. A candidate who says "I am desirous of procuring lucrative employment" scores lower than one who says "I'd love to find a job that pays well and that I actually enjoy." The second is more precise, more fluent and more natural. Aim for <strong>four or five strong collocations</strong> in a Speaking answer or Writing paragraph, placed where the meaning needs them — not a shopping list dropped in for show.</p>

  <h2>Finding and starting a job</h2>
  <p>This cluster covers the beginning of a working life — a frequent Part 1 and Task 2 area:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>apply for a job</strong> / <strong>send off an application</strong> — "I applied for three jobs last month."</li>
    <li><strong>go for an interview</strong> / <strong>land a job</strong> — "I was nervous going for the interview, but I landed the job."</li>
    <li><strong>gain hands-on experience</strong> / <strong>do an internship</strong> — useful for questions about graduates and the job market.</li>
    <li><strong>start from the bottom</strong> / <strong>work your way up</strong> — "She started from the bottom and worked her way up to manager."</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Note that <em>a job</em> is countable but <em>work</em> is uncountable: you look for "a job" or for "work," never "a work."</p>

  <h2>Describing the work itself</h2>
  <p>These pairings let you describe what a job is actually like, which is what Part 1 and cue cards ask for:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>work long hours</strong> / <strong>do overtime</strong> / <strong>work shifts</strong></li>
    <li><strong>meet a deadline</strong> / <strong>be under pressure</strong> / <strong>have a heavy workload</strong></li>
    <li><strong>a nine-to-five job</strong> / <strong>a demanding role</strong> / <strong>a rewarding career</strong></li>
    <li><strong>work from home</strong> / <strong>work remotely</strong> / <strong>commute to the office</strong></li>
  </ul>
  <p>The distinction between a <strong>job</strong> (the specific position), a <strong>career</strong> (the long path across many jobs) and a <strong>profession</strong> (a field requiring qualifications, like law or medicine) matters: using the right one signals precision.</p>

  <h2>Progress, pay and problems</h2>
  <p>Task 2 essays on job satisfaction and Part 3 discussions reward this vocabulary of progression and reward:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>get a promotion</strong> / <strong>be promoted</strong> / <strong>get a pay rise</strong> (in American English, a "raise")</li>
    <li><strong>earn a decent salary</strong> / <strong>be well paid</strong> / <strong>make ends meet</strong> (for lower pay)</li>
    <li><strong>job security</strong> / <strong>a permanent contract</strong> versus <strong>temporary</strong> or <strong>freelance work</strong></li>
    <li><strong>be made redundant</strong> / <strong>get laid off</strong> / <strong>hand in your notice</strong> (to resign) / <strong>be fired</strong> (dismissed for a reason)</li>
    <li><strong>achieve a work-life balance</strong> / <strong>suffer from burnout</strong> / <strong>feel stressed out</strong></li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Two or three idioms — used, not stacked</h2>
  <p>One well-placed idiom can lift a band. Overuse them and the answer sounds rehearsed. Reliable ones for work topics include being a <strong>workaholic</strong>, having a job that is <strong>not my cup of tea</strong>, someone who <strong>climbs the career ladder</strong>, and <strong>learning the ropes</strong> in a new role. Use <em>at most one</em> per answer, and only where it fits the point you are making.</p>

  <h2>Putting it into a Part 1 answer</h2>
  <p>Compare a flat answer with a collocation-rich one. Flat: "I have a job. It is good but hard." Stronger: "I work as a project coordinator, so my days can be pretty demanding — I'm often juggling several deadlines at once. It's a rewarding role, though, and I've been lucky enough to work my way up over the past few years." The second uses four natural pairings without straining, and that is exactly the target.   <h2>Using work vocabulary in Writing Task 2</h2>
  <p>In an essay, collocations must serve the argument, not decorate it. If you claim automation threatens jobs, the natural language is "workers being <strong>made redundant</strong>," "the need to <strong>retrain</strong> or <strong>reskill</strong>," and "industries that <strong>create jobs</strong>." Precise, purposeful vocabulary is what moves a Lexical Resource score from 6 to 7.</p>
