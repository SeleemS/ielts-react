  <p>Of the four Writing criteria, <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong> is the one most candidates lose marks on without ever knowing why. It is worth 25% of your Writing band, and the descriptors are blunt: to reach Band 7 you need "frequent error-free sentences" and only occasional slips. Most Band 6 essays are not badly written — they are competent essays undermined by the same small set of repeated errors. This guide lists the mistakes examiners see most often, why each one costs you, and exactly how to fix it.</p>

  <h2>Why grammar caps so many essays at Band 6</h2>
  <p>The Band 6 descriptor allows a "mix of simple and complex sentence forms" but expects errors that "rarely reduce communication." Band 7 demands the opposite balance: mostly accurate sentences with errors that are the exception, not the rule. The difference is rarely one dramatic mistake. It is a comma splice here, a missing article there, a wrong preposition in the next paragraph — small, frequent, and entirely fixable. Clean up the recurring ones and your accuracy climbs a whole band without you writing a single harder sentence.</p>

  <h2>1. Articles (a, an, the) — or missing them entirely</h2>
  <p>Articles are the single most common error for candidates whose first language does not use them. Two patterns dominate:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Dropping the article before a singular countable noun.</strong> "Government should build more houses" needs <em>The government</em>. A singular countable noun almost always needs an article or another determiner.</li>
    <li><strong>Adding "the" before a general plural or uncountable noun.</strong> "The pollution is a serious problem" and "The children learn faster" are wrong when you mean people or things in general. Say <em>Pollution is a serious problem</em> and <em>Children learn faster</em>.</li>
  </ul>
  <p><strong>Fix:</strong> when you write a noun, ask two questions — is it countable, and do I mean a specific one or things in general? Specific singular takes <em>the</em>; general plurals and uncountables usually take no article at all.</p>

  <h2>2. Subject-verb agreement</h2>
  <p>The verb must match the subject in number, and long sentences make this easy to lose. "The number of students <em>have</em> increased" is wrong — the subject is <em>number</em>, which is singular, so it is <em>has increased</em>. Watch especially for:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Words that come between the subject and verb: "A range of measures <em>is</em> needed," not <em>are</em>.</li>
    <li>Uncountable nouns that look plural: <em>information</em>, <em>advice</em>, <em>research</em>, <em>knowledge</em> all take a singular verb.</li>
    <li>"Each," "every," and "one of" — always singular: "Each of these factors <em>affects</em> the result."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>3. Run-on sentences and comma splices</h2>
  <p>This is the error that most often separates Band 6 from Band 7, because it appears exactly when a candidate reaches for a "complex" sentence. Joining two full clauses with only a comma is a comma splice: "Cars cause pollution, they should be taxed." Two independent clauses need one of three things: a full stop, a semicolon, or a coordinating conjunction (<em>and, but, so</em>). Correct: "Cars cause pollution, <strong>so</strong> they should be taxed."</p>
  <p><strong>Fix:</strong> if you can put a full stop where your comma is and both halves stand alone as sentences, you have a splice. Add a linking word or split the sentence.</p>

  <h2>4. Prepositions</h2>
  <p>Prepositions rarely stop the examiner understanding you, but a scatter of wrong ones drags down accuracy. The common offenders in Task 2 and Task 1:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><em>discuss</em> takes no preposition — "discuss <s>about</s> the issue" is wrong.</li>
    <li>An increase or a rise is <em>in</em> something and <em>of</em> an amount: "a rise <strong>in</strong> unemployment," "a rise <strong>of</strong> 10%."</li>
    <li><em>affect</em> takes no <em>to</em>; you <em>have an effect on</em> something.</li>
    <li>You are responsible <em>for</em> something, dependent <em>on</em> it, and it has an impact <em>on</em> people.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>5. Verb tense and time markers</h2>
  <p>In Task 2 the default is the present simple for general truths. Problems appear when candidates drift between tenses in one sentence, or use the wrong tense for the data in <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-academic-describing-charts">Academic Task 1</a>. A chart showing 1990 to 2010 is finished time, so it needs the past simple: "The figure <em>rose</em> sharply," not <em>rises</em>. A projection to 2035 needs a future form: "numbers <em>are expected to</em> rise." Match your tense to the time shown, and keep it consistent within each sentence.</p>

  <h2>6. Countable and uncountable nouns</h2>
  <p>Several very common IELTS words are uncountable and take no plural and no <em>a/an</em>: <em>information, advice, research, equipment, knowledge, traffic, money, work</em>. "Many informations" and "an advice" are both wrong. Say <em>a great deal of information</em> or <em>a piece of advice</em>. This overlaps with articles and subject-verb agreement, which is why fixing it lifts several errors at once.</p>

  <h2>7. Word form confusion</h2>
  <p>Using the noun where you need the adjective, or the adjective where you need the verb, is scored under grammar and vocabulary together. Common mix-ups: <em>economic</em> (relating to the economy) versus <em>economical</em> (cheap to run); <em>advise</em> (verb) versus <em>advice</em> (noun); <em>affect</em> (verb) versus <em>effect</em> (noun). "This will <em>benefit</em> to society" should be "This will <strong>benefit</strong> society" — <em>benefit</em> is already the verb.</p>

  <h2>The three-minute proofreading routine that saves your band</h2>
  <p>Most of these errors are ones you can already correct when you slow down — you simply do not see them while composing. So reserve the last three minutes of each task to hunt for them deliberately, in this order:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Articles and plurals.</strong> Point at every noun and check: does it need <em>a</em>, <em>the</em>, or nothing? Should it be singular or plural?</li>
    <li><strong>Verbs.</strong> Does each verb agree with its subject, and is the tense right for the time you mean?</li>
    <li><strong>Sentence boundaries.</strong> Look at every comma between two full clauses and fix any splices.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Checking one category at a time catches far more than reading through once for "anything wrong."</p>
