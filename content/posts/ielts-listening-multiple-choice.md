---
title: "IELTS Listening Multiple Choice: How to Beat the Distractors"
date: July 26, 2026
excerpt: Multiple choice is where careful listeners lose marks to deliberate distractors. Here is how to read the options in time, track the whole conversation, and pick the answer the speaker actually confirms.
---

  <p>Multiple choice sounds like the friendliest question type in IELTS Listening — the answer is right there on the page, so how hard can it be? Harder than it looks. This is the type most engineered around <strong>distractors</strong>: every wrong option is deliberately made to sound correct at some point in the recording. The audio plays once, and by the time you realise a speaker has changed their mind, the next question has already started. This guide gives you a method built specifically for that pressure.</p>

  <h2>Where multiple choice appears</h2>
  <p>IELTS Listening has four sections, about 30 minutes of audio, and 40 questions in total, ten per section. Multiple choice turns up most often in <strong>Section 3</strong> — a conversation among up to four people in an educational setting, such as two students discussing an assignment with a tutor — and sometimes in Section 2 or Section 4. Two formats exist, and you must read the instruction to know which you have:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Single answer:</strong> a question stem followed by three options, A, B and C. You choose one.</li>
    <li><strong>Multiple answer:</strong> a longer list, often A to E or A to G, and you are told to choose <em>two</em> or <em>three</em>. Each correct choice is a separate mark, and the two answers may be mentioned several sentences apart.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>As with every Listening type, the answers come <strong>in the order they appear in the recording</strong>, so question 22 is answered after question 21 and before question 23. That ordering is your safety net when you lose your place.</p>

  <h2>The real problem: reading speed, not listening skill</h2>
  <p>Most candidates who miss multiple choice do not mishear the audio — they run out of time reading the options. A single-answer question can carry three full sentences of text, and a set of five such questions is fifteen sentences to absorb while the recording moves. If you are still reading option C when the speaker answers the next question, you have lost two marks at once.</p>
  <p>The fix is to use the preparation time before each section deliberately. In those seconds, do not read every word. Instead:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Read the question stem first and underline its keyword.</strong> The stem tells you what to listen <em>for</em> — a reason, a decision, a change of plan, an opinion.</li>
    <li><strong>Reduce each option to two or three words.</strong> You do not need the full sentence in your head, only enough to recognise it when the meaning arrives in different words.</li>
    <li><strong>Notice how the options differ from each other.</strong> Often A, B and C are the same idea with one detail changed — a date, a person, a number. That changed detail is exactly what the recording will test.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>How the distractors are built</h2>
  <p>Understanding the trap is half of avoiding it. IELTS wrong options are not random; they are constructed from things the speaker genuinely says. Three patterns dominate:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>The mentioned-but-rejected option.</strong> A speaker raises an idea and then dismisses it: "We thought about surveying staff, but in the end we decided interviews would give richer data." A candidate who hears "surveying staff" and stops listening picks the wrong answer. The word <em>but</em> is doing all the work.</li>
    <li><strong>The changed mind.</strong> "I was going to focus on the 1990s, though actually the tutor suggested I widen it to the whole decade before." The answer is what they settle on, not what they started with. Listen for <em>actually</em>, <em>in the end</em>, <em>on second thoughts</em>, <em>instead</em>.</li>
    <li><strong>The word-for-word trap.</strong> One option repeats a word straight from the audio, but the surrounding meaning does not match. The correct option is usually <em>paraphrased</em>. If the question asks why a student is behind and the recording says "I underestimated how long the reading would take," the answer is the option about "misjudging the workload," not the one that happens to contain the word "reading."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>A worked example</h2>
  <p><strong>Question:</strong> Why did the students change their research topic?</p>
  <ul>
    <li>A. It was too similar to another group's.</li>
    <li>B. They could not find enough sources.</li>
    <li>C. Their tutor advised against it.</li>
  </ul>
  <p><strong>Audio:</strong> "We started on urban air quality, but honestly there just wasn't much recent data we could actually get hold of, so we switched to noise pollution instead."</p>
  <p>The recording never says "sources," and it never mentions the tutor or another group. It says "wasn't much recent data we could get hold of" — a paraphrase of <strong>B</strong>. Options A and C are plausible reasons a real project might change, which is exactly why they are there; but the speaker confirms only one. Answer only from what is said, never from what sounds reasonable.</p>

  <h2>Handling the "choose two" format</h2>
  <p>Multiple-answer questions reward patience. The two correct items are rarely side by side — a speaker may name one advantage early and another a full exchange later. Keep all the options live in your mind and lightly tick any the speaker endorses, rather than committing to the first two you hear. And read the instruction exactly: if it says choose two, three ticks scores zero for that question, the same way an over-length answer is marked wrong in gap-fill tasks.</p>

  <h2>Mistakes that quietly cost marks</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Choosing an option because you heard its words.</strong> Matching vocabulary is the trap, not the answer. Match the whole meaning.</li>
    <li><strong>Committing before the speaker finishes.</strong> The sentence after the one you liked often reverses it. Wait for the full thought.</li>
    <li><strong>Freezing after a miss.</strong> If you lose one, let it go and listen for the next question's keyword — because answers run in order, the following stem tells you where the speakers now are.</li>
    <li><strong>Not reading the number to select.</strong> "Choose TWO" and "Choose THREE" are scored strictly; check it before the audio begins.</li>
    <li><strong>Leaving blanks.</strong> There is no penalty for a wrong answer, and in the paper test you get ten minutes at the end to transfer answers (only two minutes to check on the computer), so make sure every question has a letter before the recording ends.</li>
  </ul>
