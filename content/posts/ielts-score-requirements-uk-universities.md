  <p>The United Kingdom remains one of the most popular IELTS destinations, and the first practical question for any applicant is the score. UK requirements are less uniform than you might expect: the same degree subject can require 6.0 at one university and 7.0 at another, and the wording of the requirement — overall score versus per-skill minimums — matters as much as the headline number. This guide lays out the typical ranges, explains the UKVI question that confuses almost everyone, and shows how to plan preparation backwards from your offer.</p>

  <h2>Typical requirements by level and institution</h2>
  <p>Requirements are set by each university, and often by each course. The broad patterns:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Foundation and pre-sessional routes:</strong> commonly 4.5–5.5 overall, since these programs exist partly to build English alongside academic skills.</li>
    <li><strong>Most undergraduate degrees:</strong> 6.0–6.5 overall, typically with per-skill minimums of 5.5 or 6.0.</li>
    <li><strong>Most postgraduate taught degrees:</strong> 6.5 overall with 6.0 minimums is the most common pattern.</li>
    <li><strong>Highly selective universities and courses:</strong> 7.0 overall — sometimes 7.5 — is standard for competitive institutions and for courses built on language and communication: law, medicine, journalism, English literature, teaching. Some of these set a 7.0 minimum in Writing specifically.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Two cautions. First, these are planning ranges — requirements change yearly, and the only authoritative source is the course page for your intake year. Second, within one university the spread can be wide: the engineering department may ask 6.0 while the law school asks 7.5. Always check the course, not just the institution.</p>

  <h2>IELTS Academic vs IELTS for UKVI</h2>
  <p>This is the point where most applicants get confused, so it is worth stating plainly. <strong>IELTS for UKVI</strong> is the same test as IELTS, with the same content, format and scoring — the difference is that it is administered under conditions approved by the UK Home Office as a Secure English Language Test (SELT), and it costs more.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>For degree-level study and above,</strong> universities are generally allowed to assess your English themselves, which means most accept the standard <strong>IELTS Academic</strong> test for admission and visa sponsorship. This covers the large majority of bachelor's and master's applicants.</li>
    <li><strong>For below-degree-level courses</strong> — including many foundation and some pre-sessional routes — a SELT such as IELTS for UKVI is typically required for the visa.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The practical rule: <strong>ask your university which test to book before you book it.</strong> Immigration rules and university policies both change, so confirm against the university's current guidance and official UK government sources rather than forum posts. Booking the wrong version is an expensive mistake; booking UKVI unnecessarily is merely an overpriced one.</p>

  <h2>Read the requirement's fine print</h2>
  <p>A UK offer will usually specify an overall band <em>and</em> minimums, for example "6.5 overall with no less than 6.0 in each component." The minimums are where applications fail. A 7.0 overall with 5.5 in Writing does not meet that condition; a flat 6.5 does. Plan accordingly:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Identify your floor-risk skill early.</strong> For most candidates it is Writing. Get your essays scored objectively against the official criteria — our <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">IELTS writing checker</a> gives criterion-level feedback so you know your real Writing band, not your hoped-for one.</li>
    <li><strong>Know the averaging rules.</strong> The overall band is the mean of the four skills rounded to the nearest half band, so combinations matter — 6.0 in two skills and 6.5 in two others rounds to 6.5. Run your scenarios through the <a href="/band-calculator">band score calculator</a>, and see our full explanation of <a href="/blog/ielts-band-score-calculation">how the band score is calculated</a>.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>If you miss the requirement: pre-sessional English</h2>
  <p>The UK has a well-established safety net for near-miss candidates: <strong>pre-sessional English courses</strong>. These are intensive university-run programs, typically lasting from a few weeks to a full term, that you complete before your degree starts. Miss your condition by half a band and you will often be offered a place on one, with successful completion replacing the IELTS condition. They work, but they cost real money and weeks of your year — so if your gap is small and your test date is not tight, a focused retake is often the cheaper route. Our <a href="/blog/ielts-retake-strategy">retake strategy guide</a> covers how to convert a near miss into a pass by targeting the exact skills and question types that cost you marks, rather than generically re-preparing everything.</p>

  <h2>Planning backwards from the offer</h2>
  <p>Effective preparation starts with two numbers: the score your course requires and the score you currently produce under exam conditions. Most self-study drifts because candidates only know the first. Establish your baseline with a full timed <a href="/mock-test">IELTS mock test</a>, or use the <a href="/band-estimator">band estimator</a> for a quicker snapshot, then plan by the size and shape of your gap:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Gap of half a band, one skill:</strong> four to six weeks of targeted work on that skill's weakest question types. If it is Reading, drill the types that punish you most; if it is Writing, fix structure before vocabulary — see our <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-band-7-structure">Band 7 Task 2 structure guide</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>Gap of half a band across the board:</strong> two to three months of structured daily practice, mixing skill work with regular timed sections.</li>
    <li><strong>Gap of a band or more:</strong> treat it as language development, not test prep. Daily reading and listening in English, weekly writing with feedback, and monthly mock tests to measure the curve.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Practical details that catch people out</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Validity:</strong> results older than two years at the point of enrolment are generally not accepted. Time your test so it is fresh for your intake.</li>
    <li><strong>Deadlines:</strong> conditional offers come with a date by which you must meet the English condition — usually well before the term starts, to leave time for visa processing. Count backwards from that date, allow for the roughly two-week wait for results (faster for computer-delivered), and leave room for one retake.</li>
    <li><strong>One Skill Retake:</strong> where available, this lets you re-sit a single skill. UK acceptance varies by institution and by visa route, so confirm with your university before relying on it.</li>
    <li><strong>Waivers:</strong> applicants educated in English may qualify for an exemption at some universities. Check the policy before paying for a test you might not need.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The bottom line</h2>
  <p>For most UK applicants the realistic target is <strong>6.5 overall with 6.0 minimums</strong>, rising to 7.0+ for selective institutions and communication-heavy courses. Confirm the exact requirement and the correct test version with your university, find your true baseline before building a study plan, and aim half a band above your requirement — the candidates who comfortably meet their conditions are the ones who prepared to exceed them.</p>
