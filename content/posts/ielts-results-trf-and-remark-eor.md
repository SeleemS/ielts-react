  <p>The test is done, and now you are refreshing your inbox. This guide answers the three questions every candidate has after the exam: <strong>when</strong> your results arrive, <strong>how to read</strong> the Test Report Form, and whether it is worth paying for a <strong>remark</strong> if a score comes back lower than you expected. Everything here applies to both Academic and General Training.</p>

  <h2>When your results arrive</h2>
  <p>The timing depends on how you sat the test:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Computer-delivered IELTS</strong> — results are typically available in <strong>3 to 5 days</strong> after the test.</li>
    <li><strong>Paper-based IELTS</strong> — results are released <strong>13 days</strong> after the test date.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>You will usually see your scores in your online account first, and a printed or electronic <strong>Test Report Form (TRF)</strong> follows. There is no way to pay to get results faster, and scores are not released early over the phone or by email, so treat any offer to "speed up" your result as a scam.</p>

  <h2>How to read your Test Report Form</h2>
  <p>The TRF shows <strong>five numbers</strong>: one band for each of Listening, Reading, Writing and Speaking, plus your <strong>Overall Band Score</strong>. The overall figure is the <strong>average of the four section scores</strong>, rounded to the nearest whole or half band.</p>
  <p>The rounding rule catches people out, so it is worth knowing exactly:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>An average ending in <strong>.25 rounds up</strong> to the next half band.</li>
    <li>An average ending in <strong>.75 rounds up</strong> to the next whole band.</li>
    <li>Anything below .25 rounds down; between .25 and .75 rounds to the half.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>For example, Listening 6.5, Reading 6.5, Writing 6.0 and Speaking 7.0 average to 6.5. But Listening 7.0, Reading 6.5, Writing 6.0 and Speaking 6.5 average to 6.5 as well (26 divided by 4 = 6.5). A single section can tip your overall band across the line, which is exactly why the remark question matters. If you want to see how different section scores combine, put your numbers into our free <a href="/band-calculator">IELTS band score calculator</a>, and read our full explanation of <a href="/blog/ielts-band-score-calculation">how the band score is calculated</a>.</p>

  <h2>Which scores are worth challenging</h2>
  <p>Not every section is equally likely to change on a remark. This is the single most useful thing to understand before you spend money.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Writing and Speaking</strong> are marked by trained human examiners against detailed criteria. Because judgement is involved, these are the sections where a re-mark most often moves a score.</li>
    <li><strong>Listening and Reading</strong> are marked against a fixed answer key. There is far less room for a different result, so remarks here rarely change anything unless a genuine clerical error occurred.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>So if you missed your target by half a band and the shortfall is in Writing or Speaking, a remark is often worth considering. If the gap is in Reading or Listening, your time is usually better spent preparing to retake.</p>

  <h2>How the remark (Enquiry on Results) works</h2>
  <p>The formal remark process is called an <strong>Enquiry on Results (EOR)</strong>. Here is how it runs:</p>
  <ol>
    <li>You apply through the centre where you sat the test, normally <strong>within six weeks</strong> of your test date. Do not miss this window.</li>
    <li>You choose <strong>which sections</strong> to have re-marked — one, several, or all four. You pay a fee per enquiry.</li>
    <li>A <strong>senior examiner</strong>, different from the original marker, re-assesses the sections you selected. Your score can go <strong>up, stay the same, or (rarely) down</strong>.</li>
    <li>If <strong>any</strong> band score increases, the <strong>fee is refunded</strong> in full and you receive an updated TRF. If nothing changes, you keep the original result and forfeit the fee.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>A remark takes time to come back, so if you have a looming university or visa deadline, do the maths on whether the enquiry can finish in time, and be ready with a backup plan.</p>

  <h2>Remark, retake, or One Skill Retake?</h2>
  <p>You have three realistic options after a disappointing result:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Enquiry on Results</strong> — best when you genuinely believe a Writing or Speaking score does not reflect your performance, and you only need a small lift.</li>
    <li><strong>One Skill Retake</strong> — offered for computer-delivered IELTS in many countries, this lets you re-sit a single section rather than the whole test. Useful when one section is clearly dragging you down.</li>
    <li><strong>Full retake</strong> — the right call when more than one section is below target, or when honest reflection tells you the mark was fair and you need to improve.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Before deciding on a Writing remark, it helps to know objectively where your essay stood. Paste your Task 2 answer into our free <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">IELTS Writing checker</a> for feedback against the four assessment criteria — if it points to clear weaknesses in task response or grammar, a retake with targeted practice will serve you better than an enquiry.</p>

  <h2>The bottom line</h2>
  <p>Read your TRF carefully, understand the rounding, and be honest about which section let you down. Challenge a human-marked score when the gap is small and you have real doubt; retake when the feedback says the result was fair. Either way, decide quickly — the six-week enquiry window closes faster than you think.</p>
