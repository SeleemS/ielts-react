  <p>After your hometown, the topic you are most likely to meet in IELTS Speaking Part 1 is what you do all day: your job or your studies. The examiner's opening question — "Do you work, or are you a student?" — routes you into one of two question sets, and because everyone can predict this topic, everyone prepares it. That cuts both ways. Prepared candidates sound fluent; over-prepared candidates sound like they are reading an invisible script, and examiners are trained to hear the difference. This guide gives you original model answers for both routes and, more importantly, the technique that keeps them sounding like speech rather than recitation.</p>

  <h2>How the examiner uses this topic</h2>
  <p>Part 1 questions about work and study are deliberately personal and concrete: what you do, why you chose it, what you like about it, what a typical day looks like. The examiner is not evaluating your career. They are listening for <strong>fluency</strong> (connected answers without long content pauses), <strong>vocabulary</strong> (natural workplace and study collocations), <strong>grammar</strong> (tense control, especially present simple for routines and past tenses for choices you made), and <strong>pronunciation</strong>. Aim for two to four sentences per answer: a direct response, a reason or example, and a natural stop.</p>

  <h2>Original model answers: the work route</h2>
  <p>Everything below is an <strong>original model answer written for practice</strong> — not a transcript of a real exam. Adapt the structures to your own situation instead of memorising the sentences.</p>

  <h3>What do you do?</h3>
  <p><em>"I work as a nurse at a public hospital, mostly on the paediatric ward. I've been there for about three years now, and although the shifts can be exhausting, it's the kind of job where you can see the point of what you're doing every single day."</em></p>
  <p><strong>Why it works:</strong> job title, one specifying detail, present perfect for duration, and a balanced comment with a natural concession ("although the shifts can be exhausting").</p>

  <h3>Why did you choose this job?</h3>
  <p><em>"Partly family influence, to be honest — my mother was a nurse, so I grew up around it. But the deciding factor was a hospital placement I did at eighteen. After two weeks there I couldn't really imagine doing anything else."</em></p>
  <p><strong>Why it works:</strong> "Partly… But the deciding factor was…" organises the answer; past tenses carry the story; "couldn't really imagine doing anything else" is natural, idiomatic and accurate.</p>

  <h3>What do you like most about your job?</h3>
  <p><em>"The variety, mainly. No two shifts are the same, and I get to work with a really close-knit team. If I had to pick one thing, though, it's the moment a child who came in seriously ill walks out the door healthy."</em></p>

  <h3>Is there anything you would change about it?</h3>
  <p><em>"The paperwork, definitely. A surprising amount of the day goes on documentation rather than actual patient care, and I think most of my colleagues would say the same. Better staffing levels would help too, but that's true in hospitals everywhere."</em></p>

  <h2>Original model answers: the study route</h2>
  <h3>What do you study?</h3>
  <p><em>"I'm in my second year of a civil engineering degree. This semester is mostly structural analysis and materials science, which sounds dry but is actually the part I enjoy most — it's where the maths finally connects to real buildings."</em></p>
  <p><strong>Why it works:</strong> degree, year, and a specific current detail, plus a small self-aware turn ("which sounds dry but…") that shows genuine spontaneous speech.</p>

  <h3>Why did you choose this subject?</h3>
  <p><em>"Mostly because I was always the kid who wanted to know how bridges stay up. I considered architecture as well, but I realised I cared more about whether a structure works than how it looks, so engineering felt like the better fit."</em></p>

  <h3>Do you prefer studying in the morning or the evening?</h3>
  <p><em>"Definitely the morning. My concentration falls off a cliff after about nine at night, so I'd rather start at seven and get the difficult material done before lunch. Evenings I keep for lighter things like reviewing notes."</em></p>

  <h3>What do you plan to do after you graduate?</h3>
  <p><em>"The plan is to join a construction firm for a few years to get chartered, and after that I'd like to specialise in earthquake-resistant design. That might mean a master's abroad eventually, which is actually one of the reasons I'm taking IELTS."</em></p>
  <p><strong>Why it works:</strong> future forms with realistic hedging ("might mean," "eventually"), and a genuinely personal closing detail. Honest, specific answers are always easier to deliver fluently than invented impressive ones.</p>

  <h2>The technique behind the answers</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Direct answer first, always.</strong> Every model starts by actually answering: "I work as…," "Definitely the morning," "The variety, mainly." Front-loading the answer keeps coherence marks safe even if you then wander slightly.</li>
    <li><strong>One reason or example per answer.</strong> That is what turns a Band 5 fragment into a Band 7 response. You do not need three reasons; you need one, developed.</li>
    <li><strong>Use the vocabulary of your own field.</strong> "Paediatric ward," "structural analysis," "get chartered" — specific, accurate terms from your real life are the easiest lexical resource to deploy and the hardest to fake. Prepare five or six of them.</li>
    <li><strong>Mind the work/study tense split.</strong> Routines take present simple ("I work," "I get to"), duration takes present perfect ("I've been there for three years"), past choices take past simple, plans take "planning to / hoping to / might." Most grammar marks lost on this topic come from mixing these up.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Common mistakes on this topic</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>The CV recitation.</strong> Listing your responsibilities in formal written English ("I am responsible for the coordination of…") sounds memorised. Speak the way you would to a friendly stranger.</li>
    <li><strong>Answering "what" when asked "why."</strong> "Why did you choose it?" needs a reason and usually a past tense, not a repeat of your job description.</li>
    <li><strong>Pretending to love everything.</strong> "Is there anything you'd change?" invites mild, specific criticism. "No, everything is perfect" wastes a chance to show range; complaining naturally, with hedges like "to be honest" and "a surprising amount," is exactly the register Part 1 rewards.</li>
    <li><strong>Over-length answers.</strong> Part 1 answers past four or five sentences eat time you will want in Part 3, and examiners may cut you off — which is not a penalty, but flusters many candidates.</li>
  </ul>
