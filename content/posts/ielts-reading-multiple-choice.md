  <p>Multiple choice in IELTS Reading feels reassuring — the answer is printed right in front of you, so surely you just recognise it. In practice it is one of the slower, trap-heavy question types on the paper, because every wrong option is written to look plausible and the passages are dense enough that reading the options carelessly eats the clock you need elsewhere. This guide gives you a repeatable method built around the two things that actually decide the mark: reading the options efficiently, and matching on <strong>meaning</strong> rather than on words that happen to appear in the text.</p>

  <h2>What the question type looks like</h2>
  <p>IELTS Reading gives you <strong>40 questions in 60 minutes</strong> across three passages, with no extra time to transfer answers, so an inefficient method on one question type quietly steals marks from the rest of the paper. Multiple choice appears in two formats, and the instruction tells you which you have:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Single answer:</strong> a question stem or an unfinished sentence followed by four options, A to D. You choose the one that is correct or that best completes the sentence.</li>
    <li><strong>Multiple answer:</strong> a longer list, often A to E or A to G, where you are told to choose <em>two</em> or <em>three</em>. Each correct choice is a separate mark, and the required answers can sit several lines apart in the text.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Crucially, single-answer multiple choice follows the passage in order: the answer to question 15 comes after question 14 and before question 16. A multiple-answer question, by contrast, draws on one region of the text, so all its options are usually found close together.</p>

  <h2>Read the question before the options</h2>
  <p>The most common way candidates lose time here is by reading all four options first and then hunting the passage for any of them. Reverse it. <strong>Read the stem first</strong> and turn it into a question you can hold in your head — "What was the main reason the project failed?" or "What does the writer say about early cities?" Underline the keyword in the stem, then find the matching part of the passage, and only <em>then</em> compare the options against what the text actually says. Reading the passage region once, with a clear question in mind, is far faster than testing four options blindly against three paragraphs.</p>

  <h2>Match the meaning, never the words</h2>
  <p>The single golden rule of Reading multiple choice is that the correct option <strong>paraphrases</strong> the passage, while the tempting wrong options often reuse its exact words. IELTS deliberately builds distractors from vocabulary lifted straight from the text so that a word-spotter picks them. If the passage says a policy "yielded no measurable improvement," the right option might read "had little effect," while a trap option repeats "improvement" in a sentence that means the opposite. Train yourself to distrust any option that feels right only because you recognise its words from the passage — that recognition is usually the trap, not the answer.</p>

  <h2>How the distractors are built</h2>
  <p>Understanding how wrong options are constructed is most of the defence. Four patterns recur:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>The true-but-irrelevant option.</strong> A statement that is factually supported by the passage but does not answer the actual question. It is correct information in the wrong place.</li>
    <li><strong>The half-right option.</strong> The first half matches the text and the second half quietly contradicts it, or adds a claim the passage never makes. You must read the whole option, not just its opening.</li>
    <li><strong>The extreme option.</strong> The passage says something is "often" or "sometimes" true; the option upgrades it to "always" or "never." A single strong word makes an otherwise reasonable option wrong.</li>
    <li><strong>The word-for-word trap.</strong> An option that copies a memorable phrase from the passage but sits in a sentence whose meaning has been twisted. Recognisable words, wrong idea.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>A worked example</h2>
  <p><strong>Question:</strong> What does the writer suggest about early urban planning?</p>
  <ul>
    <li>A. It was more advanced than historians once assumed.</li>
    <li>B. It relied entirely on religious considerations.</li>
    <li>C. It was copied directly from earlier villages.</li>
    <li>D. It failed because of poor water management.</li>
  </ul>
  <p><strong>Passage extract:</strong> "Excavations have revealed drainage systems and grid layouts far more sophisticated than earlier scholars had credited to these settlements."</p>
  <p>The answer is <strong>A</strong>. The passage never uses the word "advanced," but "far more sophisticated than earlier scholars had credited" is a direct paraphrase of it. Option B is an extreme claim ("entirely") the text never makes; C introduces a comparison with villages the passage does not state; D is a plausible-sounding failure the extract flatly contradicts. Only A matches the <em>meaning</em>, and it does so without sharing a single keyword with the text.</p>

  <h2>Handling the "choose two" format</h2>
  <p>Multiple-answer questions reward patience over speed. Because all the options draw on one section of the passage, read that section carefully and lightly mark <em>every</em> option the text supports before you commit, rather than grabbing the first two that look right. Read the instruction exactly, too: if it says choose two, marking three scores zero for the whole question, the same way an over-length gap-fill answer is marked wrong.</p>

  <h2>Mistakes that quietly cost marks</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Choosing an option because its words appear in the passage.</strong> Matching vocabulary is the trap; match the whole idea instead.</li>
    <li><strong>Reading only the first half of a long option.</strong> The contradiction is usually hidden in the second half.</li>
    <li><strong>Ignoring extreme language.</strong> "All," "never," "only," and "always" are worth pausing on — the passage rarely supports absolutes.</li>
    <li><strong>Spending too long on one hard question.</strong> If two options survive after about 90 seconds, mark your best guess, note the number, and move on before it drains time from easier passages.</li>
    <li><strong>Leaving blanks.</strong> There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so every question must have a letter before the hour ends.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>How to practise it</h2>
  <p>Do a focused set of multiple-choice questions, then review each one with the answer key open and, for every wrong option, name <em>why</em> it was wrong — true-but-irrelevant, half-right, extreme, or a word-for-word trap.
