  <p>"Describe a goal you would like to achieve" — sometimes phrased as "an ambition you have," "a goal you set for yourself," or "something you hope to do in the future" — is a staple of IELTS Speaking Part 2. It looks easy because everyone has goals, but it trips more candidates than the average card for one reason: goals are abstract, and abstract topics produce vague, repetitive talk. The fix is to treat the goal as a story with a past, a present and a future. This guide shows you how, with a complete original model answer and the Part 3 discussion that follows.</p>

  <h2>The cue card</h2>
  <p><strong>Describe a goal you would like to achieve.</strong> You should say:<br />what the goal is<br />when you started to have this goal<br />what you are doing to achieve it<br />and explain why this goal is important to you.</p>
  <p>Look at what the bullets are really asking for: a definition, a past-tense origin story, present-tense current effort, and a reflective explanation. The card has secretly handed you a four-paragraph structure and a full range of tenses. Use both.</p>

  <h2>The one-minute preparation</h2>
  <p>Write trigger words, not sentences — five is plenty. For this card: <em>goal — origin moment — current routine — obstacle — why it matters</em>. The two notes that rescue weak talks are <strong>origin moment</strong> and <strong>obstacle</strong>. A specific scene where the goal began gives you narrative material, and an honest obstacle gives you something concrete to discuss instead of empty determination phrases. Choose a real goal at a human scale: running a race, reaching a language level, saving for something specific, qualifying in your profession. Real goals come with real details, and details are what fill two minutes.</p>

  <h2>Original model answer</h2>
  <p>This is an <strong>original model answer written for practice on this site</strong> — not a transcript of a real exam. Borrow its structure, not its sentences.</p>
  <p><em>"The goal I'd like to talk about is finishing a half marathon, which is twenty-one kilometres — something that would have sounded completely absurd to me two years ago, because I was the kind of person who took the lift to the first floor.</em></p>
  <p><em>It started, honestly, with a health scare in the family. My father had heart problems the year before last, and his doctor was very blunt with all of us about lifestyle. I remember sitting in the hospital corridor thinking that I was thirty years younger than him and already less active. That evening I signed up for a five-kilometre fun run that was three months away, mostly so I couldn't back out, and after I finished that one the goal just kept growing.</em></p>
  <p><em>What I'm doing about it now is fairly unglamorous: I run three mornings a week before work, following a training plan from a running app, and I do one longer run on Sundays that gets a little further each fortnight. The hardest part isn't really physical, it's consistency — protecting those mornings when work gets busy or the weather is miserable. I've also had to learn to slow down, oddly enough, because beginners apparently ruin their progress by running every session too fast, and I was a textbook case.</em></p>

  <p><em>As for why it matters — on the surface it's about health, and it did start that way. But at this point it's become something slightly bigger: proof to myself that I can pick something difficult, stick with it for months, and actually change what kind of person I am. My father asks about my training every week now, which has become a small ritual between us. So when I cross that finish line — hopefully next spring — it won't really be about the running at all."</em></p>

  <h2>Why this answer scores well</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Every bullet becomes a paragraph.</strong> Definition, origin, current effort, significance — the structure is audible, so the coherence marks take care of themselves.</li>
    <li><strong>The tenses map to the content.</strong> Past narrative for the hospital scene, present simple for the routine, present perfect for progress ("it's become," "I've had to learn"), future for the finish line. That is grammatical range produced by the story itself, not bolted on.</li>
    <li><strong>The obstacle is specific.</strong> "Protecting those mornings" and the detail about running too fast are the kind of concrete, slightly self-critical material that sounds unmistakably genuine — the opposite of a memorised motivational speech.</li>
    <li><strong>The vocabulary earns its place:</strong> "back out," "unglamorous," "a textbook case," "a small ritual." Precise, idiomatic, and never decorative.</li>
    <li><strong>The ending reframes.</strong> "It won't really be about the running at all" closes the talk with reflection, which is exactly what the final bullet — the <em>explain</em> bullet — is asking for. Weak answers describe; strong answers explain.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Adapting the skeleton</h2>
  <p>The frame — <strong>what the goal is → the moment it started → the honest, unglamorous routine → one real obstacle → what it really means</strong> — works for nearly any goal card: passing a professional exam, reaching Band 7 in IELTS, learning to drive, starting a small business. It also covers neighbouring cards like "describe a difficult thing you did" (told in past tense) and "describe a skill you want to learn." Prepare one goal deeply, with a real origin scene and a real obstacle, and you own the whole topic family.</p>

  <h2>Part 3: from your goal to goals in general</h2>
  <p>Expect the discussion to widen. Two typical questions with brief original model responses:</p>
  <p><strong>Should parents set goals for their children?</strong> <em>"To a point. Young children benefit from structure they can't create themselves, so goals around study habits or sport seem healthy. But somewhere in the teenage years the ownership has to transfer, because a goal you didn't choose tends to collapse the moment the pressure behind it disappears."</em></p>
  <p><strong>Why do some people achieve their goals while others give up?</strong> <em>"I'd say the difference is usually systems rather than willpower. People who succeed tend to make the goal concrete and schedule it — a race with a date, a class every Tuesday — while people who rely on motivation alone run out of it. Circumstances matter too, of course; it's easier to persist when you have time and support."</em></p>
  <p>The pattern is the one that runs through all of <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-3-discussion">Part 3</a>: position, developed reason, qualification.</p>
