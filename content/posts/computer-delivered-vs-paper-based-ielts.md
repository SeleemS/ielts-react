---
title: "Computer-Delivered vs Paper-Based IELTS: Which Should You Choose?"
date: July 23, 2026
excerpt: Same content, same 9-band scoring, different experience. Here is exactly what changes between computer and paper IELTS, and how to pick the format that suits you.
---

  <p>Once you have decided between Academic and General Training, the next question the booking page asks is how you want to sit the test: on a <strong>computer</strong> or on <strong>paper</strong>. Many candidates pick at random, then walk into an exam that feels unfamiliar on the day. The two formats test exactly the same skills and are marked on the same scale, but the experience differs in ways that genuinely affect some people's scores. This guide explains every real difference and helps you choose deliberately.</p>

  <h2>What is identical in both formats</h2>
  <p>Start with the reassuring part. Whichever format you book, these do not change:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>The content and question types.</strong> Reading is still 40 questions in 60 minutes, Listening is still four sections and 40 questions in roughly 30 minutes, and Writing still has two tasks. The same question types appear: <a href="/reading/true-false-not-given">True/False/Not Given</a>, matching headings, multiple choice, and the rest.</li>
    <li><strong>The scoring.</strong> Both use the 9-band scale, the same raw-score-to-band conversion, and the same four Writing and Speaking criteria. Nobody scores higher simply for choosing one format.</li>
    <li><strong>The Speaking test.</strong> Speaking is always a face-to-face interview with a real examiner, never with a machine. Even for computer-delivered IELTS, you sit with a human examiner (occasionally by live video call at the centre) for the same 11 to 14 minute, three-part conversation.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>So your preparation is the same either way. Everything on the <a href="/readingquestion">Reading</a> and <a href="/listeningquestion">Listening practice</a> pages applies regardless of which format you choose.</p>

  <h2>Difference 1: Listening timing at the end</h2>
  <p>This is the difference candidates notice most, and it catches people out.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Paper-based:</strong> you write answers on the question paper as you listen, then get <strong>10 extra minutes</strong> at the end to copy them onto the answer sheet. That transfer time is when spelling mistakes creep in.</li>
    <li><strong>Computer-delivered:</strong> you type answers directly into the boxes as you listen, so there is no transfer stage. Instead you get only <strong>2 minutes</strong> at the end to check your answers.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Neither is objectively better, but they reward different habits. On computer you must be comfortable typing while listening; on paper you must copy accurately under time pressure without mishearing your own handwriting.</p>

  <h2>Difference 2: Reading and Writing on screen</h2>
  <p>In computer-delivered Reading and Writing, everything happens on screen. This brings small advantages and small annoyances:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>A live word count</strong> is shown for Writing, so you never have to count words by hand or guess whether you have passed 150 or 250.</li>
    <li><strong>Editing is effortless.</strong> You can cut, paste, and reorder sentences instead of crossing out and squeezing text into margins. For candidates who plan loosely and revise as they go, this is a real benefit.</li>
    <li><strong>You cannot physically annotate the passage.</strong> There are highlight and note tools, but no underlining with a pen. Some readers find on-screen text harder to skim, and long passages require scrolling rather than a glance down the page.</li>
    <li><strong>Handwriting is removed as a variable.</strong> If your handwriting is slow or hard to read, typing protects you from losing marks to legibility. If you type slowly, paper may serve you better.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Difference 3: How fast you get results</h2>
  <p>This is often the deciding factor for people with a visa or admissions deadline:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Computer-delivered results</strong> are typically available in about <strong>3 to 5 days</strong>.</li>
    <li><strong>Paper-based results</strong> usually take around <strong>13 days</strong>.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>If you may need to retake before a deadline, the faster turnaround of the computer test gives you far more room to book a second attempt.</p>

  <h2>Difference 4: Availability and test-day environment</h2>
  <p>Computer-delivered IELTS runs far more frequently, sometimes several times a day, and is booked at dedicated centres with individual computer stations and noise-reducing headphones for Listening. Paper-based sittings are less frequent, often on set Saturdays, in larger rooms where Listening is played over speakers. If you are sensitive to background noise, the individual headphones of the computer test can be a genuine advantage.</p>

  <h2>How to choose: a short checklist</h2>
  <p>Ask yourself these questions honestly:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Do you type faster and more comfortably than you write by hand?</strong> If yes, computer suits you, especially for Writing under time pressure.</li>
    <li><strong>Do you rely on underlining and scribbling on the passage to read?</strong> If yes, paper may feel more natural, though the on-screen tools are usable once practised.</li>
    <li><strong>Do you need your result quickly?</strong> If yes, choose computer for the 3-to-5-day turnaround.</li>
    <li><strong>Does screen reading tire your eyes over an hour?</strong> If yes, paper may keep you fresher for Reading and Writing.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Crucially, the format you choose has nothing to do with whether you sit Academic or General Training, that decision is separate and explained in our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-academic-vs-general-training">Academic vs General Training</a>. You choose the version <em>and</em> the format when you book, and neither can be changed on test day.</p>

  <h2>Prepare in the format you will sit</h2>
  <p>The single most useful thing you can do is rehearse in your chosen format. If you book computer-delivered, practise typing full Writing answers and answering Listening while typing, not on paper. If you book paper-based, handwrite your essays and rehearse the 10-minute Listening transfer so it becomes automatic. Familiarity removes a layer of stress on the day and stops the format itself from costing you marks.</p>
  <p>Whichever you choose, the content is the same, so drill it thoroughly on the <a href="/readingquestion">Reading</a> and <a href="/listeningquestion">Listening</a> practice pages, and once you have raw scores, convert them with the free <a href="/band-calculator">IELTS band score calculator</a> to see exactly where you stand before booking.</p>
