  <p>Candidates walk out of the Speaking test with theories about their performance — "I hesitated too much," "I used good idioms," "my accent is a problem." Examiners, meanwhile, mark against four published criteria, each worth exactly a quarter of the score. Most of the folk theories map onto the descriptors poorly: hesitation matters, but only a specific kind; idioms help, but only used naturally; accent, by itself, is not marked at all. This guide goes through the four criteria in plain language, so you know what is actually being assessed during those eleven to fourteen minutes.</p>

  <h2>How the marking works</h2>
  <p>The examiner rates your whole performance — all three parts together — on <strong>Fluency and Coherence</strong>, <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong>, and <strong>Pronunciation</strong>, each as a whole band from 0 to 9. The four are averaged to give your Speaking band. Because the criteria are equally weighted, a candidate with rich vocabulary but constant self-correction, or accurate grammar delivered in broken fragments, plateaus quickly. Balanced competence beats one spectacular strength.</p>

  <h2>Criterion 1: Fluency and Coherence</h2>
  <p>This is the criterion candidates misunderstand most. It does not reward speed, and it does not punish all pausing. It measures whether you can <strong>keep going at a natural rate</strong> and whether your speech is <strong>organised and connected</strong>.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>At Band 5,</strong> the speaker keeps going but relies on repetition and self-correction, or slows noticeably to search for language; overuse of certain connectives is typical.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 6,</strong> the speaker is willing to speak at length, but loses coherence at times through hesitation, repetition or self-correction.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 7,</strong> the speaker speaks at length without noticeable effort; hesitation is mostly <em>content-related</em> — pausing to think about ideas rather than to find words — and discourse markers are used with some flexibility.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 8–9,</strong> speech flows with only rare, content-related hesitation, and topics are developed coherently and appropriately.</li>
  </ul>
  <p><strong>The key distinction:</strong> pausing to think about <em>what</em> to say is human and largely forgiven; pausing to hunt for <em>words</em> is penalised. You can hear the difference in your own recordings — language-searching pauses come mid-sentence, before ordinary words. The fix is practice volume on familiar topics, which is exactly what Part 1 topic families are for: drill them with our <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-1-answers">Part 1 sample answers</a> until everyday subjects cost you no word-searching at all.</p>

  <h2>Criterion 2: Lexical Resource</h2>
  <p>This measures the <strong>range and precision</strong> of your vocabulary, your ability to <strong>paraphrase</strong> when a word will not come, and — from Band 7 up — the natural use of <strong>idiomatic language</strong> and collocation.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>At Band 5,</strong> the speaker manages familiar and unfamiliar topics but with limited flexibility, and attempts paraphrase with mixed success.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 6,</strong> vocabulary is wide enough to discuss topics at length and paraphrase generally succeeds, though word choice is sometimes inappropriate.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 7,</strong> the speaker uses vocabulary flexibly, shows awareness of style and collocation, and uses some less common and idiomatic items.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 8–9,</strong> vocabulary is used with full flexibility and precision; idiomatic language is natural and accurate.</li>
  </ul>
  <p><strong>What caps you here:</strong> planted idioms. "It's raining cats and dogs" delivered into a question about your hometown does not read as Band 7 idiomatic use; it reads as a memorised insert. What the descriptors mean by idiomatic language is the natural phrasal texture of real speech — "took it for granted," "fell through," "a close-knit team." Collect collocations by topic rather than isolated impressive words: our topic banks for <a href="/blog/ielts-vocabulary-environment">environment</a> and <a href="/blog/ielts-vocabulary-technology">technology</a> vocabulary show the method. And practise paraphrase deliberately — being visibly able to talk around a missing word is itself a scored skill.</p>

  <h2>Criterion 3: Grammatical Range and Accuracy</h2>
  <p>As in Writing, this criterion wants both <strong>variety</strong> — complex structures, not only simple sentences — and <strong>accuracy</strong>, measured largely by how many of your sentences are error-free.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>At Band 5,</strong> basic sentence forms are reasonably controlled, but complex structures are limited and error-prone.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 6,</strong> the speaker mixes simple and complex forms, but errors recur in the complex ones.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 7,</strong> a range of complex structures is used with some flexibility, and many sentences are error-free, even if some mistakes persist.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 8–9,</strong> the range is wide and errors are rare, often just slips.</li>
  </ul>
  <p><strong>How to earn range without risking accuracy:</strong> let the question pull the grammar out of you. "Has your hometown changed?" invites the present perfect; "What would you change about your job?" invites conditionals; describing a past experience in <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-2-cue-card">Part 2</a> invites narrative tenses. Answering the question fully and honestly produces natural range; bolting on rehearsed complex frames ("Were I to be given the opportunity…") produces errors and memorised-sounding delivery. In practice sessions, pick one structure you avoid — say, second conditionals — and force three honest uses per session until it stops being a risk.</p>

  <h2>Criterion 4: Pronunciation</h2>
  <p>The most misunderstood criterion. <strong>Accent is not the issue.</strong> The descriptors reward being effortlessly intelligible and using the sound systems of English — individual sounds, word stress, sentence stress, intonation and chunking — to carry meaning.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>At Band 5,</strong> pronunciation features are attempted with mixed control, and the listener occasionally has to work to understand.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 6,</strong> a range of features is used with mixed control; mispronunciations occur but rarely block understanding.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 7,</strong> features like stress and intonation are used with some flexibility, and the speaker is easy to understand throughout, even if traces of the first language remain.</li>
    <li><strong>At Band 8–9,</strong> the full range of features is used flexibly and the speaker is effortless to understand; a foreign accent may still be present and it does not matter.</li>
  </ul>
  <p><strong>The highest-yield work</strong> is usually not individual sounds but <strong>sentence stress and chunking</strong> — stressing the words that carry meaning and grouping words into natural phrases with small pauses between them. Flat, evenly-paced delivery is what makes competent English tiring to follow. Record yourself, mark the stressed words in one answer, and re-record it with deliberate emphasis; the improvement is usually audible on the first attempt.</p>

  <h2>Turning the descriptors into a practice loop</h2>
  <p>The criteria give you a four-question checklist for every recorded practice answer: Did I keep going, with only thinking-pauses? Was my vocabulary precise and collocated, with paraphrase where needed? How many sentences were fully correct, and did I use any complex structures? Would a stranger follow me effortlessly? Score yourself one criterion at a time — listening for everything at once catches nothing. Then drill with purpose: authentic-style questions for all three parts are on our <a href="/speakingquestion">Speaking practice</a> pages, and a full <a href="/mock-test">mock test</a> tells you how the criteria hold up under real timing and pressure. Examiners are trained to rate exactly these four things; from today, your practice sessions should be too.</p>
