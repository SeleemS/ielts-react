  <p>If you sit IELTS this year, you will almost certainly meet technology somewhere: a Task 2 essay on children and screens, a Speaking Part 1 set about your phone, a Part 3 discussion on artificial intelligence and jobs, or a Reading passage about the history of some invention. It is arguably the most frequent topic family in the test. Yet most candidates prepare for it with a strange, formal word list — "ubiquitous," "cutting-edge," "technological advancement" — that produces essays sounding like press releases. What the Lexical Resource criterion actually rewards is <strong>natural collocation</strong>: ordinary words in the pairings fluent English really uses. This guide gives you those pairings, organised by sub-topic, with notes on using them accurately.</p>

  <h2>The principle: precision over decoration</h2>
  <p>Compare two sentences a candidate might write about the same idea. Decorated: "In the contemporary epoch, ubiquitous technological gadgets have revolutionised the lifestyle of humanity." Precise: "Smartphones have <strong>changed how people communicate</strong>, and most of us now <strong>rely on</strong> them for everything from banking to navigation." The first strains for impressiveness and produces something no fluent writer would say; the second uses unremarkable words, perfectly partnered, and reads as genuinely proficient English. The band descriptors for Writing and Speaking both name collocation awareness as a Band 7 feature — the details are in our guides to the <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-band-descriptors">Writing band descriptors</a> and <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors">Speaking band descriptors</a>.</p>

  <h2>Devices and everyday use</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>rely on / depend on technology</strong> — "We've become heavily reliant on our phones" is natural in Speaking.</li>
    <li><strong>browse the internet; search online; look something up</strong> — the phrasal verb "look up" is exactly the idiomatic texture Speaking rewards.</li>
    <li><strong>download an app; install software; update your phone</strong> — simple, precise verb–noun pairs that many candidates get wrong ("open an application in my phone").</li>
    <li><strong>screen time; spend hours on</strong> — "Parents worry about their children's screen time" is the natural frame for a very common essay.</li>
    <li><strong>battery life; run out of battery</strong> — good Part 1 material: "My main complaint is the battery life."</li>
    <li><strong>user-friendly; intuitive</strong> — "The interface is so intuitive my grandmother learned it in a day."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Internet, media and communication</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>social media platforms; go viral</strong> — note "on social media," never "in social media."</li>
    <li><strong>stay in touch with / keep in contact with</strong> — "Video calls make it easy to stay in touch with family abroad."</li>
    <li><strong>access information; a reliable source</strong> — "The internet gives everyone access to information, but not everyone checks whether the source is reliable."</li>
    <li><strong>spread misinformation; fake news</strong> — "Platforms make it effortless to spread misinformation" is a ready-made Part 3 point.</li>
    <li><strong>online privacy; personal data; data protection</strong> — "Companies collect enormous amounts of personal data, often with only nominal consent."</li>
    <li><strong>face-to-face interaction</strong> — the standard contrast term: "Messaging is convenient, but it's no substitute for face-to-face interaction."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Work, education and AI</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>work remotely; work from home</strong> — "Remote work has blurred the line between office and home."</li>
    <li><strong>automate tasks; be replaced by machines</strong> — "Routine tasks are being automated first; jobs built on judgement are proving more resistant."</li>
    <li><strong>artificial intelligence (AI)</strong> — pair it with real verbs: AI can "generate," "predict," "recommend," "misidentify." Specific verbs beat "AI is developing very fast."</li>
    <li><strong>online learning; distance education; virtual classrooms</strong> — "Online learning widens access but demands far more self-discipline."</li>
    <li><strong>digital skills; computer literacy; the digital divide</strong> — the divide is the gap between those with access and those without, and it anchors many strong essay paragraphs.</li>
    <li><strong>keep up with / keep pace with technology</strong> — "Regulation rarely keeps pace with technology."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Evaluating technology: the language of advantages and drawbacks</h2>
  <p>Most technology questions ultimately ask you to weigh benefits against harms, so the highest-value language is evaluative:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>save time / save lives</strong> vs <strong>waste time</strong> — "The same device that saves time at work absorbs hours of it at home."</li>
    <li><strong>make life easier / more convenient</strong> — the plainest and most reliable benefit frame.</li>
    <li><strong>pose a risk to; raise concerns about</strong> — "Facial recognition raises serious concerns about surveillance."</li>
    <li><strong>a double-edged sword</strong> — a genuinely natural idiom for technology essays, provided it is used once and developed, not dropped in as decoration.</li>
    <li><strong>outweigh</strong> — the essential advantage/disadvantage verb: "The benefits of telemedicine outweigh its limitations, at least for routine care." Central to essays covered in our <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-advantages-disadvantages">advantages–disadvantages essay guide</a>.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Putting it to work</h2>
  <p>In Writing, aim for four or five of these collocations per essay, placed where the argument needs them. A Task 2 sentence like "Governments should invest in digital skills training so that older workers are not left on the wrong side of the digital divide" carries three natural pairings in one clause without a single rare word. In Speaking, the same bank works at a lighter register: "I'd be lost without my phone, honestly — I use it to look things up constantly, though I'm trying to cut down my screen time." Technology prompts to practise against are plentiful in our <a href="/ielts-writing-task-2-topics">Task 2 topic bank</a> and the <a href="/speakingquestion">Speaking question bank</a> — and the cue card "describe a useful website" pairs directly with our <a href="/blog/ielts-cue-card-describe-a-useful-website">model answer for it</a>.</p>

  <h2>A learning method that actually sticks</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Record pairings, not words.</strong> "Raise concerns about" is one vocabulary item, not three.</li>
    <li><strong>Write a true sentence for each</strong> — about your own phone, job or habits. Meaningful use is what moves phrases into active vocabulary.</li>
    <li><strong>Rotate them into timed practice.</strong> Write one technology essay a week and check whether the collocations arrive on their own; feedback from the <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">IELTS writing checker</a> will show whether they read as natural or forced.</li>
    <li><strong>Mine your Reading practice.</strong> Technology passages are constant in the Reading test — after each one, harvest three pairings you would actually reuse.</li>
    <li><strong>Cap the density.</strong> One idiom and a handful of collocations per answer, used precisely, is the Band 7+ signature. Ten in a paragraph is the Band 6 memorisation signature.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Technology and the <a href="/blog/ielts-vocabulary-environment">environment</a> are the two most predictable topic families in IELTS. Build both banks properly — pairings, true sentences, timed deployment — and a large share of your test becomes home ground.</p>
