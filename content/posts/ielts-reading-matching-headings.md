  <p><strong>Matching Headings</strong> is one of the most disliked question types in IELTS Reading, and for good reason: there are always more headings than paragraphs, the wording never matches the text directly, and one wrong choice can trigger a chain of further mistakes. Yet the skill it tests, identifying the main idea of a paragraph, is completely learnable. This guide gives you a repeatable method so you can match every paragraph with confidence instead of second-guessing yourself.</p>

  <h2>What Matching Headings Actually Tests</h2>
  <p>In this question type you are given a list of headings, each labelled with a Roman numeral (i, ii, iii...), and a passage divided into lettered or numbered paragraphs. Your job is to choose the heading that best summarises the <strong>main idea</strong> of each paragraph. The key word is <em>main</em>: a heading that matches a small detail buried in the paragraph is a trap, not the answer. You are looking for the point that the whole paragraph is built around.</p>
  <p>Two features make this type harder than it looks. First, there are always <strong>more headings than paragraphs</strong>, so some headings are never used, they exist only to distract you. Second, unlike most Reading questions, Matching Headings does <strong>not</strong> follow the order of the passage, so you cannot assume the answer to paragraph B sits below the answer to paragraph A.</p>

  <h2>A Five-Step Method That Works</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Read the paragraph first, not the headings.</strong> If you read the headings first, you will unconsciously hunt for words that match and fall for detail traps. Instead, read the paragraph and decide in your own words what it is mainly about before you look at the options.</li>
    <li><strong>Focus on the topic sentence.</strong> The main idea usually appears in the first or last sentence of the paragraph. The middle is often examples and supporting detail. Ask: what point do all these sentences add up to?</li>
    <li><strong>Summarise the paragraph in a few words.</strong> Before touching the heading list, say to yourself: "This paragraph is about the cost of renewable energy." Now you have a target to match against.</li>
    <li><strong>Match your summary to a heading.</strong> Find the heading that expresses your summary, allowing for synonyms and paraphrase. The correct heading captures the whole paragraph, not one sentence in it.</li>
    <li><strong>Cross out headings as you use them.</strong> Each heading is used only once, so eliminating used options narrows the field for the paragraphs you find harder.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Start With the Paragraphs You Find Easiest</h2>
  <p>You do not have to work through the paragraphs in order. Match the ones whose main idea is obvious first, then tackle the difficult paragraphs with a shorter list of remaining headings. This "easy first" approach turns a daunting set into a process of elimination, and it stops one hard paragraph from eating all your time.</p>

  <h2>Main Idea vs Supporting Detail</h2>
  <p>The single most common mistake is choosing a heading that matches a word or fact that appears in the paragraph but is not what the paragraph is <em>about</em>. Consider a paragraph that argues electric cars are becoming cheaper, and mentions in passing that early models were slow. A heading like "The poor performance of early electric cars" matches a real detail but misses the point, the paragraph is about <strong>falling prices</strong>. Always test a candidate heading with one question: does this cover the whole paragraph, or just one sentence in it?</p>

  <h2>Beware Headings That Are Too Similar</h2>
  <p>Examiners often include two headings that look almost the same, for example "The benefits of remote work" and "The challenges of remote work." When you spot near-identical options, slow down and read the paragraph for its overall stance. A paragraph that lists problems belongs to "challenges," even if it briefly acknowledges a benefit. The distinction is usually a single evaluative word, positive or negative, so read for tone, not just topic.</p>

  <h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Reading the headings first.</strong> This primes you to keyword-match and fall for detail traps. Read the paragraph first, form your own summary, then choose.</li>
    <li><strong>Matching a single word.</strong> A shared word between heading and paragraph proves nothing. The heading must summarise the whole paragraph.</li>
    <li><strong>Assuming the questions are in order.</strong> They are not. Any heading can match any paragraph.</li>
    <li><strong>Spending too long on one paragraph.</strong> Skip it, match the easier ones, and return with fewer headings left to choose from.</li>
    <li><strong>Leaving blanks.</strong> There is no penalty for wrong answers, so if you are unsure, use one of your remaining headings rather than leaving a gap.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>A Quick Worked Example</h2>
  <p>Suppose a paragraph reads: <em>"Although the festival began as a small village gathering, it now draws visitors from across the country. Local businesses report their busiest weekend of the year, and hotels are booked out months in advance."</em></p>
  <ul>
    <li>Heading: "The origins of the festival" → tempting, because the paragraph mentions its village beginnings, but that is one clause, not the main idea. <strong>Wrong.</strong></li>
    <li>Heading: "The festival's growing economic impact" → captures what the whole paragraph is about: more visitors, busier businesses, full hotels. <strong>Correct.</strong></li>
  </ul>
  <p>The detail heading is designed to catch anyone who keyword-matched the word "began." The main-idea heading wins.</p>
