  <p>Form completion is almost always the first thing you meet in IELTS Listening: a booking form, a membership application, an insurance claim, filled in during a phone call between two speakers. The vocabulary is everyday, the pace is gentle, and every mark counts exactly as much as a mark from the hardest academic section — which makes this the best value real estate on the test. It is also where well-prepared candidates leak marks with strange reliability, because Part 1's difficulty is not the language: it is a set of engineered traps that recur test after test. This guide is a field manual for those traps.</p>

  <h2>The ground rules</h2>
  <p>You hear the recording <strong>once</strong>. Before each section you get time to read the questions — use it to study the form: the headings tell you the scenario, and each gap's label tells you what is coming (a name, a date, an amount). Check the word limit — usually "ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER" or "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER" — and hold it in mind, because an over-length answer is a zero regardless of content. Predict each gap's type before the audio starts: knowing that question 3 wants a phone number and question 4 wants a street name means you are listening <em>for</em> something, not just listening.</p>

  <h2>Trap 1: the correction</h2>
  <p>The signature Part 1 trap. A speaker gives a piece of information, then changes it:</p>
  <p><em>"Shall we say Tuesday the 4th? — Actually, could we make it the 6th? I'm away on the 4th."</em></p>
  <p>The answer is the 6th, and the recording relies on you writing the 4th and moving on. The defence is knowing the trigger words: <strong>actually, sorry, no wait, on second thought, I'm afraid that's changed, let me just check…</strong> When you hear one, your pencil goes back to the gap you just filled. Corrections cluster around exactly the details forms collect — dates, times, prices, quantities — and a typical Part 1 contains at least one. Treat every early answer as provisional until the speakers move to a new topic.</p>

  <h2>Trap 2: the spelled-out name</h2>
  <p>Names and postcodes are dictated letter by letter — <em>"That's Kearsley, K-E-A-R-S-L-E-Y"</em> — and they are only easy if your alphabet recognition is automatic under speed. The classic confusions for many language backgrounds: <strong>A/E/I</strong>, <strong>G/J</strong>, <strong>B/V/P</strong>, <strong>M/N</strong>, and <strong>double letters</strong> ("double T") which some candidates write once. There is no strategy here, only training: get a friend or an app to dictate ten random surnames and postcodes daily for a week, at speed. A misspelled answer scores nothing, and unlike Reading, you cannot look anywhere to check — what you catch in the air is all you get.</p>

  <h2>Trap 3: numbers built to be misheard</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>-teen vs -ty:</strong> thirteen/thirty, fifteen/fifty. The stress is your clue — thir<strong>teen</strong> stresses the second syllable, <strong>thir</strong>ty the first. Drill the pairs until the stress pattern, not the vowel, is what you hear.</li>
    <li><strong>Phone numbers:</strong> read in rhythmic groups, with "oh" for zero and "double four" for 44. Write digits as they come; never try to hold a full number in memory.</li>
    <li><strong>Prices:</strong> listen for the correction pattern ("it's normally £45, but with the student discount that comes down to £38") — the form almost always wants the final figure, but read the gap's label: "standard price" and "price paid" are different questions.</li>
    <li><strong>Dates:</strong> "the 21st of June" may need to fit a gap as "21 June" — either standard written form scores, but a rambling transcription may break the word limit.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Trap 4: the plausible decoy before the answer</h2>
  <p>Forms ask for one detail; conversations mention several. Asked for the caller's <em>daytime</em> number, the caller gives home first, then mobile. Asked for the street, the speaker mentions the street she used to live on. The gap's label is your contract — <strong>read the words around the gap precisely</strong>, because they are chosen to disambiguate exactly this. "Name of <em>agent</em>" means the other name you hear is the decoy.</p>

  <h2>Trap 5: the paraphrased label</h2>
  <p>Even in Part 1, the form's wording rarely matches the audio's wording. The form says "occupation"; the audio says "And what do you do for a living?" The form says "date of arrival"; the audio says "when will you be getting here?" Candidates waiting to hear the label's exact words miss the answer entirely. During preview, glance at each label and ask: how would a human actually say this on the phone?</p>

  <h2>Keeping your place — the quiet killer</h2>
  <p>The most expensive Part 1 error is not mishearing a word; it is losing your place after a missed answer and unravelling three more questions while you panic. The rule: <strong>a missed gap costs one mark only if you abandon it instantly.</strong> Put a dash, move your eyes to the next gap, and re-anchor on its label. At the end of the test you will transfer answers (or review them, on the computer-delivered version) — an intelligent guess in a dashed gap costs nothing and sometimes pays.</p>

  <h2>A practice routine that fixes Part 1</h2>
  <ol>
    <li>Do a form completion set cold and mark it. Do not stop there.</li>
    <li>Classify every error against this guide's traps: correction missed, spelling, number pair, decoy, paraphrased label, lost place. The classification is the diagnosis.</li>
    <li>Replay the audio for each miss and find the exact second the answer — and the trap — occurred. Hearing the machinery once is worth ten untargeted practice tests.</li>
    <li>Drill your weakest trap in isolation for a week (dictated spellings, -teen/-ty pairs), then retest with a fresh set from our <a href="/listeningquestion">Listening practice</a> bank.</li>
  </ol>
