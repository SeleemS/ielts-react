  <p>Getting a result below your target is disheartening, but a retake is not a gamble — it is a project with a clear starting point. The candidates who improve on the second attempt are rarely the ones who simply "study harder." They are the ones who read their result carefully, work out which single skill is costing them, and rehearse the exam conditions until the gap closes. This guide shows you how to turn a disappointing Test Report Form into a focused plan for a higher band.</p>

  <h2>First, understand why you can retake as often as you like</h2>
  <p>There is <strong>no limit</strong> on how many times you can sit IELTS, and no compulsory waiting period between attempts — you can rebook as soon as a slot is available. You also do not have to resit every skill: the <strong>One Skill Retake</strong> option, available for computer-delivered tests at many centres, lets you retake just one of the four components and keep your other three scores. If three of your bands are already at target and only one is short, this can be faster and cheaper than a full resit. Check whether the organisations you are applying to accept a One Skill Retake result before you book it, because not all do.</p>

  <h2>Read your Test Report Form like a diagnosis</h2>
  <p>Your overall band is the average of the four skills, rounded to the nearest half band — a .25 rounds up to the next half and a .75 rounds up to the next whole band. That rounding rule is the first thing to check, because it often means you are closer than you think. If your four scores are 6.5, 6.5, 6.0 and 6.5, your average is 6.375, which rounds to <strong>6.5</strong>. Raising that single 6.0 to a 6.5 would push the average to 6.5 exactly — no change — but raising it to 7.0 would give an average of 6.625, which rounds to <strong>7.0</strong>. Small movements on the right skill can jump your overall band; the same effort on a skill that is already strong may do nothing. Put your four scores into the free <a href="/band-calculator">IELTS band score calculator</a> and test which skill, lifted by half or one band, actually changes your overall result.</p>

  <h2>Target the skill with the most room, not your favourite</h2>
  <p>Most candidates re-prepare by doing more of what they already enjoy — usually Reading or Listening, where progress feels measurable. But if those are your strong skills, extra practice there is wasted. Rank your four bands and be honest about which one is holding your average down, then spend the majority of your preparation on it.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>If Writing is lowest,</strong> it is almost always Task Response or Coherence and Cohesion, not vocabulary. Rewrite your weakest essays one criterion at a time, and get a criterion-by-criterion estimate from the free <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">AI IELTS essay checker</a> so you can see which of the four bands is dragging you down.</li>
    <li><strong>If Reading is lowest,</strong> identify the question types you lose most marks on — usually <a href="/reading/true-false-not-given">True/False/Not Given</a> and matching headings — and drill them in isolation before returning to full passages.</li>
    <li><strong>If Listening is lowest,</strong> review with the transcript open and note whether you are mishearing, mis-spelling, or losing your place; each needs a different fix.</li>
    <li><strong>If Speaking is lowest,</strong> record yourself and listen for the specific problem — hesitation, short answers, or repeated grammar slips — rather than practising "speaking" in general.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Why the same score keeps coming back</h2>
  <p>If you retook today with the same preparation, you would most likely score the same band, because your method — not your effort — set your ceiling last time. Three patterns cause repeat results:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>You never rehearsed the real conditions.</strong> Practising untimed, in pieces, on different days, does not prepare you for a three-hour sitting. Do at least two full, timed mock papers before you rebook.</li>
    <li><strong>You practised without reviewing.</strong> Doing question after question without checking <em>why</em> each answer was wrong teaches nothing. The review is where the band moves.</li>
    <li><strong>You misread the task.</strong> In Writing especially, answering the wrong question — describing problems when asked for causes, or forgetting to give an opinion — caps Task Response no matter how good your English is.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Consider requesting a remark before you resit</h2>
  <p>If you were unexpectedly close on one skill — for example, you felt your Writing or Speaking should have been half a band higher — you can apply for an <strong>Enquiry on Results</strong> (a remark) within six weeks of the test. A senior examiner re-marks the component, and if your score goes up you receive a refund of the fee. A remark is most worth considering for the two productive skills, Writing and Speaking, where marking involves more judgement than the objective Reading and Listening papers. It is often cheaper and quicker than a full resit, though there is no guarantee the score will change.</p>

  <h2>A four-week retake plan</h2>
  <p>Once you know your weakest skill, structure the run-up rather than drifting:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Week 1 — technique.</strong> Relearn the method for your weakest skill, slowly and untimed. Understand your errors before you try to fix them at speed.</li>
    <li><strong>Week 2 — timed sections.</strong> Add the clock to that skill, and review each attempt for twice as long as it took to complete.</li>
    <li><strong>Week 3 — full papers.</strong> Sit complete timed papers to build stamina and find where your concentration drops.</li>
    <li><strong>Week 4 — taper and confirm.</strong> One full mock early in the week, then reduce volume and revisit your error log. Rebook only when two separate mocks land at or above your target.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>For a fuller day-by-day version of this, follow our <a href="/blog/ielts-30-day-study-plan">30-day IELTS study plan</a>, adapted so that most of the daily time goes to the one skill your report identifies.</p>

  <h2>Know you are ready before you pay again</h2>
  <p>The test of readiness is not a good feeling — it is two timed mock papers at or above your target, on different days, with your weakest skill no longer the outlier. Drill the specific weakness on the <a href="/readingquestion">Reading</a>, <a href="/listeningquestion">Listening</a>, <a href="/writingquestion">Writing</a>, and <a href="/speakingquestion">Speaking</a> question banks, retest, and confirm the numbers on the <a href="/band-calculator">band score calculator</a>. A retake built on that evidence is not a gamble — it is the natural next step once your practice scores have already moved.</p>
