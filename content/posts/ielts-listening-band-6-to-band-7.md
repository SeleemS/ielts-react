  <p>Plenty of candidates understand almost everything they hear in <strong>IELTS Listening</strong> and still land on Band 6. The problem is rarely comprehension — it is losing a mark here and there to a spelling slip, a distractor, a missed word limit, or one gap that throws off the next three answers. This guide shows you exactly what separates a Band 6 from a Band 7 and the habits that close the gap.</p>

  <h2>What Band 6 and Band 7 Actually Mean</h2>
  <p>The Listening test has <strong>40 questions across four sections</strong>, and the recording is played <strong>once only</strong>. Your raw score out of 40 is converted to a band using published indicative boundaries. Roughly, <strong>around 23 correct answers gives Band 6, and around 30 gives Band 7</strong>. So the jump you are chasing is only about <strong>seven extra correct answers</strong> — usually the ones you are currently losing to avoidable mistakes rather than to difficult listening.</p>
  <p>The same test and the same scale apply to both <strong>Academic and General Training</strong> candidates. If you are unsure how a Listening band feeds into your overall result, try our free <a href="/band-calculator">IELTS band score calculator</a>.</p>

  <h2>Predict Before You Listen</h2>
  <p>The single biggest Band 6 habit is reading the questions passively and waiting for answers to appear. Band 7 candidates use every spare second to <strong>predict</strong>. Before each section starts you are given time to read the questions — use it to ask:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>What type of word is missing?</strong> A number, a name, a date, a noun, a plural?</li>
    <li><strong>What is the word limit?</strong> "No more than two words" means three words scores zero, even if they are correct.</li>
    <li><strong>What might I hear?</strong> Underline keywords in each question so your ear is primed for them or their synonyms.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The audio almost never uses the exact words on the page. If the question says "the class starts at," you might hear "we kick off at half past six." Predicting the answer <em>type</em> keeps you ready when the wording changes.</p>

  <h2>Listen for Corrections and Distractors</h2>
  <p>Test writers deliberately plant wrong answers first and correct them a moment later. You might hear "It's on Bridge Road — sorry, no, Bridge <em>Street</em>," or "Tuesdays… actually, we've moved it to Thursdays." Band 6 candidates write the first thing they hear; Band 7 candidates listen to the <strong>whole sentence</strong> and stay alert to signposts like <em>but, actually, in fact, sorry,</em> and <em>instead</em>. In multiple-choice questions especially, all the options are usually mentioned — only one answers the exact question asked.</p>

  <h2>Never Leave a Blank</h2>
  <p>There is <strong>no penalty for wrong answers</strong> in IELTS, so an empty box is a wasted chance. If you miss an answer, write your best guess from context and move on immediately — the worst mistake is freezing on a missed gap and then losing the next two answers while you panic. Keep pace with the recording at all costs.</p>

  <h2>Protect Marks on Spelling and Format</h2>
  <p>These are the marks Band 6 candidates lose without realising:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Spelling counts.</strong> A correctly heard word spelled wrong is marked wrong. Drill commonly tested words — <em>accommodation, Wednesday, restaurant, February</em> — and learn to write down names as they are spelled out letter by letter.</li>
    <li><strong>Numbers and dates.</strong> Know how dates can be written and how prices, phone numbers, and postcodes are said aloud.</li>
    <li><strong>Word limits.</strong> Re-read the instruction for every completion task. Exceeding the limit is an automatic zero.</li>
    <li><strong>Both capitals and lower case are accepted</strong>, so writing in ALL CAPITALS is a safe way to keep proper nouns clear and legible.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>A Section-by-Section Plan</h2>
  <p>The four sections rise in difficulty, and each rewards a slightly different focus:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Section 1</strong> — an everyday conversation, usually a form to complete. Aim for full marks here: it is mostly names, numbers, and spellings. Losing marks in Section 1 is what caps many scores at Band 6.</li>
    <li><strong>Section 2</strong> — a monologue on a social topic, often with a map or matching task. Track direction words and the order information is given.</li>
    <li><strong>Section 3</strong> — a discussion between two to four speakers, often about study. Follow <em>who</em> holds each opinion, as questions frequently ask which speaker said what.</li>
    <li><strong>Section 4</strong> — an academic lecture with <strong>no break in the middle</strong>. There is no pause to catch up, so prediction matters most. See our dedicated guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-section-4-strategy">Section 4 strategy</a>.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Practise the Right Way</h2>
  <p>Passive listening to podcasts helps your general ear, but it will not move your band. To gain those seven marks, practise under <strong>exam conditions</strong>: play the audio once, answer in real time, then mark strictly. Crucially, review every wrong answer and label <em>why</em> you lost it — misheard, mis-spelled, wrong word count, or fell for a distractor. Patterns will emerge, and fixing your two or three most common errors is the fastest route from Band 6 to Band 7.</p>

Close the small leaks, and Band 7 is well within reach.</p>
