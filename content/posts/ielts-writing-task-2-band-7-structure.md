  <p>Most candidates lose marks in <strong>IELTS Writing Task 2</strong> not because their English is weak, but because their essay has no clear shape. Examiners reward answers that are easy to follow, fully developed, and directly focused on the question. The good news is that a single, flexible structure works for almost every task type, and once you internalise it you can stop worrying about organisation and put your energy into ideas and language.</p>

  <h2>How Task 2 Is Marked</h2>
  <p>Your essay is scored on four equally weighted criteria, each worth 25% of your Writing Task 2 band:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Task Response:</strong> Do you answer every part of the question with a clear position and developed ideas?</li>
    <li><strong>Coherence and Cohesion:</strong> Is your essay logically organised into paragraphs with smooth linking?</li>
    <li><strong>Lexical Resource:</strong> Do you use a range of vocabulary accurately and appropriately?</li>
    <li><strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy:</strong> Do you use a mix of sentence structures with few errors?</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Note that Task 2 is worth roughly twice as much as Task 1 in your overall Writing score, so it deserves the larger share of your 60 minutes: aim to spend about 40 minutes on it.</p>

  <h2>The Four-Paragraph Structure</h2>
  <p>For a 250-word essay, four paragraphs is the sweet spot. It gives you enough room to develop ideas fully without drifting off topic.</p>

  <h3>1. Introduction (2 to 3 sentences)</h3>
  <p>Paraphrase the question in your own words, then state your position or outline what the essay will cover. Do not copy the prompt word for word: repeated wording is not counted toward your word total and signals a limited vocabulary. A strong introduction answers the question immediately so the examiner knows exactly where you stand.</p>

  <h3>2 and 3. Two Body Paragraphs</h3>
  <p>Each body paragraph should contain one central idea, introduced by a clear topic sentence. Then explain that idea and support it with a specific example or a logical explanation. A reliable pattern is:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Point</strong> — state the main idea of the paragraph.</li>
    <li><strong>Explain</strong> — say why it is true or how it works.</li>
    <li><strong>Example</strong> — give a concrete illustration.</li>
    <li><strong>Link</strong> — connect it back to the question.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Depth beats breadth. Two well-developed ideas score higher than five undeveloped ones.</p>

  <h3>4. Conclusion (2 sentences)</h3>
  <p>Restate your position and summarise your main points. Do not introduce new ideas here. A conclusion that clearly mirrors your introduction gives the essay a satisfying, complete feel.</p>

  <h2>Language That Lifts Your Band</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Use a variety of linking devices (<em>however</em>, <em>as a result</em>, <em>for instance</em>) but do not overload every sentence with them.</li>
    <li>Show grammatical range with conditionals, relative clauses, and complex sentences, not just simple statements.</li>
    <li>Prefer precise, topic-specific vocabulary over memorised "big words" used incorrectly.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Practise Under Timed Conditions</h2>
  <p>Structure only becomes automatic through repetition. Write full essays in 40 minutes, then compare them against model answers to see where your development or cohesion falls short. You can find authentic prompts to practise on our <a href="/writingquestion">IELTS Writing Questions</a> page, and if you are aiming higher still, our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-band-9-strategies">realistic Band 9 strategies</a> breaks down what separates a 7 from a 9.</p>

  <p>Master this one structure, drill it until it feels natural, and you remove organisation as a source of lost marks, freeing you to focus on the ideas and language that push you into Band 7 and beyond.</p>
