  <p>IELTS Speaking Part 1 lasts four to five minutes and covers familiar topics: your home, your work or studies, and two or three everyday subjects such as food, weather, music or transport. It feels like small talk, so candidates treat it like small talk — and lose marks before the harder parts even begin. The single most common problem is answer length. This guide fixes that with a formula you can apply to any question.</p>

  <h2>What Part 1 is actually testing</h2>
  <p>Every part of the Speaking test is marked on the same four criteria, each worth 25% of your Speaking band: <strong>Fluency and Coherence</strong>, <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong>, and <strong>Pronunciation</strong>. Part 1 is not a warm-up that gets discounted. It is a full quarter of your speaking time, and the examiner is scoring it.</p>
  <p>What Part 1 rewards is the ability to speak naturally and continuously about ordinary things without hesitating or padding. It is not testing sophisticated opinions — save those for <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-3-discussion">Part 3</a>.</p>

  <h2>The length problem</h2>
  <p>Two failure modes dominate.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Too short.</strong> "Do you like cooking?" — "Yes, I do." The examiner has to ask another question immediately. You have given almost no language to assess, and repeated one-word answers make the whole test feel disjointed.</li>
    <li><strong>Too long.</strong> A ninety-second speech about your grandmother's kitchen. The examiner will interrupt you, which breaks your rhythm, and you will get through fewer questions — meaning fewer chances to show range.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The target is <strong>two to three sentences, roughly 15 to 30 seconds</strong>. Long enough to show grammar and vocabulary, short enough that the conversation keeps moving.</p>

  <h2>The formula: Answer + Reason + Detail</h2>
  <p>For almost any Part 1 question, use three moves:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Answer</strong> the question directly in the first few words.</li>
    <li><strong>Give a reason</strong> — because, since, the main reason is.</li>
    <li><strong>Add one detail</strong> — an example, a contrast, or a time reference.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Then stop. The detail sentence is your natural finish line.</p>

  <h3>Worked examples</h3>
  <p><strong>Q: Do you enjoy cooking?</strong></p>
  <p>"Not really, to be honest. I find it quite time-consuming after a long day at work, so I tend to keep meals very simple. That said, I do quite enjoy cooking at weekends when I'm not in a rush."</p>
  <p><strong>Q: Is the weather important to you?</strong></p>
  <p>"Yes, more than I'd like to admit. Grey skies definitely affect my mood and I get much less done on those days. In summer I'm far more likely to go out and see friends."</p>
  <p><strong>Q: Do you prefer to travel by bus or by train?</strong></p>
  <p>"Train, without a doubt. It's a lot more comfortable and I can actually get some reading done, whereas on a bus I feel sick if I look at a page. The only downside is that tickets are considerably more expensive."</p>
  <p>Notice that none of these answers are impressive opinions. They are ordinary thoughts expressed with linking words, a range of tenses, and a few natural phrases.</p>

  <h2>Handling "no" and "I don't know"</h2>
  <p>You are never marked on the truth of your answers, only on the language. If a question lands on something you have no experience of, you have two options.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Answer honestly, then pivot.</strong> "I've never really been into sport, actually. I was never picked for teams at school and I think that put me off. My brother's the opposite — he plays football every weekend."</li>
    <li><strong>Invent something plausible.</strong> Perfectly acceptable. A believable made-up detail beats a genuine silence.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>What you must avoid is "I don't know" as a full stop. It gives the examiner nothing.</p>

  <h2>Five mistakes that flatten your band</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Repeating the question.</strong> "Do I like cooking? Well, do I like cooking..." — this reads as stalling and hurts Fluency.</li>
    <li><strong>Memorised openers.</strong> "That's a very interesting question" on every answer is instantly recognisable to examiners and adds nothing.</li>
    <li><strong>Using only the present simple.</strong> Part 1 questions invite past habits ("I used to..."), present habits, and future plans. Mixing tenses naturally is free marks under Grammatical Range.</li>
    <li><strong>Over-formal vocabulary.</strong> "I derive considerable satisfaction from culinary activities" sounds rehearsed. Natural collocations — "I'm not really into it", "it's a bit of a chore" — score better.</li>
    <li><strong>Trailing off.</strong> Ending with "...and so on, yeah, things like that" signals you have run out of ideas. Finish on your detail sentence and stop confidently.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>How to practise this week</h2>
  <p>Pick ten familiar-topic questions and record yourself answering each one. Then check three things: Did every answer have all three moves? Did any run past 30 seconds? Did you use more than one tense? Ten minutes a day for a week will change your default answer shape permanently.</p>

  <h2>The bottom line</h2>
  <p>Part 1 is the easiest place in the whole test to gain marks, because the topics are predictable and the language required is ordinary. Answer directly, give a reason, add one detail, then stop. Do that consistently for four minutes and you walk into Part 2 having already shown the examiner fluent, controlled, natural English.</p>
