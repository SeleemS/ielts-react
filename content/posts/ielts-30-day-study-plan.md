date: July 24, 2026
excerpt: A realistic four-week IELTS study plan that builds skills, then speed, then exam stamina. Includes a daily time budget and how to know if you are ready.
---

  <p>Most people who "study for a month" and score below their target did not study badly — they studied without a plan. They did a bit of everything every day, never finished a full timed section, and walked into the exam having never rehearsed the actual conditions. A <strong>30-day IELTS study plan</strong> fixes that by giving each week a single job: build the skills, then build speed, then build exam stamina, then peak. This is a schedule you can start today with roughly one to two focused hours a day.</p>

  <h2>Before day 1: get a baseline</h2>
  <p>You cannot plan a route without knowing your starting point. Spend your first session taking one timed Reading section and one Listening section, and writing one Task 2 essay under the clock. Then be honest about the numbers. Reading is 40 questions in 60 minutes and Listening is 40 questions across four sections in about 30 minutes, so a raw score out of 40 tells you exactly where you stand. Convert those raw scores with the free <a href="/band-calculator">IELTS band score calculator</a>, and run the essay through the <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">AI Writing Checker</a> for a criterion-by-criterion estimate. Write the four bands down. This is the only way to tell, on day 30, whether the month worked.</p>

  <h2>Week 1: Fix technique, one question type at a time</h2>
  <p>Speed is useless if your method is wrong, so week one is slow and deliberate. Do <em>not</em> time yourself yet. Instead, take one question type each day and learn how it actually works.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Reading:</strong> spend two or three days on the types that cost you most. For most people that is <a href="/reading/true-false-not-given">True/False/Not Given</a> and matching headings, because they punish guessing. Do sets untimed and check the exact line in the passage that proves each answer.</li>
    <li><strong>Listening:</strong> drill form and note completion, then map labelling. Always review with the transcript open so you can see the phrase that gave the answer.</li>
    <li><strong>Writing:</strong> learn the shape of one Task 2 essay type using our guide to the <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-band-7-structure">Band 7 essay structure</a>, and write one body paragraph a day — not whole essays yet.</li>
    <li><strong>Speaking:</strong> record two-minute answers to Part 2 cue cards and listen back for pauses and repetition.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>By the end of week one you should understand <em>why</em> you get questions wrong, not just that you do.</p>

  <h2>Week 2: Add the clock</h2>
  <p>Now apply time pressure to the technique you built. Each day, do one full timed section and then review it for twice as long as it took to complete. The review is where the learning happens — the section itself only diagnoses.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Reading:</strong> practise pacing so no single passage eats the time meant for the next. There is no extra transfer time in Reading, so answers must be on the sheet when the hour ends.</li>
    <li><strong>Listening:</strong> the audio plays once and never stops, so rehearse letting a missed answer go and catching the next one instead of freezing.</li>
    <li><strong>Writing:</strong> write one complete essay in 40 minutes, then one Task 1 in 20. Check each against the four criteria — Task Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy — rather than your own impression.</li>
    <li><strong>Speaking:</strong> practise the full three-part format so you get used to the two-minute long turn and the abstract Part 3 questions.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Keep a one-line error log every day: the question type, the mistake, and the fix. Patterns will appear within a week.</p>

  <h2>Week 3: Build stamina with full papers</h2>
  <p>Individual sections do not prepare you for the fatigue of a real sitting. In week three, do complete Listening-plus-Reading blocks back to back, on the same day, at the same time you will sit the real test. Aim for two or three full mock papers this week. The goal is not a perfect score; it is discovering where your concentration drops — usually the third Reading passage or Listening Part 4 — and training through it.</p>
  <p>For Writing, stop writing new essays from scratch every day. Instead, rewrite your weakest essays from week two, fixing the single lowest criterion each time. Rewriting one paragraph until it is genuinely strong teaches more than drafting five mediocre new ones.</p>

  <h2>Week 4: Simulate, taper, and target</h2>
  <p>The final week is about peaking, not cramming. Do one complete four-skill mock early in the week under full exam conditions — correct timing, no phone, no pauses. Convert the raw scores on the <a href="/band-calculator">band calculator</a> and compare them with your day-one baseline. That gap is your progress.</p>
  <p>Then taper. In the last three days, reduce volume and revisit only your notes and error log. Cramming new material now raises anxiety without raising your band. Confirm the logistics instead: know your test format, the reporting requirement you need, and remember that your overall band is the average of the four skills rounded to the nearest half band (a .25 rounds up to the next half, a .75 up to the next whole band). One weak skill can pull the average down, so if three skills are strong and one is a band behind, the last few days are best spent there.</p>

  <h2>A realistic daily budget</h2>
  <p>If you have about 90 minutes a day, a workable split is 30 minutes on your weakest skill, 30 on a timed section, and 30 on review and your error log. If you have more time, add a second skill rather than doubling the same one — variety keeps concentration fresh and mirrors the real paper, which switches skills constantly.</p>

  <h2>How to know you are ready</h2>
  <p>You are ready when your mock scores land at or above your target on two separate papers, not one lucky attempt, and when you finish each section inside the time limit without rushing the last few questions. If you are close but not there, it is usually one identifiable skill holding you back. Practise it specifically on the <a href="/readingquestion">Reading</a>, <a href="/listeningquestion">Listening</a>, <a href="/writingquestion">Writing</a>, and <a href="/speakingquestion">Speaking</a> question banks, retest, and only then book. Thirty focused days beats three unfocused months.</p>
