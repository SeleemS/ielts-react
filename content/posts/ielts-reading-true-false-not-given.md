  <p><strong>True/False/Not Given</strong> is the question type that frustrates IELTS candidates more than any other. The reading itself feels manageable, yet the marks slip away because one category, <strong>Not Given</strong>, forces you to prove a negative. Most people lose points here not through weak English but through a flawed decision process. This guide gives you a reliable, repeatable method so you stop guessing and start choosing the correct answer on purpose.</p>

  <h2>What the Three Answers Actually Mean</h2>
  <p>These questions test whether a statement agrees with information in the passage. There are only three possible answers, and getting the definitions exactly right is half the battle:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>True:</strong> the statement agrees with the information in the text. The passage confirms it, even if the wording is different.</li>
    <li><strong>False:</strong> the statement contradicts the information in the text. The passage says the opposite, or something that cannot both be true.</li>
    <li><strong>Not Given:</strong> there is no information in the text to confirm or contradict the statement. The passage is simply silent on it.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The entire difficulty lives in the gap between False and Not Given. <strong>False</strong> means the text actively disagrees with the statement. <strong>Not Given</strong> means the text neither agrees nor disagrees, the information is absent. If you can separate "the text contradicts this" from "the text says nothing about this," you have solved 90% of the problem.</p>

  <h2>True/False/Not Given vs Yes/No/Not Given</h2>
  <p>You will also meet a close cousin, <strong>Yes/No/Not Given</strong>. The mechanics are identical, but the target is different. True/False/Not Given deals with <strong>facts and information</strong> in the passage, while Yes/No/Not Given deals with the <strong>writer's opinions, claims, or views</strong>. Whenever a question set asks about what the author <em>believes</em> or <em>argues</em>, expect the Yes/No version. The four-step method below works for both.</p>

  <h2>A Four-Step Method That Works Every Time</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Read the statement first and find the keywords.</strong> Underline the names, dates, numbers, and any word that carries the core meaning. Identify what the statement is actually claiming before you touch the passage.</li>
    <li><strong>Locate the matching part of the text.</strong> These questions follow the order of the passage, so answer three sits below answer two. Scan for your keywords or their synonyms rather than re-reading everything.</li>
    <li><strong>Compare meaning, not words.</strong> The test rewrites ideas with synonyms and paraphrase. Ask a single question: does the text <em>confirm</em> this (True), <em>contradict</em> this (False), or <em>say nothing</em> about it (Not Given)?</li>
    <li><strong>Decide and move on.</strong> If you have scanned the relevant section and the specific claim is simply not addressed, the answer is Not Given. Do not keep hunting, absence of evidence <em>is</em> the evidence.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Watch the Qualifying Words</h2>
  <p>A statement often turns on one small word. Pay close attention to:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Quantifiers:</strong> all, some, most, none, every, few. If the text says "some scientists" and the statement says "all scientists," that is <strong>False</strong>, not Not Given.</li>
    <li><strong>Frequency:</strong> always, usually, sometimes, never, occasionally.</li>
    <li><strong>Certainty:</strong> will, might, could, definitely, possibly. A statement claiming certainty when the text only suggests possibility is a contradiction.</li>
    <li><strong>Comparatives:</strong> more, less, the most, the biggest. A reversed comparison ("X is larger than Y" when the text says Y is larger) is <strong>False</strong>.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The Traps That Cost Marks</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Using outside knowledge.</strong> Answer only from the passage. A statement can be true in real life but Not Given because the text never mentions it.</li>
    <li><strong>Confusing False with Not Given.</strong> Ask yourself: can I point to the exact words that contradict this? If yes, it is False. If you are inferring or assuming, it is probably Not Given.</li>
    <li><strong>Over-thinking.</strong> The answer is based on what the text plainly says, not on clever deductions. If your reasoning needs three logical leaps, you have gone too far.</li>
    <li><strong>Leaving blanks.</strong> There is no penalty for wrong answers, so every statement must have an answer. If you are stuck between two, choose and move on.</li>
    <li><strong>Changing correct answers.</strong> Your first careful reading is usually right. Only change an answer if you find clear textual evidence.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>A Quick Worked Example</h2>
  <p>Suppose the passage states: <em>"The museum, which opened in 1998, attracts around 200,000 visitors annually."</em></p>
  <ul>
    <li>Statement: "The museum opened in the 1990s." → <strong>True</strong> (1998 confirms it).</li>
    <li>Statement: "The museum receives fewer than 100,000 visitors a year." → <strong>False</strong> (200,000 contradicts it).</li>
    <li>Statement: "The museum is the most popular in the city." → <strong>Not Given</strong> (the text never compares it to others).</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Notice how the third statement sounds plausible but is never addressed. That is the essence of Not Given.</p>
