  <p>Most IELTS Academic Task 1 preparation is built around charts and graphs, so when a <strong>process diagram</strong> appears on test day, candidates who have only practised describing numbers freeze. The good news is that a process is often <em>easier</em> to score well on than a bar chart, because there are no figures to read, no trends to compare, and no risk of misreporting data. What it demands instead is a different toolkit: the passive voice, sequencing language, and the discipline to describe stages in the right order. This guide gives you a reliable structure and a model answer you can adapt to any process you are shown.</p>

  <h2>What a process diagram asks you to do</h2>
  <p>In Academic Writing Task 1 you have about <strong>20 minutes</strong> and a <strong>150-word minimum</strong> to describe the visual you are given. A process diagram shows how something happens or is made, as a series of connected stages with arrows. There are two broad kinds, and spotting which you have shapes your grammar:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>A man-made or manufacturing process</strong> — how a product is made or a system works, such as how cement is produced, how paper is recycled, or how water is purified. These use the <strong>passive voice</strong> because a human or machine performs each step.</li>
    <li><strong>A natural process or life cycle</strong> — how something occurs in nature, such as the life cycle of a frog, the water cycle, or how a volcano forms. These often use the <strong>active voice</strong> in the present simple, because nature is the "doer" ("the eggs hatch," "the water evaporates").</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Either way, you are writing a factual report of the stages in sequence. You never explain <em>why</em> the process happens or whether it is good — Task 1 gives no marks for opinion.</p>

  <h2>How a process is marked</h2>
  <p>The four criteria are the same as for any Task 1, each worth a quarter of your Task 1 band:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Task Achievement:</strong> Do you include an overview, cover every stage, and describe them in the correct order?</li>
    <li><strong>Coherence and Cohesion:</strong> Do the stages flow with clear sequencing language?</li>
    <li><strong>Lexical Resource:</strong> Can you paraphrase the labels and vary your linking words?</li>
    <li><strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy:</strong> Can you handle the passive voice accurately across many sentences?</li>
  </ul>
  <p>As with charts, the single most common reason candidates stall at Band 5 or 6 is a missing <strong>overview</strong>. On a process, the overview is not about high and low values — it states how many stages there are and whether the process is a cycle or has a clear start and end.</p>

  <h2>A four-paragraph structure that always works</h2>
  <h3>1. Introduction (1 sentence)</h3>
  <p>Paraphrase the prompt rather than copying it. If the task says "The diagram below shows how coffee is produced," you might write: "The diagram illustrates the various stages involved in the production of coffee, from harvesting to packaging." Swap <em>shows</em> for <em>illustrates</em>, <em>depicts</em>, or <em>demonstrates</em>, and reword the rest.</p>
  <h3>2. Overview (1-2 sentences)</h3>
  <p>State the big picture without detail: the number of stages and the overall shape. Begin with <em>Overall</em>. For example: "Overall, the process consists of eight distinct steps, beginning with the harvesting of the beans and ending with the finished product being shipped to retailers. It is a linear process that requires no raw materials beyond the beans themselves." For a life cycle, say instead that it is a continuous cycle with no clear beginning or end.</p>
  <h3>3 and 4. Two body paragraphs (the stages)</h3>
  <p>Split the stages into two logical groups — for example, the first half of the process in one paragraph and the second half in the other. Describe each stage in order, one flowing into the next, and paraphrase the labels on the diagram rather than copying them word for word.</p>

  <h2>The grammar that carries a process answer</h2>
  <p>Two structures do most of the work, and drilling them is the fastest way to lift a process report.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>The passive voice</strong> for man-made processes: <em>the beans are picked</em>, <em>the mixture is heated</em>, <em>the paper is then pulped</em>. Form it with <em>is/are</em> plus the past participle. This is where accuracy marks are won or lost, so practise it until it is automatic.</li>
    <li><strong>Sequencers</strong> to show order and avoid a flat list: <em>first</em>, <em>initially</em>, <em>next</em>, <em>after that</em>, <em>subsequently</em>, <em>once this is complete</em>, <em>finally</em>. Vary them — repeating "then" ten times caps your Coherence and Cohesion.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>You can also show range by combining two steps into one sentence with <em>before</em>, <em>after</em>, or <em>where</em>: "Once the beans have been dried, they are roasted at a high temperature, after which they are ground into powder." Mixing single-step and combined sentences signals grammatical range without any risk of error from numbers.</p>

  <h2>A short model extract</h2>
  <p><strong>Prompt:</strong> The diagram shows how bricks are manufactured.</p>
  <p><strong>Introduction and overview:</strong> "The diagram illustrates the process by which bricks are produced for the building industry. Overall, there are seven stages, beginning with the digging of clay and ending with delivery, and the process is linear from start to finish."</p>
  <p><strong>Body extract:</strong> "First, the clay is dug from the ground using a large digger and placed onto a metal grid, where it is broken into smaller pieces. Next, sand and water are added, and the mixture is shaped into bricks either by a mould or by a wire cutter. After this, the bricks are left to dry in an oven for one to two days before being fired at a high temperature."</p>
  <p>Notice that every action is in the passive voice, the labels are paraphrased ("dug from the ground" rather than a copied caption), and each sentence links to the next with a sequencer.</p>

  <h2>Common mistakes to avoid</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Using trend language.</strong> "Rose," "increased," and "peaked" belong to charts, not processes. A process has no data to go up or down.</li>
    <li><strong>Getting the voice wrong.</strong> "The machine mixes the sand" is weaker than "the sand is mixed" for a man-made process; examiners expect the passive here.</li>
    <li><strong>Skipping the overview.</strong> Without a sentence stating the number of stages and whether it is a cycle, Task Achievement is capped.</li>
    <li><strong>Describing stages out of order.</strong> Follow the arrows exactly; a reordered process confuses the reader and loses coherence marks.</li>
    <li><strong>Copying the labels.</strong> Repeating the diagram's words verbatim earns no credit under Lexical Resource — paraphrase them.</li>
    <li><strong>Writing under 150 words.</strong> You lose marks automatically; aim for roughly 160 to 190, and keep to 20 minutes so Task 2 keeps its 40.</li>
  </ul>
