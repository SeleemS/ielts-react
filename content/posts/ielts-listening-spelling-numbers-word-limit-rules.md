  <p>Here is the cruel thing about IELTS Listening: the marking is not "did you understand?" — it is "did you write the exact answer, spelled correctly, within the word limit?" You can hear the word perfectly and still score zero because you added an "s," misspelled it, or wrote three words when the instruction said two. These are not comprehension failures; they are avoidable clerical errors, and they are the easiest marks on the whole test to throw away. This guide covers every mechanical rule that governs whether your answer is accepted, plus the specific traps that catch strong listeners.</p>

  <h2>How Listening is marked</h2>
  <p>The Listening test has <strong>40 questions across four sections</strong> and lasts about <strong>30 minutes</strong>. On the paper-based test you then get <strong>10 extra minutes</strong> to copy your answers onto the answer sheet; on the computer-delivered test there is no transfer time — you type as you go and get about <strong>2 minutes</strong> at the end to check. Each question is worth one mark, and your raw score out of 40 converts to a band on the 9-band scale. Every question is marked right or wrong with no partial credit, so a single stray letter costs a full mark. Because the four skills are averaged into your overall band, throwing away three or four Listening marks on spelling can pull your whole result down half a band.</p>

  <h2>Spelling must be perfect</h2>
  <p>An answer that is spelled incorrectly is marked <strong>wrong</strong>, full stop. There is no "close enough." If the recording says <em>accommodation</em> and you write <em>accomodation</em>, you lose the mark even though you clearly heard it. This matters most in form-completion and note-completion tasks, where the answers are often ordinary words a candidate could spell — names, days, materials, jobs — that fall apart under time pressure.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Both British and American spellings are accepted.</strong> "Colour" and "color," "organisation" and "organization," "centre" and "center" are all fine. You do not need to match the accent you hear — just pick one correct spelling and stay consistent.</li>
    <li><strong>Names are usually spelled out for you.</strong> When an answer is a surname or a place the test-writers expect you not to know, the speaker spells it letter by letter. Write the letters as they come; do not try to guess the "normal" spelling.</li>
    <li><strong>Watch the letters that sound alike:</strong> "M" and "N," "S" and "F," "B" and "P," "A" and "E," "G" and "J." If a spelling is read fast, these are where errors creep in.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The fix is not last-minute cramming — it is knowing the 40 or 50 words that recur in Listening answers (accommodation, receipt, restaurant, February, Wednesday, jewellery, questionnaire, brochure) and being able to spell them cold.</p>

  <h2>The safest habit: write in CAPITAL LETTERS</h2>
  <p>IELTS accepts answers written entirely in capitals. Writing every answer in <strong>BLOCK CAPITALS</strong> removes any risk of a capitalisation error (for proper nouns like names and places) and makes your handwriting clearer for the examiner. This is the single easiest habit to adopt and it costs you nothing. If you prefer normal case, remember that days, months and place names still need their capital letter — but all-caps sidesteps the whole issue.</p>

  <h2>Numbers, dates and money</h2>
  <p>Numbers are a hidden minefield. A few rules keep you safe:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Figures or words are both accepted</strong> for most numbers: "30" and "thirty" are equally correct. Figures are faster and safer under pressure.</li>
    <li><strong>A number counts within the word limit.</strong> The instruction "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER" means you may write two words plus a number — for example "10 <em>March</em>" or "two <em>bedrooms</em>."</li>
    <li><strong>Dates:</strong> "18 April," "18th April" and "April 18" are all normally accepted. Do not agonise over the format — just get the day and month right.</li>
    <li><strong>Prices:</strong> if the recording says "fifteen pounds fifty," write <em>£15.50</em> or <em>15.50</em> — but check whether the answer box already prints the £ sign, in which case writing it again may be marked wrong.</li>
    <li><strong>Big numbers spoken quickly</strong> ("fifteen" vs "fifty," "thirteen" vs "thirty") are a classic Section 1 trap. Listen for the stress: fif-<em>TEEN</em> stresses the end; <em>FIF</em>-ty stresses the start.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The word limit is not a suggestion</h2>
  <p>If the instruction says "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS," writing three words scores <strong>zero</strong>, even if the correct answer is inside your three. This is where good listeners lose marks by over-writing. Two rules protect you:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Read the instruction for every task</strong> — it changes between sections. One task may allow "ONE WORD ONLY," the next "TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER."</li>
    <li><strong>Cut the words the question already gives you.</strong> If the gap reads "The tour leaves from the ______" and you hear "leaves from the main entrance," the answer is "main entrance," not "the main entrance." Don't repeat words already printed in the sentence.</li>
  </ol>
  <p><strong>Hyphenated words count as one word:</strong> "part-time" and "check-in" are single words, so they are safe under a one-word limit. Contractions like "don't" are best avoided in an academic answer — write the full form if you can.</p>

  <h2>Singular vs plural — the trap that hides in plain sight</h2>
  <p>If the recording clearly says "you'll need two <em>forms</em> of ID," then "form" is wrong and "forms" is right. Grammar in the sentence around the gap tells you which to use: "a ______" signals a singular noun; "several ______" or "a range of ______" signals a plural. Always let the sentence, not just the audio, confirm the ending. A missing or extra "s" is one of the most common ways to lose an answer you actually heard.</p>

  <h2>Turn these rules into marks</h2>
  <p>None of this requires better listening — it requires better answering. Before your test, drill the recurring spelling words, decide now that you will write in capitals, and train yourself to check the word limit on every task.
