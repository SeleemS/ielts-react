  <p>Matching sentence endings is one of the less common Reading question types, which is exactly why it catches candidates unprepared. You get a list of sentence beginnings, a box of endings labelled A, B, C and so on — always more endings than beginnings — and the job of pairing them so that each completed sentence matches what the passage says. It looks like a puzzle you could solve without the passage at all. That is the trap: several endings will fit each beginning grammatically and plausibly. Only the passage decides.</p>

  <h2>The rules of the type</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>There are always spare endings.</strong> Typically two or three more endings than beginnings, written to be plausible. You cannot solve this by elimination alone.</li>
    <li><strong>The beginnings follow passage order.</strong> Like sentence completion, the information for beginning 1 appears in the text before the information for beginning 2. The endings in the box, of course, are in no order at all.</li>
    <li><strong>Each ending is used once at most.</strong> If two beginnings both seem to demand ending C, one of them is wrong.</li>
    <li><strong>The completed sentence must match the passage's meaning</strong> — not merely be true in general, and not merely be grammatical. This is the line between candidates who guess well and candidates who score.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The method: grammar shortlists, passage decides</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Read the beginnings first, not the endings.</strong> The beginnings carry the locator keywords and they follow passage order, so they are your map. Underline the stable words in each — names, technical terms, numbers.</li>
    <li><strong>Use grammar to shortlist endings.</strong> A beginning that ends "the researchers were unable to…" needs an ending starting with a base verb; one ending in "resulted in…" needs a noun phrase. A quick pass often cuts each beginning's candidates from seven endings to three. Shortlist — but never let grammar alone choose.</li>
    <li><strong>Locate beginning 1 in the passage and read that region properly.</strong> The answer's evidence is usually contained within a sentence or two. Ask: according to <em>this text</em>, how does this idea continue?</li>
    <li><strong>Test your shortlisted endings against the passage, not against plausibility.</strong> The correct pairing paraphrases the text. The decoys are built from real phrases in the passage attached to the wrong idea — recognising their vocabulary is precisely what makes them dangerous.</li>
    <li><strong>Move through the beginnings in order,</strong> crossing off endings as you commit. Passage order plus a shrinking box makes the last items faster than the first — the type rewards momentum.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>A worked example</h2>
  <p><strong>Passage extract:</strong> "The canal scheme was abandoned in 1837, not because the engineering was beyond the era's capability, but because successive investors withdrew once the railway's cheaper freight rates became public. Ironically, later surveys confirmed the proposed route would have functioned exactly as designed."</p>
  <p><strong>Beginning:</strong> 4. The canal project was abandoned because…</p>
  <p><strong>Endings box (extract):</strong> A. the engineering problems could not be solved. B. it lost its financial backing. C. later surveys revealed flaws in the route. D. freight rates on the canal rose sharply.</p>
  <p>Grammar admits all four — this is normal. The passage decides: A is explicitly denied ("not because the engineering was beyond… capability") yet uses the passage's own vocabulary, the classic decoy. C inverts the text — the surveys <em>confirmed</em> the route, and they came after the abandonment anyway. D garbles the detail: the cheap rates were the <em>railway's</em>. B paraphrases "successive investors withdrew" as "lost its financial backing" without sharing a single content word with the passage. The answer is <strong>B</strong> — and it displays the signature of correct answers across this type: right meaning, different words.</p>

  <h2>The traps, named</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>The vocabulary-magnet decoy:</strong> an ending built from the passage's own memorable words, attached to a claim the passage denies or never makes. If an ending feels right because you recognise its words, slow down.</li>
    <li><strong>The true-but-unstated ending:</strong> plausible in the real world, absent from the text. The question asks what the passage says, nothing else.</li>
    <li><strong>The world-knowledge completion:</strong> finishing the sentence from your own logic because the pairing "makes sense." Every decoy is written to make sense.</li>
    <li><strong>The chain error:</strong> committing a wrong ending early silently breaks a later pairing. If a later beginning has no acceptable ending left, revisit your least certain earlier answer rather than forcing the leftovers.</li>
    <li><strong>The time sink:</strong> because every pairing invites re-reading, this type can quietly absorb five minutes. Shortlist fast, decide from the located sentence, and move — lingering here starves the questions after it.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Where it sits among its neighbours</h2>
  <p>Do not confuse this type with <a href="/blog/ielts-reading-sentence-completion">sentence completion</a>, where you copy words from the passage under a word limit — here there is no limit and nothing to copy, only selection. Its true siblings are the other matching types: like <a href="/blog/ielts-reading-matching-features">matching features</a> it uses a reusable-looking option box, but unlike matching features its questions follow passage order — an enormous advantage worth consciously exploiting.</p>
