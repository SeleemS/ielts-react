  <p>Of all the visuals you can be given in <strong>IELTS Academic Writing Task 1</strong>, the table is the one candidates dread most. A line graph or bar chart tells you what to say — it goes up, it goes down. A table is just a grid of numbers with no built-in story, and it is your job to find one. The good news is that tables reward exactly the same skills as every other Task 1: selecting key features, writing a clear overview, and grouping data logically. Once you know how to read a table strategically, it becomes one of the easier visuals to score well on.</p>

  <h2>What Makes a Table Different</h2>
  <p>A table often contains more raw data than any other Task 1 visual — sometimes 20 or 30 numbers across several rows and columns. The trap is trying to describe all of it. The examiner is not testing whether you can copy numbers; they are testing whether you can identify what matters. As with every chart, Task 1 tables are marked on four equally weighted criteria: <strong>Task Achievement</strong>, <strong>Coherence and Cohesion</strong>, <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, and <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong>. The candidates who stay at Band 5 or 6 almost always fail on Task Achievement — they list figures instead of selecting and comparing them, and they skip the overview.</p>

  <h2>Step 1: Read the Table Before You Write</h2>
  <p>Spend the first two minutes reading, not writing. Ask three questions:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>What do the rows and columns represent?</strong> Usually one axis is categories (countries, age groups, products) and the other is either a time period or a set of measures.</li>
    <li><strong>Is there a time dimension?</strong> If the columns are years (2010, 2015, 2020), you describe change over time. If they are categories at a single point, you compare across categories.</li>
    <li><strong>Where are the extremes?</strong> Circle the highest and lowest figures, the biggest change, and anything that breaks the pattern. These are your key features.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>This reading step is what separates a focused report from a panicked data dump.</p>

  <h2>Step 2: Structure — The Same Four Paragraphs</h2>

  <h3>1. Introduction (1 sentence)</h3>
  <p>Paraphrase the prompt. If it says "The table below shows the percentage of households with internet access in four countries," write something like: "The table compares the proportion of homes connected to the internet across four nations over a fifteen-year period." Change <em>shows</em> to <em>compares</em> or <em>gives information about</em>, and reword the rest — never copy the sentence.</p>

  <h3>2. Overview (2 sentences)</h3>
  <p>This is the most important sentence in the report. State the two or three biggest features with <strong>no specific numbers</strong>. For a table with a time dimension, name the overall direction and the category that stands out: "Overall, internet access rose in every country over the period, with the sharpest growth in China, which had the lowest figure at the start but one of the highest by the end." For a table with no time dimension, name the highest and lowest categories instead. Always begin with <em>Overall</em> so the examiner sees it instantly.</p>

  <h3>3 and 4. Two Body Paragraphs</h3>
  <p>Now support the overview with selected figures. Group logically — do not go cell by cell. Two reliable groupings work for almost any table:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>By ranking:</strong> put the highest-value categories in one paragraph and the lowest in the other.</li>
    <li><strong>By time:</strong> describe the starting figures in one paragraph and the end figures (or the change) in the other.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Report accurate numbers with units, and — crucially — <strong>compare</strong> them rather than just stating them. "Germany stood at 78 per cent, more than double the figure for Brazil" scores higher than "Germany was 78 per cent. Brazil was 35 per cent."</p>

  <h2>Language That Lifts a Table Report</h2>
  <p>Tables reward precise comparison language. Build a small bank you can reuse:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Comparison:</strong> higher than, twice as many as, the highest of the four, considerably greater than, roughly equal to</li>
    <li><strong>Proportions:</strong> a quarter, a third, half, the majority, a small minority, one in five</li>
    <li><strong>Change over time:</strong> rose from X to Y, increased by 12 points, more than doubled, fell slightly</li>
    <li><strong>Approximation:</strong> just over, nearly, approximately, around, well above</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Vary your grammar: alternate "sales rose sharply" (verb + adverb) with "there was a sharp rise in sales" (adjective + noun) to show range.</p>

  <h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Describing every cell.</strong> Selective comparison scores far higher than an exhaustive list.</li>
    <li><strong>Forgetting the overview.</strong> No overview caps your Task Achievement at Band 5.</li>
    <li><strong>Mixing up "percentage" and "number."</strong> If the table shows percentages, do not write "the number of people" — accuracy of meaning is marked.</li>
    <li><strong>Writing under 150 words.</strong> You are penalised automatically; aim for 160–190.</li>
  </ul>
