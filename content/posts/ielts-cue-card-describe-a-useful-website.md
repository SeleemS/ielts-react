  <p>"Describe a website you often use" — or its close cousins "a useful website," "a website that helped you learn something," "an app or website you would recommend" — is a Part 2 topic almost every candidate can expect to be ready for. It is also one of the most forgiving cue cards on the circuit, because everyone genuinely uses websites, so nobody needs to invent material. The challenge is not ideas; it is filling two minutes with organised, connected speech. This guide walks through the preparation minute, gives a complete original model answer, and covers the Part 3 discussion that typically follows.</p>

  <h2>The cue card</h2>
  <p>A typical version reads:</p>
  <p><strong>Describe a website you find useful.</strong> You should say:<br />what the website is<br />how often you visit it<br />what you use it for<br />and explain why you find it so useful.</p>
  <p>You get one minute to prepare, with paper and a pencil, then speak for one to two minutes. The examiner will not interrupt with questions until your time is up.</p>

  <h2>Using the preparation minute</h2>
  <p>Do not write sentences — you will be tempted to read them, and reading kills your fluency score. Write <strong>four or five trigger words</strong>, one per bullet, plus one word for a story. For this card that might be: <em>maps — daily — navigation/reviews — trip story — saves arguments</em>. The single most valuable note is the story trigger: a specific incident is what carries you from ninety seconds to two minutes without padding, and specific detail is what the coherence criterion rewards.</p>
  <p>Pick a website you can describe with real detail, not the one that sounds most intellectual. A cooking site you use weekly beats an encyclopedia you barely open, because detail generates language and vagueness generates pauses.</p>

  <h2>Original model answer</h2>
  <p>The following is an <strong>original model answer written for practice on this site</strong> — it is not a transcript from a real test, and you should adapt its structure to a website you actually use rather than memorising it.</p>
  <p><em>"The website I'd like to talk about is an online map service that I use pretty much every day, usually on my phone rather than a computer. I honestly can't remember the last time I went somewhere unfamiliar without checking it first.</em></p>
  <p><em>I mainly use it for three things. The obvious one is navigation — finding the fastest route and checking traffic before I leave, because my city's traffic is completely unpredictable. The second is public transport: it tells me when the next bus actually arrives, not when it's supposed to arrive, which are rarely the same thing. And the third, which I think is underrated, is reading reviews. Whenever someone suggests a restaurant, I'll look it up, skim a few recent reviews and check the photos before agreeing to go.</em></p>
  <p><em>The clearest example of why I find it so useful was a trip I took last year to a city I'd never visited. I'd planned the whole first day around a museum, and while I was on the way the site flagged that it had closed early for a private event. It rerouted me to a gallery nearby that I'd never heard of, which honestly ended up being the highlight of the trip. Without the site I'd have wasted half a day and probably my mood as well.</em></p>
  <p><em>So I suppose the reason it's so useful is that it removes a whole category of small daily uncertainty — where things are, how long they take, whether they're worth it. It's one of the very few pieces of technology I'd genuinely struggle to give up, and the fact that it's free still slightly amazes me."</em></p>

  <h2>Why this answer scores well</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>It covers all four bullets in order</strong> — what it is, how often, what for, why useful — which gives the talk an audible structure without robotic signposting.</li>
    <li><strong>It organises the middle with a list</strong> ("three things… the obvious one… the second… and the third"), a simple device that sustains coherence for a full paragraph.</li>
    <li><strong>It tells one specific story.</strong> The museum incident does the heavy lifting: past narrative tenses, sequencing language, and genuine engagement. One developed example always beats three vague ones.</li>
    <li><strong>The vocabulary is natural and precise:</strong> "flagged," "rerouted," "underrated," "skim a few recent reviews," "a whole category of small daily uncertainty." Nothing is showy; everything is accurate.</li>
    <li><strong>It ends deliberately.</strong> "So I suppose the reason…" signals a conclusion, so the candidate finishes rather than trailing off — a small touch examiners notice.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Adapting the skeleton to any website</h2>
  <p>Strip the model to its frame and you can reuse it for nearly any "describe a website/app" card: <strong>what it is and how often you use it → two or three uses, listed → one specific story → why it matters, with a closing evaluation</strong>. A language-learning site, a video platform, a second-hand marketplace and a news site all fit this frame. Prepare one website deeply and you are covered for a whole family of cue cards, including "something that makes your life easier" and "a piece of technology you rely on."</p>

  <h2>The Part 3 questions that follow</h2>
  <p>Part 3 will lift the topic from your life to society. Expect questions like these, with brief original model responses:</p>
  <p><strong>Do old and young people use the internet differently?</strong> <em>"Broadly, yes. Younger people tend to treat it as the default for everything — shopping, socialising, entertainment — while many older people use it for specific tasks and still prefer doing important things like banking in person. Though that gap is closing faster than people assume."</em></p>
  <p><strong>Can we trust the information we find online?</strong> <em>"Selectively. The internet has made both good information and misinformation easier to access, so the burden has shifted to the reader — checking who published something and whether anyone else confirms it. I'd say the skill of evaluating sources is now as important as reading itself."</em></p>
  <p>Notice the shape: direct position, one developed reason, one qualification. That is the Part 3 formula, covered in depth in our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-3-discussion">Speaking Part 3 discussion questions</a>.</p>

  <h2>How to practise</h2>
  <p>Give yourself exactly one minute with pen and paper, then record a full two-minute attempt at this card. Check three things on playback: did you reach ninety seconds without stalling, did you include one specific story, and did you finish with a sentence rather than a fade-out.
