  <p>"How many words does IELTS Writing need?" is one of the first things candidates search for — and one of the most misunderstood. The rules are simple, but the <em>consequences</em> of breaking them are often exaggerated or misreported. This guide gives you the exact minimums, explains what the penalty for going under really is, whether writing more earns you a higher band, and how to check your length in seconds without stopping to count every word.</p>

  <h2>The exact minimums</h2>
  <p>There are two numbers to remember, and they are the same for Academic and General Training:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Task 1: at least 150 words.</strong> This is the report (Academic) or letter (General Training).</li>
    <li><strong>Task 2: at least 250 words.</strong> This is the essay, in both modules.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>These are <strong>minimums, not targets</strong>. The instruction on the paper says "write at least 150 words" and "write at least 250 words" — there is no official maximum. You have 60 minutes for both tasks combined, and the recommended split is <strong>20 minutes on Task 1 and 40 minutes on Task 2</strong>, because Task 2 carries twice the weight of Task 1 in your overall Writing band.</p>

  <h2>What the penalty for going under really is</h2>
  <p>This is where myths take over. You will read that examiners "deduct a fixed number of marks" for every word you are short. In the current marking scheme that is not how it works. Writing is scored on <strong>four equally weighted criteria</strong> — Task Achievement/Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy — each worth 25% of the task.</p>
  <p>An under-length response hurts you most under <strong>Task Achievement (Task 1)</strong> and <strong>Task Response (Task 2)</strong>. Here is the logic: if you write only 200 words for the essay, you almost certainly have not fully developed your position, extended your ideas, or covered all parts of the question. The band descriptors for Task Response reward a <em>fully developed</em> answer, and a short one simply cannot meet that standard. So the "penalty" is real, but it is indirect — a short answer is an underdeveloped answer, and underdeveloped answers cannot reach the top bands. You can read exactly what each level requires in our guide to the <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-band-descriptors">IELTS Writing band descriptors</a>.</p>
  <p>The practical takeaway: aim comfortably above the minimum — roughly <strong>160–190 words for Task 1</strong> and <strong>260–290 words for Task 2</strong> — so a miscount never drags you under the line.</p>

  <h2>Does writing more earn a higher band?</h2>
  <p>No. There is no maximum, but length is not rewarded for its own sake. A 400-word essay is not "better" than a focused 270-word one. In fact, writing too much is one of the most common ways strong candidates lose marks:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>You run out of time.</strong> Every extra minute on Task 2 is a minute stolen from checking your grammar or finishing Task 1.</li>
    <li><strong>You make more errors.</strong> More sentences means more chances for slips in Grammatical Range and Accuracy and Lexical Resource.</li>
    <li><strong>You drift off-topic.</strong> Padding an essay to 350 words often means adding a paragraph that does not answer the question, which lowers Task Response.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Quality and relevance beat quantity every time. Write enough to fully answer the question, then stop and check.</p>

  <h2>How words are counted</h2>
  <p>Examiners count the words in your answer, and a few conventions are worth knowing so you do not miscount:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Contractions</strong> such as <em>don't</em> or <em>it's</em> count as one word.</li>
    <li><strong>Hyphenated words</strong> such as <em>well-known</em> or <em>part-time</em> count as one word.</li>
    <li><strong>Numbers written as figures</strong>, such as <em>2015</em> or <em>500</em>, count as one word.</li>
    <li>The title or any copied wording from the question is generally not counted toward your total, so do not pad with the prompt.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>How to check your length without counting every word</h2>
  <p>On the <strong>computer-delivered test</strong>, this is solved for you: the screen shows a live word counter beneath your answer, so glance at it and stop worrying. On the <strong>paper-based test</strong>, counting all 250 words by hand wastes precious minutes. Use this estimate instead:</p>
  <ol>
    <li>Count the number of words in three or four typical lines and take the average — most candidates write about <strong>8–11 words per line</strong>.</li>
    <li>Multiply by the number of lines you have filled. At roughly 10 words a line, <strong>15 lines ≈ 150 words</strong> and <strong>25 lines ≈ 250 words</strong>.</li>
    <li>Learn what your own 150- and 250-word answers look like in practice so you can recognise the length by sight on test day.</li>
  </ol>
