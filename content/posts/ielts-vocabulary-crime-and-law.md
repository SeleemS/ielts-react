  <p>Crime and the law is one of the most reliable topics in the whole <strong>IELTS</strong> exam. It surfaces in Speaking Part 1 ("Is your neighbourhood safe?"), in Part 3 discussions about prisons and youth crime, and in Writing Task 2 essays about punishment, rehabilitation and prevention. Because the theme is so predictable, examiners have heard the same recycled phrases thousands of times. What actually lifts your band is not a longer word list but accurate, natural <strong>collocations</strong> used in the right place. This guide gives you the crime and law vocabulary that moves your score, and shows you how to deploy it.</p>

  <h2>Why This Vocabulary Matters</h2>
  <p><strong>Lexical Resource</strong> is one of the four equally weighted marking criteria in both Speaking and Writing, so it accounts for roughly a quarter of your score in each. The band descriptors do not reward rare or dramatic words for their own sake — they reward range, precision and natural collocation. A Band 5 candidate says "do a crime"; a Band 7 candidate says "commit an offence" or "break the law." The idea is identical, but the second sounds like a real English speaker rather than a translated phrase.</p>

  <h2>Learn Collocations, Not Single Words</h2>
  <p>Legal English pairs words together in fixed ways you cannot guess from grammar. You <em>commit</em> a crime, you do not "make" a crime; a judge <em>hands down</em> a sentence; a criminal <em>serves</em> time. Memorising these chunks is what keeps your English fluent under pressure. Group them by sub-topic so you can pull the right set the moment a question lands.</p>

  <h3>Offences and Offenders</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>commit a crime / an offence</strong> — the standard verb; never "do" a crime</li>
    <li><strong>break the law</strong>; <strong>a law-abiding citizen</strong> (someone who obeys the law)</li>
    <li><strong>petty crime</strong> (minor: shoplifting, vandalism) vs <strong>serious / violent crime</strong></li>
    <li><strong>a repeat / first-time offender</strong>; <strong>reoffend</strong> — commit another crime after release</li>
    <li><strong>white-collar crime</strong> — fraud, embezzlement and other non-violent financial crime</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Police and the Courts</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>arrest a suspect</strong>; <strong>charge someone with</strong> an offence</li>
    <li><strong>stand trial</strong>; <strong>be found guilty / not guilty</strong>; <strong>acquit</strong> (declare not guilty)</li>
    <li><strong>a fair trial</strong>; <strong>the burden of proof</strong>; <strong>a miscarriage of justice</strong> (a wrongful conviction)</li>
    <li><strong>hand down a sentence</strong>; <strong>bring someone to justice</strong></li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Punishment and Sentencing</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>serve a prison sentence</strong>; <strong>be sentenced to</strong> five years</li>
    <li><strong>a custodial sentence</strong> (prison) vs a <strong>community sentence</strong> / <strong>community service</strong></li>
    <li><strong>impose a fine</strong>; <strong>be released on parole</strong>; <strong>a suspended sentence</strong></li>
    <li><strong>capital punishment</strong> (the death penalty); <strong>a deterrent</strong> — something that discourages crime</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Prevention and Society (for Task 2)</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>tackle / crack down on crime</strong>; <strong>reduce the crime rate</strong></li>
    <li><strong>rehabilitate offenders</strong>; <strong>reintegrate into society</strong>; <strong>cut reoffending</strong></li>
    <li><strong>the root causes of crime</strong> — poverty, unemployment, lack of education</li>
    <li><strong>tackle crime at its source</strong>; <strong>invest in crime prevention</strong>; <strong>increase police presence</strong></li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Model Sentences You Can Adapt</h2>
  <p>Vocabulary only earns marks inside a natural sentence. Notice how the collocations below carry the meaning without any forced "big" words:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><em>Speaking Part 1:</em> "My area is pretty safe — there's a bit of petty crime like vandalism, but nothing serious, and there's a visible police presence at night."</li>
    <li><em>Speaking Part 3:</em> "I think prisons should focus on rehabilitating offenders rather than just punishing them, because helping people reintegrate into society is what really cuts reoffending."</li>
    <li><em>Writing Task 2:</em> "Rather than simply imposing longer custodial sentences, governments should tackle the root causes of crime, since poverty and unemployment drive many first-time offenders."</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Each of these would strengthen the <strong>Lexical Resource</strong> band without a single memorised idiom sounding artificial.   <h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>"Do" a crime.</strong> The verb is always <em>commit</em>. "He did a crime" is a classic slip that costs accuracy marks.</li>
    <li><strong>Confusing the people.</strong> Keep <em>suspect</em> (accused), <em>defendant</em> (on trial), <em>offender</em> and <em>convict</em> (found guilty) distinct — precision is marked.</li>
    <li><strong>Over-dramatic vocabulary.</strong> Reaching for words like "heinous" or "atrocious" in every sentence sounds forced; natural collocations score higher than rare adjectives.</li>
    <li><strong>Ignoring register.</strong> "Cops" and "crook" are too informal for Task 2 — use <em>the police</em> and <em>offender / criminal</em> in academic writing.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>How to Practise</h2>
  <p>Do not try to memorise all of these at once. Pick one sub-topic, write three or four of your own sentences using its collocations, and say them aloud until they feel automatic. Then recycle them across question types — a sentencing collocation from a Part 3 answer can reappear in a Task 2 essay on punishment. Build the same habit for other frequent themes, such as the language in our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-vocabulary-technology">IELTS vocabulary for technology</a>. Precise, natural collocations used with confidence are exactly what separates a Band 6 from a Band 7 in Lexical Resource.</p>
