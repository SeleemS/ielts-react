  <p>The pie chart looks like the friendliest visual in Academic <strong>Writing Task 1</strong> &mdash; a few colourful slices and some percentages &mdash; yet it is where many candidates quietly lose marks. The reason is that a pie chart does not test trend language at all. It tests your ability to describe <strong>proportions</strong>: which slice is biggest, how the shares compare, and, when there are two or more charts, how those shares change. Get the proportion vocabulary right and a pie chart becomes one of the easiest Task 1 questions to score well on. This guide gives you the structure, the language, and a full model answer.</p>

  <h2>What a pie chart actually asks you to do</h2>
  <p>Task 1 is marked on four equally weighted criteria: <strong>Task Achievement</strong>, <strong>Coherence and Cohesion</strong>, <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, and <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong>. You have 20 minutes and must write at least 150 words. For a pie chart, Task Achievement means you have identified the largest and smallest segments, grouped and compared the shares sensibly, and &mdash; crucially &mdash; included a clear <strong>overview</strong>. A report with no overview is capped at Band 5 for Task Achievement, no matter how good the rest is.</p>

  <p>One idea decides everything else: a pie chart shows <em>parts of a whole</em>, and the parts always add up to 100 per cent. So your language should constantly signal proportion &mdash; "made up", "accounted for", "a share of" &mdash; rather than the "rose" and "fell" verbs you would use for a line graph.</p>

  <h2>The four-paragraph structure</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Introduction (1 sentence):</strong> paraphrase the question. Change the wording but keep the meaning.</li>
    <li><strong>Overview (2 sentences):</strong> state the most striking features &mdash; usually the largest and smallest slices &mdash; with no specific figures.</li>
    <li><strong>Body paragraph 1:</strong> describe the biggest segments in detail, with percentages.</li>
    <li><strong>Body paragraph 2:</strong> describe the remaining, smaller segments, with percentages.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>If you are given <strong>two pie charts</strong> (for example, the same categories in 2010 and 2020), change your grouping: use one body paragraph for the categories that grew and one for those that shrank, and let your overview name the single biggest change. The same shape works either way &mdash; only the grouping logic shifts.</p>

  <h2>Proportion vocabulary that scores</h2>
  <p>Strong Lexical Resource on a pie chart comes from three families of language: verbs of proportion, fraction and percentage phrases, and comparison structures.</p>

  <h3>Verbs and phrases for a share of the whole</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>X accounted for / made up / constituted / represented</strong> 40 per cent of the total.</li>
    <li><strong>The largest / greatest proportion</strong> went to X.</li>
    <li><strong>X held / had</strong> the biggest share, at just over a third.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Fractions instead of bare numbers</h3>
  <p>Converting a percentage into an approximate fraction shows range and reads more naturally. Learn these conversions:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>50%</strong> &rarr; half</li>
    <li><strong>25%</strong> &rarr; a quarter / one in four</li>
    <li><strong>33%</strong> &rarr; a third</li>
    <li><strong>20%</strong> &rarr; a fifth</li>
    <li><strong>10%</strong> &rarr; a tenth</li>
    <li><strong>75%</strong> &rarr; three quarters</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Soften them with <em>roughly, approximately, just over, just under, nearly</em>: "just under a quarter", "roughly a third". This keeps your report accurate without repeating the same "X per cent" pattern in every sentence.</p>

  <h3>Comparison structures</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Twice / three times as much as:</strong> "Spending on housing was twice as high as spending on transport."</li>
    <li><strong>Comparatives:</strong> "a far larger share than", "slightly smaller than".</li>
    <li><strong>Ranking:</strong> "the second most common category", "the smallest segment".</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Model answer</h2>
  <p>Imagine a pie chart showing how an average household spends its monthly budget: housing 40%, food 25%, transport 20%, and other 15%. A Band 7 report might read:</p>
  <p><em>"The pie chart illustrates how a typical household divides its monthly spending across four categories. Overall, housing was comfortably the largest expense, while the smallest share was allocated to a mix of other items. Housing accounted for the greatest proportion of the budget, at 40 per cent &mdash; roughly twice as much as was spent on transport, which made up a fifth of the total. Food represented the second largest category at a quarter of all spending. The remaining 15 per cent, grouped as 'other', constituted the smallest share, standing just below the amount devoted to transport."</em></p>
  <p>Notice how few "per cent" figures appear: the report leans on fractions, ranking words, and one clean comparison. That variety is exactly what the descriptors reward.</p>

  <h2>Common mistakes to avoid</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Using trend verbs on a single chart.</strong> With one pie chart there is no time axis, so nothing "rises" or "falls". Only use change language when two or more charts let you compare across dates.</li>
    <li><strong>Skipping the overview.</strong> Begin it with <em>Overall</em> and name the biggest and smallest slices &mdash; this is where most easy marks are won.</li>
    <li><strong>Listing every slice in order.</strong> Group and compare instead of reading the chart clockwise like a list.</li>
    <li><strong>Adding opinions.</strong> Task 1 is a factual report; never explain <em>why</em> a category is large or say whether it is good.</li>
  </ul>
