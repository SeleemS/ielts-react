  <p>Of all the question types in IELTS Listening, <strong>completion tasks</strong> — filling gaps in a form, a set of notes, a table, or a flow chart — are the ones you will meet most often, especially in Section 1 and Section 4. They feel simple: you just write the missing word. Yet they are where careful listeners quietly leak marks, because the answer is scored on more than whether you heard it. Spell it wrong, write one word too many, or miss a plural, and a correct answer becomes a zero. This guide gives you a method built around the two things that actually decide the mark: <strong>prediction before you listen</strong> and <strong>accuracy when you write</strong>.</p>

  <h2>Where completion tasks appear</h2>
  <p>IELTS Listening has four sections, about 30 minutes of audio, and 40 questions in total, and the recording plays only once. Completion tasks dominate the sections built around concrete information:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Form completion</strong> shows up in Section 1 — an everyday transaction such as booking accommodation or joining a club, where you note names, dates, phone numbers, and prices.</li>
    <li><strong>Note, table, and flow-chart completion</strong> appear most in Section 4, the academic monologue, where you follow a lecture and fill gaps in a structured summary.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Every completion task carries a word limit in the instruction, usually <strong>"NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER"</strong> or "ONE WORD ONLY." That line is not decoration — it is a scoring rule, and ignoring it is the single most common way to lose a mark you had already earned.</p>

  <h2>Win the marks before the audio starts</h2>
  <p>You are given a few seconds to read each section before it plays. Most candidates waste that time skimming. Instead, use it to <strong>predict</strong> what each gap needs, so that when the audio arrives you are confirming an expectation rather than hearing cold. For every gap, ask two questions:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>What type of word is missing?</strong> Read the words around the gap. Do the grammar and meaning demand a noun, a number, a date, a name, a plural? "The tour leaves at ______" needs a time. "Members must bring their own ______" needs a noun, probably countable. Knowing the type in advance means you cannot be fooled by a distractor of the wrong shape.</li>
    <li><strong>How many words can I write?</strong> Fix the word limit in your mind before the gap arrives so you are never tempted to write a whole phrase when only one word is allowed.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Underline the keyword next to each gap as well. That keyword — or a paraphrase of it — is your signal that the answer is about to be spoken, so you know exactly when to listen hardest.</p>

  <h2>The word limit is a hard rule</h2>
  <p>If the instruction allows two words and a number, then "9.30 am" or "old library" is fine, but "the old library building" scores <strong>zero</strong> even though it contains the right answer. Two habits keep you safe:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Drop unnecessary articles.</strong> If you hear "a return ticket" and the limit is two words, write "return ticket," not "a return ticket," unless the article is genuinely part of the answer.</li>
    <li><strong>Remember hyphenated and compound words.</strong> A hyphenated word such as "well-known" counts as one word, and a number written in figures such as "1995" or "£40" counts as your number — so you can still add words alongside it.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Spelling, numbers, and plurals decide the rest</h2>
  <p>The audio hands you the word; your pen or keyboard has to reproduce it exactly. Three things catch people out:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Spelling must be correct.</strong> A misspelt answer is wrong, full stop. Both British and American spellings are accepted, but only if they are complete and accurate. Names and unusual words are often spelled out letter by letter in the audio precisely because they are being tested — so drill the alphabet, and listen for "double L" and "P as in Peter."</li>
    <li><strong>Singular versus plural must fit the sentence.</strong> Writing "ticket" when the surrounding sentence needs "tickets" is a grammatical error and is marked wrong. Your prediction step tells you which one the gap wants.</li>
    <li><strong>Numbers and dates have a form.</strong> You can usually write numbers as figures, which is faster and safer than spelling them. Watch for the classic distractor where a speaker says one figure and then corrects it: "It's twelve pounds — sorry, that's the member price, it's fifteen for guests." The answer is the confirmed one, not the first one you hear.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>A short worked example</h2>
  <p><strong>Notes:</strong> "Equipment provided: helmet and ______" &nbsp;(NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS)</p>
  <p><strong>Audio:</strong> "We give everyone a helmet, and there are life jackets in the shed if you want one — you don't need to bring your own."</p>
  <p>The answer is <strong>life jackets</strong>, plural, because the notes list equipment for the group and the speaker offers them to everyone. "Life jacket" singular would be risky, and "life jackets in the shed" breaks the two-word limit. Prediction told you a noun was coming; the grammar of the list told you it was plural.</p>

  <h2>The completion checklist</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Read ahead and predict the word type and word count for every gap.</li>
    <li>Never exceed the stated word limit, even by an article.</li>
    <li>Write numbers as figures unless told otherwise.</li>
    <li>Check singular versus plural against the sentence around the gap.</li>
    <li>Spell every answer fully and correctly; use the letter-by-letter parts of the audio.</li>
    <li>If you miss a gap, leave it and move on — the answers come in order, so the next keyword shows you where the speaker now is.</li>
    <li>Never leave a blank; there is no penalty for a wrong answer, and you get 10 minutes to transfer on paper or 2 minutes to check on computer.</li>
  </ul>
