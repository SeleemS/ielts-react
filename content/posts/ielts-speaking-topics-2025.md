  <p>Every year, candidates hunt for "this year's Speaking topics" as if the test reinvents itself each January. It does not. IELTS Speaking has drawn on the same stable pool of everyday themes for decades — your home, your habits, people, places, objects, experiences, and the societal questions that grow out of them — because the test measures how well you communicate, not what you know. Nobody can tell you your exact test-day questions; anyone claiming to sell "this month's real questions" is selling unreliable recall at best. The good news is that you do not need predictions. The topic pool is stable enough to prepare systematically, and this guide maps it, Part by Part, with a method that transfers to any variation the examiner asks.</p>

  <h2>The topic families that recur in Part 1</h2>
  <p>Part 1 (four to five minutes) covers two or three familiar topics, and the first is almost always <strong>where you live</strong> or <strong>what you do</strong> — your hometown, your home, your work or studies. After that opener, the recurring families include:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Daily life:</strong> routines, mornings and evenings, weekends, sleep, food and cooking, shopping.</li>
    <li><strong>Interests:</strong> music, films and TV, reading, sport, hobbies, photography.</li>
    <li><strong>People and places:</strong> friends, family, neighbours; parks, cafés, libraries, the countryside.</li>
    <li><strong>Objects and technology:</strong> your phone, apps, clothes, gifts, things you own.</li>
    <li><strong>Light abstractions:</strong> weather, colours, numbers, being busy, patience, saving money.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The questions change wording; the families persist. Prepare the two near-certain openers thoroughly — our model answers for <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-1-home-hometown">home and hometown</a> and <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-1-work-study">work and study</a> show the technique — and touch the rest through rotating practice.</p>

  <h2>The cue card types behind every Part 2 topic</h2>
  <p>Part 2 hands you a card and two minutes to speak. Cards feel endlessly varied, but nearly all of them are one of five types:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>A person</strong> — someone you admire, a helpful friend, an interesting older person.</li>
    <li><strong>A place</strong> — a favourite city, a quiet spot, a building, somewhere you'd like to visit.</li>
    <li><strong>An object or thing</strong> — a useful website or app, a gift, a photo, a piece of technology.</li>
    <li><strong>An experience or event</strong> — a journey, a celebration, a time you helped someone, a difficult decision.</li>
    <li><strong>A goal, plan or abstract</strong> — an ambition, a skill you want to learn, a change you want to make.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Prepare one strong, detailed story per type — five stories total — and you can adapt to almost any card, because cards within a type share their skeleton: a useful website and a favourite app are the same talk wearing different names. See the method in action in our worked model answers for <a href="/blog/ielts-cue-card-describe-a-useful-website">describe a useful website</a> and <a href="/blog/ielts-cue-card-describe-an-important-goal">describe an important goal</a>, and the general two-minute technique in our <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-2-cue-card">Part 2 cue card guide</a>.</p>

  <h2>Part 3: the same themes, zoomed out</h2>
  <p>Part 3 takes your cue card topic and widens it to society: a card about a website becomes a discussion of how the internet changes education; a card about a goal becomes questions about ambition and whether parents should push children. The recurring discussion axes are remarkably stable — <strong>past vs present</strong> ("How has X changed?"), <strong>young vs old</strong>, <strong>individual vs government responsibility</strong>, <strong>advantages vs drawbacks</strong>, and <strong>predictions</strong> ("How might X change in future?"). Preparing these five question shapes is far more valuable than preparing topics, because they repeat across every theme. The answer pattern — direct position, one developed reason, one qualification — is covered in our <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-3-discussion">Part 3 discussion guide</a>.</p>

  <h2>How one theme travels through all three parts</h2>
  <p>Take "technology": Part 1 asks how often you use your phone; Part 2 may ask you to describe a useful app; Part 3 asks whether people depend on technology too much. Practising that <em>progression</em> — personal detail, then structured story, then general argument on one theme — is the single most efficient Speaking preparation there is, because it rehearses exactly what the test does. Run the drill on the big five themes: technology, work and study, places and travel, people and relationships, health and lifestyle.</p>

  <h2>Vocabulary: prepare themes, not scripts</h2>
  <p>For each major theme, build a small bank of natural collocations you can genuinely deploy — "career progression" and "a close-knit team" for work, "screen time" and "look things up" for technology, "renewable energy" and "public transport" for environment questions. Our topic banks for <a href="/blog/ielts-vocabulary-technology">technology</a> and <a href="/blog/ielts-vocabulary-environment">environment</a> vocabulary model the approach. What you must not build is scripts: examiners are trained to detect memorised answers, and delivery flattens audibly the moment recitation starts. Prepare ideas, stories and phrases — never paragraphs.</p>

  <h2>A weekly routine that covers the pool</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Pick one theme</strong> and answer five Part 1 questions aloud, without notes, recording yourself.</li>
    <li><strong>Take one cue card</strong> from that theme's type: one minute of pencil notes, two minutes speaking.</li>
    <li><strong>Answer three Part 3 questions</strong> on the same theme, 30–45 seconds each, using position–reason–qualification.</li>
    <li><strong>Review the recording</strong> against the four marking criteria — fluency, vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation — and re-record the weakest answer once. The criteria are unpacked in our guide to the <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors">Speaking band descriptors</a>.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Rotate themes weekly and the whole pool is covered inside two months. If a theme feels thin when you practise it — you run out of things to say about, say, the countryside or old buildings — that is valuable information: spend ten minutes collecting two personal anecdotes and five collocations for it, and the thinness disappears. Practise against fresh, original questions in our <a href="/speakingquestion">Speaking question bank</a> — and stop refreshing forums for predictions. The test's topics were never the secret; the preparation method was.</p>
