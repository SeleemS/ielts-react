  <p>Most candidates who lose marks in <strong>IELTS Listening</strong> lose them in the last ten questions. The test has <strong>four parts, 40 questions and about 30 minutes of audio</strong> (plus 10 minutes to transfer answers in the paper test, or two minutes to check them on the computer-delivered version), and the difficulty climbs steadily. Part 4 is a single-speaker <strong>academic monologue</strong> — usually a short university-style lecture on a subject like migration patterns, urban design or the history of a material. There is no conversation to give you thinking gaps, the vocabulary is denser, and the recording, like every part of IELTS Listening, plays <strong>once only</strong>. This guide is about how to survive it.</p>

  <h2>What makes Section 4 different</h2>
  <p>Two things set Part 4 apart from Parts 1 to 3, and both change how you should approach it:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>It is one continuous talk.</strong> One speaker, no dialogue, no interruptions. You cannot rely on a second voice repeating or correcting information the way you can in Parts 1 and 3.</li>
    <li><strong>It plays with no mid-section break.</strong> In Parts 1 to 3 the recording stops halfway so you can read the next set of questions. Part 4 runs <em>straight through from question 31 to question 40</em>. You get time to read the questions before it starts, and then you are on your own until the end.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>That single structural quirk — no pause to catch up — is why people fall behind here and never recover. Miss one answer and panic, and you can easily lose the next three while you are still hunting for the one you missed.</p>

  <h2>Use every second of the reading time</h2>
  <p>Before Part 4 begins you get roughly half a minute to look at the questions. Because there is no break later, this is your <em>only</em> planning window, so spend it deliberately:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Read all ten questions, not just the first few.</strong> You want the shape of the whole section in your head so you know what is coming.</li>
    <li><strong>Notice the headings and sub-topics.</strong> Part 4 questions are very often laid out as a set of notes with headings. Those headings tell you exactly how the lecture is organised and roughly when each answer will arrive.</li>
    <li><strong>Underline the word limit</strong> — usually "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS" or "ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER." Writing three words when the limit is two makes the answer wrong even if the words are correct.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Predict the answer before you hear it</h2>
  <p>The strongest Part 4 skill is prediction. Most questions are gap-fills in a sentence or a note, and the grammar of the gap tells you what kind of word must go in it. Look at the words <em>around</em> each blank and ask: is this a noun, a verb, a plural, a number, an adjective? A gap after "a" needs a singular noun; a gap after "increased" probably needs a percentage or a figure. Deciding this in advance means that when the answer arrives you only have to catch a word you are already expecting, instead of processing the whole sentence from scratch. This is the same predict-then-catch method that works on <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-form-note-completion">form, note and table completion</a> throughout the test — it just matters most in Part 4, where you have no second chance to reconsider.</p>

  <h2>Follow the signposts to know where you are</h2>
  <p>Because the talk is continuous, your biggest risk is losing your place. Academic lecturers signpost heavily, and those signposts line up with the order of the questions — which always follow the order of the recording. Train your ear for phrases that mark a move to the next point:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"Let's turn to…", "Moving on to…", "The next factor is…"</strong> — a new heading or topic is starting, so the next answer is close.</li>
    <li><strong>"For example…", "such as…", "in particular…"</strong> — a specific detail is coming, often the exact word for a gap.</li>
    <li><strong>"However…", "On the other hand…", "Despite this…"</strong> — a contrast, which frequently carries the answer the question is testing.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>If you realise you have missed an answer, do not freeze. Write nothing, leave the space blank, and jump forward to the <em>next</em> question you can still catch. Chasing a lost answer is how one blank becomes five.</p>

  <h2>Common mistakes to drop</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Waiting to hear the exact words on the page.</strong> The speaker paraphrases the printed notes; the heading might say "reasons for decline" while the lecturer says "why numbers fell." Listen for the meaning, not the identical wording.</li>
    <li><strong>Writing more than the word limit.</strong> A correct idea over the limit scores zero. Trust the instruction you underlined.</li>
    <li><strong>Getting stuck decoding hard vocabulary.</strong> If a technical term throws you, let it go and keep listening — the next answer will not wait for you.</li>
    <li><strong>Ignoring spelling.</strong> Answers must be spelled correctly to count. Part 4 is dense, so a rushed, misspelt word loses a mark you had already earned.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>These overlap with the broader set of <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-common-traps">common IELTS Listening traps</a>, which are worth reviewing alongside this section.</p>

  <h2>How to practise Part 4</h2>
  <p>Prediction and place-keeping are habits, so drill them in isolation. Take any Part 4 transcript, cover the audio, and for every gap write down what <em>type</em> of word it must be before you check — you will be surprised how often the grammar alone narrows it to one or two possibilities. Then listen straight through once, at full speed, and force yourself to abandon any answer you miss rather than rewinding. Build that discipline on authentic material on the <a href="/listeningquestion">IELTS Listening Questions</a> page, and Part 4 stops being the section that quietly wrecks your score.</p>

  <p>Section 4 is not harder because the English is impossible; it is harder because it never pauses and never repeats. Read ahead in your one window, predict the shape of each answer, follow the signposts, and let go of anything you miss. Keep your place, and the last ten questions become just ten more you can answer.</p>
