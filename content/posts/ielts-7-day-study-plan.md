  <p>Seven days is not enough time to raise your level of English — but it is more than enough time to stop throwing away marks you should already be earning. With a test date this close, the goal is not to learn new grammar or memorise word lists. It is to master the format, fix the handful of technique errors that cost most candidates a whole band, and walk in rehearsed rather than surprised. This <strong>7-day IELTS study plan</strong> assumes roughly two focused hours a day and no prior structured revision. If you have longer, follow the fuller <a href="/blog/ielts-30-day-study-plan">30-day study plan</a> instead; if the test really is next week, start here today.</p>

  <h2>Day 1: Take a timed baseline and find your weakest skill</h2>
  <p>You cannot fix what you have not measured. In one sitting, do a full timed Reading section and a full Listening section, and write one Task 2 essay in 40 minutes. Then face the numbers honestly. Reading is <strong>40 questions in 60 minutes</strong> and Listening is <strong>40 questions across four sections in about 30 minutes</strong>, so a raw score out of 40 tells you exactly where you stand. Convert those raw scores with the free <a href="/band-calculator">IELTS band score calculator</a>, and run your essay through the <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">AI Writing Checker</a> for a criterion-by-criterion estimate. Write all four bands down. Because your overall band is the average of the four skills, the fastest way to raise it in a week is to lift your single lowest skill — not to polish the one you are already good at.</p>

  <h2>Day 2: Reading — technique, not speed reading</h2>
  <p>Most Reading marks are lost to method, not vocabulary. Spend today on the two question types that punish guessing hardest: <a href="/reading/true-false-not-given">True/False/Not Given</a> and matching headings. For True/False/Not Given, drill the difference between <em>False</em> (the passage contradicts the statement) and <em>Not Given</em> (the passage simply does not say) — confusing these two is the most common Reading error there is. Practise scanning for keywords rather than reading every word, and remember there is <strong>no extra transfer time in Reading</strong>: your answers must be on the answer sheet when the hour ends. Do sets on the <a href="/readingquestion">Reading question bank</a> and, for every wrong answer, find the exact line that proves the correct one.</p>

  <h2>Day 3: Listening — train yourself to let answers go</h2>
  <p>The Listening audio plays <strong>once and never stops</strong>, so the skill that saves the most marks is recovering from a missed answer instead of freezing on it. Today, do two or three full sections and rehearse that recovery: if you miss one, immediately refocus on the next question rather than dwelling on the gap. Learn the classic traps — spelling of names and numbers, plurals, and speakers who correct themselves ("it's on King Street — sorry, Queen Street"). Read the questions during the pauses before each section so you know what to listen for. Use the <a href="/listeningquestion">Listening question bank</a> and always review with the transcript open so you can see the exact phrase that carried each answer.</p>

  <h2>Day 4: Writing Task 2 — one reliable structure</h2>
  <p>You do not have time to master five essay types this week, so master one dependable structure that works for almost any prompt: a paraphrased introduction with a clear position, two developed body paragraphs each built around a single main idea, and a short conclusion that restates that position. Task 2 is worth twice Task 1 and demands a <strong>minimum of 250 words</strong>. Write one full essay today in 40 minutes, then check it against the four criteria — Task Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy — using the <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">Writing Checker</a> rather than your own impression. The single most common Task 2 mistake is not answering the whole question, so before you write, underline exactly what the prompt asks.</p>

  <h2>Day 5: Writing Task 1 and a rewrite</h2>
  <p>In the morning, learn the shape of your Task 1. Academic candidates should practise selecting and grouping the main features of a chart and always writing an <strong>overview</strong> sentence — the missing overview is what caps most Task 1 answers at Band 5. General Training candidates should drill the three letter tones (formal, semi-formal, informal) and the fixed openings and closings each one uses. Task 1 needs a <strong>minimum of 150 words</strong> in about 20 minutes. In the afternoon, do not write a brand-new essay — instead rewrite yesterday's Task 2, fixing only its lowest criterion. Repairing one paragraph until it is genuinely strong teaches more than drafting another mediocre essay from scratch.</p>

  <h2>Day 6: Speaking — rehearse out loud</h2>
  <p>Speaking is the skill candidates neglect most in a rush, yet it is a quarter of your overall band and rewards simple preparation. The test lasts <strong>11 to 14 minutes</strong> in three parts: familiar questions about you, a two-minute long turn from a cue card, and an abstract discussion. Record yourself answering several Part 2 cue cards for the full two minutes, then listen back for pauses, repetition, and one-word answers. Extend every reply with a reason and an example so you never dry up. Do not memorise scripted answers — examiners are trained to spot them and it lowers your fluency score. Practise thinking aloud in English for ten minutes today using the <a href="/speakingquestion">Speaking question bank</a> prompts.</p>

  <h2>Day 7: One light mock, then rest</h2>
  <p>The day before the test is for confidence, not cramming. Do one short timed section of your strongest skill so you finish on a success, then stop studying by early afternoon. Confirm the logistics instead: know your test format, your reporting deadline, what identification to bring, and how you will get there. Remind yourself how the maths works — your overall band is the average of the four skills rounded to the nearest half band, where a <strong>.25 rounds up to the next half band and a .75 rounds up to the next whole band</strong>. A good night's sleep will do more for tomorrow's score than another hour of practice.</p>

  <h2>What a single week can and cannot do</h2>
  <p>Be realistic: seven days will not turn a Band 5 into a Band 7. What it will do is stop you from scoring below your true level because you misread the instructions, ran out of time, left the answer sheet incomplete, or froze in the Speaking test. Those are the marks candidates lose needlessly, and they are exactly the ones a focused week recovers. Check your progress by converting a final raw score on the <a href="/band-calculator">band calculator</a> and comparing it with your day-one baseline. Then walk in knowing the format cold — because on a short timeline, that is the highest-value thing you can possibly know.</p>
