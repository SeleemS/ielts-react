---
title: "How to Go From Band 6 to Band 7 in IELTS Writing: The Four Gaps That Hold You Back"
date: August 15, 2026
excerpt: "A Band 6 essay is competent; a Band 7 essay is controlled. Here is exactly what changes across all four Writing criteria — and the concrete habits that close the half-band gap."
---

  <p>The jump from Band 6 to Band 7 in IELTS Writing frustrates more candidates than any other, partly because a Band 6 script does not feel broken. Your ideas are clear, your grammar mostly works, and you finished on time — yet the score stalls. The reason is that Band 7 is not "Band 6 but longer" or "Band 6 with fancier words." It is a different <em>quality of control</em>, and it shows up in four specific places. Your Writing score is the average of four equally weighted criteria: <strong>Task Response</strong>, <strong>Coherence and Cohesion</strong>, <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, and <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong>. This guide walks through the gap between 6 and 7 in each, with the exact habit that closes it.</p>

  <h2>Gap 1: Task Response — answering fully, not partially</h2>
  <p>At Band 6, the descriptors say you "address the task" but often do so incompletely or with a position that is not fully developed. At Band 7, you must present "a clear position throughout" and develop each main idea. The single most common Band 6 failure is answering only half the question. If a Task 2 prompt says <em>"Discuss both views and give your opinion,"</em> a Band 6 answer often discusses both views and forgets the opinion — or gives an opinion so faint the examiner cannot locate it.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Underline every task word before you plan.</strong> "To what extent," "both views," "advantages and disadvantages" — each demands specific content. Skipping one caps you at 6.</li>
    <li><strong>State your position in the introduction and hold it to the conclusion.</strong> If paragraph two argues one way and paragraph three quietly switches, that reads as "no clear position throughout."</li>
    <li><strong>Extend, don't just list.</strong> Band 7 develops ideas: point, then <em>why</em>, then a concrete example. One well-extended reason beats three underdeveloped ones.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Gap 2: Coherence and Cohesion — logic the reader never has to reconstruct</h2>
  <p>Band 6 "arranges information coherently" but the linking is "mechanical" or over-used. Band 7 "logically organises information" with each paragraph having "a clear central topic." The tell-tale Band 6 habit is starting sentences with <em>Firstly, Secondly, Moreover, In addition, Furthermore</em> like beads on a string. Examiners are trained to treat over-signposting as a weakness, not a strength.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>One idea per paragraph, announced in the first sentence.</strong> A clear topic sentence does more for cohesion than any connector.</li>
    <li><strong>Prefer reference and substitution over connectors.</strong> "This trend," "such policies," "the former" tie sentences together invisibly — the mark of Band 7 cohesion.</li>
    <li><strong>Cut connectors you don't need.</strong> If the logic is already clear, "Moreover" is noise. Use linkers to signal a genuine turn, not to decorate.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Gap 3: Lexical Resource — precision, not decoration</h2>
  <p>This is where the most points are quietly lost. Band 6 uses "an adequate range" with "some errors in word choice." Band 7 uses vocabulary "with some flexibility and precision" and shows "awareness of style and collocation." Note what Band 7 does <strong>not</strong> require: rare or academic words. It requires <em>the right ordinary word in the right place</em>. Candidates who paste in memorised phrases like "it is a double-edged sword" or "plethora of" usually drop marks, because the phrase does not quite fit and the error is visible.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Fix collocation, not rarity.</strong> "Commit a crime," "raise awareness," "a heavy fine" score; "do a crime," "make awareness," "a strong fine" do not — same difficulty, opposite result.</li>
    <li><strong>Paraphrase the prompt with meaning, not a thesaurus.</strong> Swapping "children" for "juveniles" changes the register wrongly. Band 7 paraphrasing keeps the meaning and the tone.</li>
    <li><strong>One precise word beats three vague ones.</strong> "Governments should curb emissions" is stronger than "Governments should try to reduce and lower the amount of emissions."</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The fastest way to see your own lexical errors is to write a timed essay and run it through the free <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">IELTS writing checker</a>, which flags word-choice and collocation slips a self-review usually misses.</p>

  <h2>Gap 4: Grammatical Range and Accuracy — error-free sentences, on purpose</h2>
  <p>Band 6 uses "a mix of simple and complex sentences" but makes errors that "rarely reduce communication." Band 7 produces "a variety of complex structures" with "frequent error-free sentences" and only "a few errors." The key phrase is <strong>frequent error-free sentences</strong>. It is not about attempting harder grammar; it is about controlling the grammar you already use.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Master three complex structures, deeply.</strong> A relative clause ("which has led to…"), a conditional ("If governments invested…, they would…"), and a concession ("Although X, Y"). Range means variety used accurately, not every tense in the book.</li>
    <li><strong>Hunt your two repeat errors.</strong> Most candidates lose accuracy to the same two things — articles (a/the) and subject-verb agreement. Fixing those two alone often lifts the whole criterion.</li>
    <li><strong>Leave two minutes to proofread.</strong> Read only for grammar on the final pass. Catching four errors can be the difference between "a few errors" (7) and "frequent errors" (6).</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Put it together</h2>
  <p>You do not need to be perfect in all four to score 7 — the criteria are averaged, so a 7, 7, 6, 7 still rounds to a 7 (IELTS reports Writing to the nearest half band). But a single 5 will drag the average down fast, so protect your weakest criterion first. Diagnose where you actually sit by reading the full <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-band-descriptors">Writing band descriptors</a> against your own essays, and use the free <a href="/band-calculator">band score calculator</a> to see how your Writing average combines with your other three sections into an overall score.</p>
  <p>The half-band gap is real but narrow. It closes not by writing more, but by writing with more control: answer the whole task, organise around clear topic sentences, choose precise ordinary words, and produce sentences you have actually checked. Do those four things consistently and Band 7 stops being luck.</p>
