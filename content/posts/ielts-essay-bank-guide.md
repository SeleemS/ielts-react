  <p>An <strong>IELTS essay bank</strong> is a large, organised collection of Writing Task 2 practice prompts paired with model essays. Let's be clear about what that means, because the term gets misused online: a legitimate essay bank contains <strong>original practice material</strong> — prompts written in the style of the exam, and model answers written by teachers to demonstrate high-band technique. Real IELTS test papers are confidential property of the exam boards and are never published, and everything on this site is original material created for practice. That is not a limitation; it is the point. What raises your Writing band is not glimpsing a question in advance — it is internalising the structures, decisions and language that high-band writing is made of, and an essay bank is the most efficient tool ever devised for exactly that.</p>

  <h2>What a good essay bank gives you</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Coverage of every question format.</strong> Task 2 questions come in a handful of recurring shapes — opinion (agree/disagree), discussion (discuss both views), problem–solution, advantages–disadvantages, and two-part questions. A bank organised by format lets you practise each shape deliberately until no wording can surprise you.</li>
    <li><strong>Coverage of the recurring topic families.</strong> Education, technology, environment, health, work, crime, society — the exam draws from a stable pool of themes. Practising across them builds the ideas and topic vocabulary you will need whatever appears on your test day.</li>
    <li><strong>Models of the standard, not just descriptions of it.</strong> The band descriptors tell you a Band 7 essay has "clear progression throughout"; a model essay <em>shows</em> you what that reads like. Seeing the standard embodied is faster than reading about it.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The wrong way to use one</h2>
  <p>Two failure modes account for most wasted essay-bank hours. The first is <strong>passive reading</strong>: consuming twenty model essays like blog posts and retaining nothing but a vague sense of their tone. The second is <strong>memorisation</strong>: learning whole introductions or essays by heart to reproduce in the exam. Examiners are specifically trained to spot memorised content, and a prefabricated essay bolted onto the actual question fails Task Response — the criterion that asks whether you answered <em>this</em> question — no matter how polished the English. Both failure modes share a root: treating the model as the product. The model is not the product. Your own next essay is the product; the model is the diagnostic tool.</p>

  <h2>The method: write first, compare second</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Write your own essay first, under exam conditions.</strong> Pick a prompt, set a 40-minute timer, no dictionary, minimum 250 words. This step is non-negotiable — comparison only teaches when you have committed to your own choices first.</li>
    <li><strong>Compare structurally, criterion by criterion.</strong> Put your essay beside the model and audit the decisions, not the words: Did the model answer every part of the question, and did you? What does each of its paragraphs do, and can you say the same for yours? How does it move from idea to idea — and where do your transitions strain?</li>
    <li><strong>Harvest small, transferable patterns.</strong> Not whole sentences — patterns: a way of conceding before rebutting ("While it is true that…, this argument overlooks…"), a way of introducing an example, a precise collocation like "ease the pressure on public services." Three reusable patterns per model is a good haul; thirty is a memorisation project in disguise.</li>
    <li><strong>Rewrite one of your paragraphs immediately,</strong> applying what you took. This is where the learning actually happens — reading recognises quality, rewriting produces it.</li>
    <li><strong>Retest on a fresh prompt within a week.</strong> The measure of a study session is whether the improvement appears in the <em>next</em> essay, on a question you have never seen.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Reading a model like an examiner</h2>
  <p>When you study a model essay, mark it up against the four public criteria in turn: find the thesis and check every paragraph serves it (Task Response); trace how each paragraph opens, develops one idea, and links forward (Coherence and Cohesion); circle the five or six word partnerships doing real work — you will find precise collocations rather than rare vocabulary (Lexical Resource); and count the variety of sentence structures, noticing how complexity is used where the argument needs it, not everywhere (Grammatical Range). Our criterion-by-criterion guide to the <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-band-descriptors">Writing band descriptors</a> pairs directly with this exercise, and our <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-band-7-structure">Band 7 essay structure guide</a> gives you the skeleton most strong models share.</p>

  <h2>A weekly cycle that compounds</h2>
  <p>One serious essay per week, fully processed, beats five essays merely written. A workable cycle: early in the week, write timed on a fresh prompt from our <a href="/ielts-writing-task-2-topics">Task 2 topic bank</a>; mid-week, compare against a model and rewrite your weakest paragraph; late in the week, get objective scoring — our <a href="/ielts-writing-checker">IELTS writing checker</a> grades your essay against the same four criteria and tells you which one is capping your band, which is precisely the information self-review is worst at producing. Keep a running error log of your three most repeated mistakes; recurring errors in articles, sentence boundaries or subject–verb agreement hold bands down more stubbornly than any vocabulary shortage, as our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-common-grammar-mistakes">common grammar mistakes</a> shows.</p>

  <h2>The bottom line</h2>
  <p>An essay bank will not hand you your exam question in advance — nothing legitimate will, and nothing needs to. Task 2 is a repeating structure wearing different topics, and a bank of original prompts and model essays lets you rehearse that structure until it is automatic. Write first, compare like an examiner, harvest patterns rather than paragraphs, and measure yourself on fresh prompts in the <a href="/writingquestion">Writing practice section</a>. Used this way, an essay bank stops being reading material and becomes what it should be: a training ground.</p>
