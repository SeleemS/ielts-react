  <p>Multiple choice is widely rated the hardest question type in IELTS Listening, and the reason is structural: unlike a form gap, where wrong answers simply fail to appear, a Listening multiple choice question is built so that <strong>every option is mentioned in the audio</strong>. You will hear words connected to A, B and C — the recording guarantees it. The skill is not hearing the options; it is tracking what the speaker actually <em>means</em> while all three flash past. That skill rests on two pillars: a disciplined preview before the audio starts, and pattern-recognition of the four ways distractors are built. This guide drills both; for the type's general method, see our main <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-multiple-choice">Listening multiple choice guide</a>.</p>

  <h2>Pillar one: the preview</h2>
  <p>The seconds you are given before each section are worth marks, and multiple choice consumes them fastest because there is so much to read. Use them in this order:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Read the question stems first — all of them.</strong> The stems tell you the topics in order, and because Listening questions follow the recording, the stems are a route map: when the audio moves to question 24's topic, question 23 is over, answered or not.</li>
    <li><strong>Underline the focus of each stem.</strong> "What does the tutor think of the <em>first draft</em>?" is about opinion, of the tutor, about the draft — not the student's opinion, not the final version. Most wrong answers on opinion questions are the right opinion from the wrong mouth.</li>
    <li><strong>Skim the options for their <em>differences</em>, not their content.</strong> If A, B and C are all reasons for a delay, you need only remember: A money, B weather, C staffing. Reducing each option to a keyword is what makes it possible to hold three in mind at speaking speed.</li>
    <li><strong>Do not try to memorise everything.</strong> If time runs out, prioritise stems over options — a candidate who knows the questions can half-reconstruct the options, but not the reverse.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Pillar two: the four distractor patterns</h2>
  <h3>1. The mentioned-and-rejected option</h3>
  <p><em>"I did think about extending the deadline — but that wouldn't be fair on the students who submitted on time, so instead we're offering an optional resit."</em></p>
  <p>The option "extend the deadline" was mentioned vividly and killed within the sentence. This is the workhorse distractor of the whole test. The defence is listening past every candidate answer for its fate: the words <strong>but, though, unfortunately, in the end, instead, that turned out to be impossible</strong> are execution notices for the idea just mentioned. Never commit at the moment of mention; commit at the moment of resolution.</p>
  <h3>2. The correction</h3>
  <p><em>"We're expecting about eighty people — well, eighty-five now, with the late registrations."</em></p>
  <p>First figure given, then amended. Same trap as in <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-form-completion-traps">form completion</a>, wearing multiple choice clothes: one option carries the superseded value. The final version of any corrected detail is the answer.</p>
  <h3>3. The wrong-voice option</h3>
  <p>In dialogues, one speaker proposes what another rejects: the student suggests interviewing shoppers, the tutor prefers an online survey, and the question asks what they <em>agree</em> to do. Options faithfully record the discarded suggestions. Your underlined stem is the defence — <em>whose</em> view, and is it a proposal, a preference or the final decision? Agreement questions resolve only at phrases like "all right, let's go with that."</p>
  <h3>4. The word-match option</h3>
  <p>The option repeats a striking word from the audio inside a claim the speaker never made: the speaker says the museum's <em>collection</em> outgrew the building, and an option says the building was <em>damaged</em> — while another option paraphrases "outgrew" as "needed more space" without reusing a single word. In Listening as in Reading, <strong>correct answers paraphrase; distractors echo</strong>. An option that feels right purely because you heard its words is the one to distrust.</p>

  <h2>Managing the format under time</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Answer at resolution, not at mention</strong> — but do answer. When the audio moves to the next question's territory, force a choice from what you have and let it go. Multiple choice punishes lingerers: one question mourned is two questions missed.</li>
    <li><strong>Mark eliminations in real time.</strong> A light stroke through option A the moment it is rejected in the audio turns a three-way decision into a two-way one and keeps your reasoning visible if you must guess.</li>
    <li><strong>Never leave a blank.</strong> There is no penalty for guessing, and eliminating even one option makes the guess profitable.</li>
    <li><strong>For "choose TWO letters" formats,</strong> expect the answers to arrive scattered across a longer stretch of audio, and read the instruction exactly — two letters means two.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>A practice loop that builds the skill</h2>
  <ol>
    <li>Do a multiple choice set from the <a href="/listeningquestion">Listening practice</a> bank and mark it.</li>
    <li>For every error, replay and name the distractor pattern: rejected, corrected, wrong voice, word match. The naming is the training — patterns you can name in review are patterns you notice live.</li>
    <li>Replay once more with the transcript if available, and find the exact resolution phrase you missed ("but," "instead," "let's go with that").</li>
    <li>Weekly, sit a full section under test conditions — Part 3, where multiple choice clusters, is the priority — or a complete <a href="/mock-test">mock test</a> to check the skill survives fatigue.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Progress on this type is slower than on completion tasks, because you are training attention, not technique alone. But it compounds: the preview discipline and distractor radar transfer straight to every other Listening format — the same machinery catalogued in our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-common-traps">common Listening traps</a> — and when your raw scores start climbing, the <a href="/band-calculator">band score calculator</a> will show you what beating the hardest type does to your band.</p>
