---
title: "IELTS Reading: Skimming and Scanning — The Two Reading Speeds That Save You Time"
date: August 16, 2026
excerpt: You have 60 minutes for 40 questions and roughly 2,700 words of dense text. You cannot read it all carefully. Here is exactly when to skim, when to scan, and when to read closely.
---

  <p>The single biggest reason candidates run out of time in <strong>IELTS Reading</strong> is that they read every passage the way they read a novel: word by word, front to back, once. The Reading test gives you <strong>60 minutes to answer 40 questions</strong> across three passages, and in Academic Reading those passages together run to roughly 2,000–2,700 words of dense, unfamiliar text. There is no separate transfer time in the paper test — your answers must be on the answer sheet inside that hour. Careful reading of every line simply does not fit. The candidates who finish comfortably are not faster readers; they are <em>strategic</em> readers who switch between three speeds on purpose.</p>

  <h2>The three reading speeds</h2>
  <p>Skilled test-takers move between three modes, and knowing which one a task needs is half the battle:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Skimming</strong> — reading fast to get the <em>gist</em>: what the passage is about and how it is organised. You do not read every word; your eyes glide over the text catching topic sentences and keywords.</li>
    <li><strong>Scanning</strong> — hunting for a <em>specific</em> piece of information (a name, a date, a number, a keyword) while ignoring everything else. Your eyes move quickly until the target "jumps out."</li>
    <li><strong>Close reading</strong> — slow, careful reading of the two or three sentences that actually contain the answer, to check meaning precisely.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The trap is using close reading for the whole passage. Instead, skim once for the map, scan to find the right neighbourhood, then read closely only the sentences that decide the answer.</p>

  <h2>How to skim effectively</h2>
  <p>Spend about <strong>two to three minutes</strong> skimming a passage before you look at the questions. You are not trying to understand everything — you are building a mental map so you know <em>where</em> to look later. Do this:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Read the title, any subheadings, and the first and last paragraphs</strong> in full. These frame the whole text.</li>
    <li><strong>Read the first sentence of every middle paragraph.</strong> In well-structured writing, the topic sentence tells you what that paragraph is about — which is exactly what you need for the map.</li>
    <li><strong>Let your eyes fall on repeated keywords, names, and dates</strong> without stopping to process the detail around them.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>After skimming you should be able to say, in one line, what each paragraph covers. That paragraph-by-paragraph sense of the structure is what makes <a href="/reading/matching-headings">matching headings</a> questions manageable, because those test whether you grasped each paragraph's main idea rather than its details.</p>

  <h2>How to scan effectively</h2>
  <p>Scanning is what you do <em>after</em> reading a question, to locate the answer fast. The skill is choosing the right thing to hunt for:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Target the words least likely to be paraphrased.</strong> Proper nouns, numbers, dates, capitalised terms and technical vocabulary usually appear in the text unchanged — they are the perfect scanning anchors.</li>
    <li><strong>Move your eyes in a fast zig-zag</strong> down the column, not line by line. You are looking for the <em>shape</em> of your target word, not reading sentences.</li>
    <li><strong>Use the order of the questions.</strong> Many IELTS Reading question types (True/False/Not Given, sentence completion, summary completion) follow the order of the passage, so the answer to question 5 sits below the answer to question 4. This turns the whole passage into a narrowing search, not a repeated re-read.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The step that catches everyone: read closely to confirm</h2>
  <p>Scanning finds a keyword; it does not find the answer. The most common mistake is seeing a matching word and choosing the option that contains it, without reading the surrounding sentence. IELTS writers deliberately reuse a question's keyword in a sentence that says something <em>different</em>, or place a distractor nearby. So once you have scanned to the right spot, slow down and read those one or two sentences carefully. This matters most for <a href="/reading/true-false-not-given">True / False / Not Given</a>, where the difference between "False" and "Not Given" turns on whether the text actually contradicts the statement or simply never addresses it — a distinction you can only judge by close reading, never by keyword-matching alone.</p>

  <h2>Which speed each question type needs</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Matching headings, choosing a title, "what is the writer's purpose"</strong> — these test the gist, so <em>skimming</em> does most of the work.</li>
    <li><strong>Sentence completion, table/summary completion, short-answer, "which paragraph contains…"</strong> — these want a specific detail, so <em>scanning</em> leads and close reading confirms.</li>
    <li><strong>True/False/Not Given, Yes/No/Not Given, multiple choice</strong> — scan to locate, then <em>read closely</em>; the answer hinges on precise meaning.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Common mistakes to drop</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Reading the whole passage carefully before looking at the questions.</strong> You will spend eight minutes absorbing details you will never be asked about.</li>
    <li><strong>Sub-vocalising every word.</strong> Silently "saying" each word in your head caps your speed at talking pace. Let your eyes run ahead.</li>
    <li><strong>Getting stuck on hard vocabulary.</strong> You rarely need to understand every word — skim past it and rely on context. Stopping to decode one term wastes time you cannot recover.</li>
    <li><strong>Never checking meaning.</strong> The opposite error: matching a keyword and moving on without reading the sentence. Speed without the confirm step just produces fast wrong answers.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>How to practise</h2>
  <p>Skimming and scanning improve fast because they are habits, not knowledge. Take any practice passage and give yourself only <strong>three minutes</strong> to skim and write one line per paragraph; check whether your map was right. Then time yourself scanning for ten specific facts. Build the two speeds separately before combining them under full test conditions. Once the switching feels natural, layer in pacing — roughly 20 minutes per passage — using our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-reading-time-management">IELTS Reading time management</a>, and drill authentic questions on the <a href="/readingquestion">IELTS Reading Questions</a> page.</p>

  <p>Reading fast is not about moving your eyes quicker; it is about reading each part of the passage at the speed that part deserves. Skim for the map, scan for the spot, read closely for the answer — and the 60 minutes stops being a race.</p>
