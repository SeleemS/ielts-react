---

  <p>Summary completion, note completion and table completion look different on the page — a paragraph with gaps, a bulleted outline, a grid — but they test one identical skill: matching a condensed, heavily paraphrased version of the passage against the original, and lifting the exact missing words. Candidates who treat them as three separate types waste preparation; candidates who learn the shared method get three question types for the price of one. This guide covers the common strategy, the one major variation (the box of options), and the traps specific to each format.</p>

  <h2>The family rules</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Answers come from one section of the passage,</strong> not the whole text. Your first job is to find that section; after that, everything happens within a few paragraphs.</li>
    <li><strong>Within the summary, answers usually follow the order of the source section</strong> — but less strictly than sentence completion, especially in tables, where rows can reorganise the passage's information.</li>
    <li><strong>The word limit is law.</strong> "ONE WORD ONLY" means exactly that; "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER" means a number does not count against the word limit. Over-length answers score zero, however correct their content.</li>
    <li><strong>The summary is a paraphrase minefield by design.</strong> Almost every content word around a gap has been reworded from the passage. The unparaphrasable items — names, dates, figures, technical terms — are your anchors.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The core method</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Read the title and skim the whole summary first.</strong> The heading tells you the topic, which tells you the passage section. Thirty seconds of skimming also shows you the summary's logic — chronological, cause-and-effect, comparison — which you will need to track it against the text.</li>
    <li><strong>Predict each gap before hunting.</strong> Grammar first: noun, verb, adjective, number? Then meaning: a place, a quantity, a process? A gap after "caused by" wants a cause; a gap after "such as" wants an example. Prediction converts blind scanning into targeted checking.</li>
    <li><strong>Locate the source section with the summary's anchors,</strong> then read it properly. The summary compresses several paragraphs; read the relevant part sentence by sentence with the summary alongside, ticking off each idea as it appears in reworded form.</li>
    <li><strong>Extract exact words, respecting the limit,</strong> and re-read the completed phrase. It must work grammatically and mean what the passage means. If a completed sentence reads smoothly but contradicts the text, you have picked the distractor sitting next to the answer.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>The variation that changes everything: the option box</h2>
  <p>Some summary completion tasks give you a box of words or phrases (A–J, say) to choose from instead of copying from the passage. This flips the strategy. There is no word limit to police, but the options are engineered in near-synonym pairs — "growth" and "expansion," "rarely" and "never" — where only one fits the passage's actual meaning. With a box: work by elimination, use grammar aggressively (only some options can fit each slot), and expect more options than gaps, so several are pure decoys. The box version rewards slow comparison of the final candidates; the copy version rewards precise extraction. Check which you are facing before you start.</p>

  <h2>Format-specific traps</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Summary completion:</strong> the flowing-paragraph format hides how far apart the answers sit in the passage. One summary sentence can compress a whole paragraph of text, so do not panic when consecutive gaps are separated by ten lines of source.</li>
    <li><strong>Note completion:</strong> the outline strips grammar down — gaps often sit after a dash or colon with little sentence structure to guide prediction. Use the note's heading hierarchy instead: a gap indented under "Advantages" must be an advantage.</li>
    <li><strong>Table completion:</strong> read the row and column headers before anything else — they are the grammar of the table. The classic error is filling a cell with a true fact from the wrong row: the right answer for the wrong century, the correct material for the wrong product. Always confirm both coordinates.</li>
    <li><strong>All three:</strong> singular/plural copying errors. If the passage says "fibres" and you write "fibre," the completed sentence often breaks. Copy the exact form, then let the completed sentence verify it.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>A brief worked example</h2>
  <p><strong>Passage:</strong> "Early lighthouses burned coal in open braziers, a method both costly and unreliable in storms. Oil lamps, introduced in the eighteenth century, offered steadier light, and the addition of mirrored reflectors greatly extended their range."</p>
  <p><strong>Note task:</strong> Lighthouse lighting — 1: ________ (early fuel, unreliable in bad weather) · 2: oil lamps — improved by ________ (increased range). <em>(ONE WORD ONLY)</em></p>
  <p>Gap 1 predicts a fuel noun: <strong>coal</strong> ("braziers" is the container, the nearby distractor). Gap 2 predicts the improving device: "mirrored reflectors" is two words and the limit is one — so the answer is <strong>reflectors</strong>, the head noun. That final trim — keeping the essential word when the limit squeezes you — is the single most practised move in this family.</p>
